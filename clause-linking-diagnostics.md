# Clause-linking diagnostics

This bank separates examples already present in the current grammar from newly constructed combinations and English-only semantic stimuli. It records consequences of the adopted analysis rather than elicited judgements. An asterisk is reserved for a structural violation licensed by an explicit rule; a context incompatible with the proposed meaning is described in words. Phase IV specifies the temporal nominal constructions; discourse-anaphoric schemas remain dependent on Phase V.

## Morphological and referential controls

- `Marek zdravení Janek znóvime` is an existing example in Chapter 16: `Marek sleep-CIRC Janek study-PROG`, ‘While Marek sleeps, Janek is studying’. The reform replaces its former `SEQ` gloss with `CIRC`, matching circumstantial rather than obligatorily anterior meaning.
- `pjaštu` and `pjaštení` are existing converb outputs of `pjašt-` ‘eat’. Constructed `pjaštěc` follows the front-grade coronal mapping `t → ť`, written `tě` before /ɛ/, plus final `c` of `-ěc`; its Class B lexical G0 vowel remains short because the construction contains neither a finite strong aspectual exponent nor a quantity-bearing inner derivation. No antipassive replacement applies. G0 does not shorten an independently long Class C root, and an applicative or causative retains the quantity licensed by its own inner domain. Its meaning and acceptability in a given context remain distinct from this morphological derivation.
- `nóčkek` is an existing intransitive perfective ‘ran’ in `Mé autobusam zrolit nóčkek`; `stójek` is an existing intransitive perfective ‘went’ in `Tězně knářit stójek`. Their Class A finite perfectives select G1 `ó` with short `-ek`. Direct-case `Marek` can supply the actor of an active converb and the mover of either finite verb; an overt retained patient such as `travě` ‘bread’ remains indirect inside the converb. These converb forms do not acquire the patient orientation or antipassive structure of an independent transitive perfective.
- `Marek zomenic, Janek pjáščev` and `Marek zomenezmy, Janek pjaštáž` are existing conditional examples. Comparing them directly confounds connective choice with matrix mood; the controlled alternatives below hold the matrix constant.
- Event `pjaštou` has established indirect singular `pjaštově`; expanded `Marcí travěte pjaštou` retains actor and theme as indirect nominal dependents. In `Marcí travěte pjaštově POST`, only the event head changes case. The dependent case forms do not themselves identify actor versus theme. Context must maintain the intended event reading, since lexical `pjaštou` also means ‘food’.
- Postpositions follow the complete indirect nominal phrase and are written separately. Their existing prosodic attachment to its final independent word does not make them nominal suffixes. Spatial `z`, `do`, `na`, and `u` already exist; Phase IV licenses their temporal uses explicitly, with the same indirect nominal complement and existing prosodic attachment.

## Published controlled triple (Phase II)

The chapter now presents these explicitly constructed sentences with identical predicates and arguments:

| Form | Segmentation/gloss | Contextual interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| `Marek travě pjaštu stójek` | Marek.DIR bread.IND eat-SEQ go-PF | Eating is completed before the journey. |
| `Marek travě pjaštení stójek` | Marek.DIR bread.IND eat-CIRC go-PF | Eating supplies a relevant, temporally overlapping background. |
| `Marek travě pjaštěc stójek` | Marek.DIR bread.IND eat-SIM go-PF | Eating accompanies the journey within one integrated episode. |

The G0/G1, PAL, argument-case and finite-aspect controls above apply to every row. Overlap in the second row is selected by its context; `-ení` itself does not encode it. The third row needs overlap only with the relevant part of the journey. Its manner/accompaniment relation does not entail that eating causes locomotion. These contrastive readings implement the adopted semantic analysis; they are not independently collected acceptability judgements.

## 1. Completed preliminary act and maintained circumstance

Constructed pair: `Marek pjaštu stójek` ‘Having eaten, Marek went’ versus `Marek pjaštení stójek` ‘With eating as the relevant circumstance, Marek went’. Both use the same direct actor and finite matrix form. Under the revised analysis, `-u` requires completion and local precedence, while `-ení` requires circumstantial relevance and leaves its temporal realisation to context. The awkwardly explicit second English paraphrase states the semantic diagnostic rather than a normal translation.

English stimulus: ‘She opened the door and then entered’ versus ‘The door having been opened, she entered through it’. Test the second with a context in which the door closes before entry: this removes the accessible-passage result, although it need not remove every possible circumstantial relation. Whether a maintained-result reading survives independently of such context remains unresolved. No door-opening Iridian lexical paradigm is supplied here.

## 2. Independent simultaneous activities and distinct pivots

Existing positive example: `Marek zdravení Janek znóvime` ‘While Marek sleeps, Janek is studying’. Constructed alternative: `Marek zdravu Janek znóvime` ‘After Marek has slept, Janek is studying’, with `zdrav-u` inferred from the same stem exposed by `zdrav-ení`. The second cannot describe only uncompleted overlap under bounded-anterior `-u`.

A further constructed substitution, `Marek zdravěc Janek znóvime`, tests integrated simultaneity: `v` takes the independently specified labial PAL realisation before `ěc`. Independent concurrent activities provide little integration; this is an interpretative disadvantage rather than an established categorical different-pivot prohibition. A contrived jointly organised episode must be tested separately. The first existing example rules out obligatory pivot identity for `-ení`.

## 3. Manner within one episode

Constructed pair: `Marek pjaštěc nóčkek` ‘Marek ran whilst eating’ versus `Marek pjaštu nóčkek` ‘Having eaten, Marek ran’. The same actor performs both actions; the first models temporal overlap within one episode, while the second requires completed eating before running. Eating need not be the means of locomotion for the simultaneity contrast to hold.

English stimulus: ‘He sings whilst walking’ versus ‘Having sung, he walks’. A later lexical expansion may use established `kan-` ‘sing’, but no walking paradigm is invented. Test whether the overlapping activity supplies manner, accompaniment, or actual means rather than treating those readings as equivalent.

## 4. Succession without causation

Use constructed `Marek pjaštu Janek nóčkek` ‘After Marek ate, Janek ran’. Overt different actors preserve the ordinary converb case frame. Add the English continuation ‘Janek's running had nothing to do with Marek's meal’. Under the analysis, denial of causation is compatible with `-u`; denial of completion or precedence is incompatible. No Iridian negative dependent form is inferred from this English continuation.

Alternative stimulus: replace `-u` with circumstantial `-ení` whilst explicitly denying any recoverable relevance between the events. That context defeats the circumstantial analysis, although a merely denied causal connection does not: relevance may be temporal, enabling, or framing.

## 5. Event causation and circumstantial relevance

English stimulus: ‘Rain fell; the road became slippery’ versus ‘Rain fell; the road remained dry because it was covered’. Test circumstantial `-ení` without requiring causal production of slipperiness in every use. Event causation can be inferred from the predicates and context; the connective alone supplies no causal entailment. `pozebžev-` ‘rain’ is lexicalised, but no unverified road/slippery paradigm is supplied. Compare `-u` only where rainfall is construed as a completed bounded episode.

## 6. Speech-act justification

English pair: ‘Because it rained, the road became slippery’ versus ‘Since it is raining, take an umbrella’. The first relates represented events; the second justifies an instruction. Further compare ‘The road is wet, so it probably rained’, which makes an epistemic inference in the reverse causal direction. Finite `-e` and independent discourse linkage provide separate candidate environments; an event-oriented converb must not acquire scope over an imperative merely from its English translation. A canonical specialised inferential anaphor remains uncoined.

## 7. Temporal endpoint and negation

Phase IV implemented phrase: `Marcí travěte pjaštově do` ‘until Marek's eating of the bread’, with actor/theme interpretation held constant by context. English pair: ‘Janek waited until Marek began eating’ versus ‘Janek jumped once until Marek began eating’. The first matrix eventuality is extended; the second requires a repeated or otherwise extended reinterpretation to approach the endpoint construction.

Contrast ‘Janek did not eat until Marek began eating’ with ‘Janek waited until the expected meal, which was cancelled’. Matrix negation must be evaluated separately from negation inside an event nominal, for which no productive verbal negation domain is established. No assertion of eating at the boundary follows solely from negating the matrix eventuality. Future or modal reference must be distinguished from the affirmative episodic case, and an anticipated boundary must not be made actual by stipulation. Phase IV makes onset the default endpoint; an explicit context may identify completion instead. Its constructed full sentence `Marcí travěte pjaštově do Janek znóvime` ‘Janek is studying until Marek starts eating the bread’ takes an anticipated meal as its endpoint, without asserting that the meal begins. A cancelled event supplies an endpoint only if a projected schedule independently identifies it; cancellation itself does not establish an actual eating event.

## 8. Temporal source versus integrated sequence

Phase IV implemented phrase: `Marcí travěte pjaštově z` ‘since/after Marek's eating of the bread’. Compare the English ‘After eating, Marek left’ with constructed `Marek pjaštu stójek`, and ‘Marek has been resting since eating’ with the same nominal source. An extended matrix interval supports ‘since’; a later event supports ‘after’. Hold the nominal event token and its selected boundary constant. Phase IV makes completion the temporal-source default, with onset available only through explicit contextual identification. Its constructed full sentence `Marcí travěte pjaštově z Janek znóvime` ‘Janek has been studying since Marek ate the bread’ uses an established meal and continuing progressive study; the English perfect does not introduce an Iridian retrospective suffix.

### Temporal location/proximity control

Phase IV constructed pair: `pjaštově na Janek znóvime` ‘Janek is studying during eating’ versus `pjaštově u Janek znóvime` ‘Janek is studying around the time of eating’. A context where studying ends shortly before the meal excludes the nonempty-overlap requirement of `na`, while `u` remains available if the gap counts as near. Neither containment nor exact duration is encoded by `na`; `u` need not entail before, after, or overlap individually. No obligatory identity relates Janek to the unexpressed eater. Nominal event reference is established relative to its interpretive world and time, rather than by a factive or finite-tense suffix in `-ou`.

## 9. Prospective anteriority and nonactualisation

Phase III constructed example now published: `Marek zomenesny, Janek pjášček` ‘Janek ate before Marek left’. `zomen-esny` preserves the plain stem exposed by existing `zomen-ic` and `zomen-ezmy`; no finite aspect or mood is inserted in the dependent predicate. `Janek` is the direct agent of matrix antipassive perfective `pjášč-ek`, whose independently established strong antipassive stem supplies the agent-oriented perfective frame. `Marek` is the overt direct actor of the active converb. Departure may occur subsequently or remain unrealised. Constructed `Marek zomenu, Janek pjášček` would instead require a completed prior departure and reverse the temporal orientation; its plain converb stem does not acquire the matrix antipassive.

English pair: ‘She left before help arrived’ versus ‘She died before help could arrive’. `-esny` must allow a projected reference event without entailing its occurrence; its conventional temporal relation is prospective anteriority. Add ‘and help never arrived’ to test that distinction. Compare an affirmative episodic ‘after help arrived’ construction, whose event reference is established in that context. These stimuli do not establish a new irrealis suffix inside the converb.

## 10. Open and expected conditions

Existing example: `Marek zomenezmy, Janek pjaštáž` ‘When/once Marek leaves, Janek will eat’. Controlled constructed alternative: `Marek zomenic, Janek pjaštáž` ‘If Marek leaves, Janek will eat’. Only the connective differs. Expected `-ezmy` presents the departure as anticipated; open `-ic` leaves that stance uncommitted. Both antecedents remain unasserted.

Hold the matrix irrealis constant in a second constructed pair: `Marek zomenic, Janek pjáščev` versus `Marek zomenezmy, Janek pjáščev`. The first is existing; the second tests the grammar's described marked combination of expectation and consequent possibility. Phase III preserves expectation of the antecedent even with an uncertain consequent; no retreat from that expectation is entailed by matrix irrealis. Test cancellation of the expected departure, generic rules, procedural triggers, and independent conditional utterances separately. Do not equate expectation with certainty, imminence, or a factive presupposition.

## 11. Concession and incidental counter-expectation

Phase III publishes constructed `Marek zomenouc, Janek pjaštáž` with two contextually differentiated readings: ‘Although Marek is leaving, Janek will eat’, where departure is accepted, and ‘Even if Marek leaves, Janek will eat’, where departure is entertained. Plain `zomen-ouc` adds no finite viewpoint or mood, while matrix `pjašt-áž` retains contemplative aspect. Each overt actor is direct in its own nominative-domain clause. The counter-expectation is conventional; actuality varies with the factual versus entertained context, and temporal overlap is not required. These constructed contexts specify the analysis rather than report collected usage.

English pair: ‘Although he was tired, he ran’ versus ‘He ran whilst eating, unexpectedly’. `-ouc` conventionally marks an expectation defeated by the consequent; `-ěc` can combine integrated overlap with a contextual surprise, but surprise alone does not identify a concessive relation. Compare factual ‘although P’ with entertained ‘even if P’: concessive conditionality must not assert P. Whether the relevant stative predicate licenses the general verbal converb or a restricted state-frame construction requires independent lexical checking.

## 12. Discourse continuation and inference

English pair: ‘She opened the door. Nu, he entered’ versus ‘The road is wet. Therefore it probably rained’. Established `nu` supplies weak continuation or transition; consequent and inferential readings need contextual support. A full utterance boundary is compatible with discourse linkage. Anaphoric schemas ‘that being so’, ‘if that is so’, and ‘even so’ remain English schemas until a propositional anaphor and source construction are specified. Nominal-anaphoric `i/ji/ni` are not thereby licensed as propositional pronouns.

## 13. Purposive motion and fulfilment

Existing positive example: `Mé autobusam zrolit nóčkek` ‘We ran to catch up with the bus’. Here `autobusam` is the indirect patient of `zrolit`; `mé` is the direct actor/mover, and `nóčkek` supplies finite perfective aspect. Compare the English continuation ‘but we never caught it’ with ‘and we caught it’. Purpose remains compatible with either outcome. Phase III distinguishes selected-complement control from adverbial continuity and separates wanting/trying (no necessary realisation) from beginning (inception, without necessary completion).

Existing alternative: `Tězně knářit stójek` ‘(They) went swimming in the lake’. A controlled English temporal paraphrase, ‘Having swum, they went’, reverses the relation between the intended event and motion; replacing the purposive with bounded sequence changes the construction and cannot preserve its selected prospective-complement analysis automatically.

## 14. Omitted pivot and discourse reference

Existing pair of constructions: `Tězně knářit stójek` and `Mé autobusam zrolit nóčkek`. For a controlled constructed pair, add overt `Marek` to the first: `Marek tězně knářit stójek` ‘Marek went swimming in the lake’. The overt actor and recoverable null actor use the same morphosyntax; neither changes the purpose relation. The selected purposive complement's control relation must remain distinct from the defeasible same-pivot preference of adverbial chains.

Contrast the existing different-pivot `Marek zdravení Janek znóvime` with constructed `Marek zdravení znóvime`, in a context where Marek is the only salient actor. Same-pivot recovery is the default in the latter, while a sufficiently clear discourse antecedent may favour a different actor. No switch-reference suffix is introduced. A continuing discourse topic need not be either grammatical pivot.

## Cross-cutting falsification protocol

For each set, record separately: the suffix's conventional requirement; temporal/aspectual construal; contextual inference; incompatible interpretation; and any untested distribution. Change one factor at a time: participant identity, clause order, matrix aspect, matrix polarity, question/imperative force, modal embedding, or the availability of an overt coherence cue. Reversing clause order must respect dependent-before-matrix syntax; a failed reversal may diagnose order rather than connective meaning.

Phase III `sec:converb-inflection-scope` explicitly excludes independent finite viewpoint, mood, aspect-conditioned alignment and inner evidential suffixes from all eight converb predicates; independently licensed lower lexical/applicative/causative structure is retained. No productive internal negation is added merely for testing, since its attachment and concord domain remain unspecified. Finite linked clauses preserve their independently licensed categories, while event nominal dependents retain nominal case. A predicted semantic incompatibility is not a claim about elicited ungrammaticality, frequency, register, or historical development.
