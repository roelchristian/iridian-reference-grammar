# Suprasegmental reform: comparative research review

Reviewed 30 October 2026. This note evaluates the external comparisons in the supplied proposal; the adopted Iridian rules derive their authority from the reform itself. Comparative resemblance does not supply experimental evidence for Iridian pronunciation.

## Findings and scope

The proposed combination of initial primary stress, binary subsidiary footing, and independent vowel quantity is coherent. Its strictly quantity-insensitive secondary-stress algorithm is an Iridian specification, rather than a direct transcription of Finnish or Estonian phonology. The grammar should distinguish that specification from possible acoustic realisations, and avoid treating a metrical head as necessarily a pitch peak or a lengthened vowel. The regional description of a restrained Central/Northern European sound is an aesthetic judgement and should not become a typological claim.

Only two new works are needed in the grammar's bibliography: Schiering, Bickel and Hildebrandt (2010) and Ladd (2008). The existing Czech references adequately support the limited comparison retained in Chapter 2. The remaining research below belongs to this review unless the grammar makes the corresponding comparative claims.

## Czech: fixed stress and weak acoustic marking

The existing citation is sufficient: Radek Skarnitzl and Anders Eriksson (2017), “The acoustics of word stress in Czech as a function of speaking style”, *Interspeech 2017*, 3221–3225, [DOI and proceedings record](https://doi.org/10.21437/Interspeech.2017-417), [full article](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2017/skarnitzl17_interspeech.pdf). The abstract and results distinguish predictable initial stress from reliable increases in vowel duration, intensity, pitch, or spectral emphasis. This supports caution about stress correlates; it does not establish Iridian acoustic values or prove that subsidiary stress must be inaudible.

The proposal's 2018 reference is real: Radek Skarnitzl, “Fonetická realizace slovního přízvuku u delších slov v češtině”, *Slovo a slovesnost* 79(3): 199–216. Its identity and pagination are confirmed by the [author's university publication list](https://fonetika.ff.cuni.cz/ustav/vyucujici/radek-skarnitzl/). The [article abstract](https://www.ceeol.com/search/article-detail?id=687647) reports weak stress cues and word-final vowel lengthening within prosodic phrases. The full 2018 text was not inspected, so no detailed page-specific claim from it has been added to the grammar. Ordinary word-final lengthening in that study should not be conflated with lengthening restricted to strong intonational boundaries.

## Finnish: quantity and rhythm coexist, but footing is more complex

Kari Suomi, Juhani Toivanen and Riikka Ylitalo (2008), *Finnish Sound Structure: Phonetics, Phonology, Phonotactics and Prosody*, Studia Humaniora Ouluensia 9, Oulu: University of Oulu, [university-hosted full book](https://oulurepo.oulu.fi/bitstream/handle/10024/36099/isbn978-951-42-8984-2.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=1). The preface and §9.1 (p. 75) establish contrastive quantity independently of stress, initial primary stress, and subsidiary stress usually on the third or fourth syllable, then alternating. Segmental and morphological structure affect subsidiary positions; a final heavy syllable can sometimes bear secondary stress.

Consequently, Finnish supports the coexistence of quantity contrasts and metrical organisation, but cannot substantiate an exceptionless rule assigning secondary stress solely by odd-numbered syllable position. See also the primary analysis [“Explaining Nonfinality: Evidence from Finnish”](https://lingref.com/cpp/wccfl/26/paper1685.pdf), especially its discussion of heavy syllables, morphology, and competing analyses on pp. 307–309. No Finnish compound-stress rule is imported into Iridian.

## Estonian: the comparison needs a narrower formulation

Eva Liina Asu and Pire Teras (2009), “Estonian”, *Journal of the International Phonetic Association* 39(3): 367–372, [publisher full text](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-international-phonetic-association/article/estonian/629DFE570A2E6606733B29A0145B4512), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S002510030999017X). The “Stress” subsection describes mostly initial primary stress, odd-position subsidiary stresses, and suffixes that attract stress. The immediately following “Quantity system” subsection explicitly relates quantity to stress, pitch, and disyllabic foot timing.

Estonian therefore supplies a comparison for rhythmic and morphological subsidiary prominence. It does not establish the same independence of vowel length from stress that Iridian and Finnish have, and its three-way quantity opposition should not be treated as a simple short–long vowel system extending uniformly to unstressed syllables.

## Polish: retain the disagreement and its methodological basis

Luiza Newlin-Łukowicz (2012), “Polish stress: looking for phonetic evidence of a bidirectional system”, *Phonology* 29(2): 271–329, [publisher abstract and metadata](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/phonology/article/abs/polish-stress-looking-for-phonetic-evidence-of-a-bidirectional-system/D81D3088C180095FF0C3DD7A39E06324), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0952675712000139). The abstract supports the reported failure to find iterative prominence in simple words, while allowing different prosodic structures in compounds.

Beata Łukaszewicz (2018), “Phonetic evidence for an iterative stress system: the issue of consonantal rhythm”, *Phonology* 35(1): 115–150, [full publisher text](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/phonology/article/phonetic-evidence-for-an-iterative-stress-system-the-issue-of-consonantal-rhythm/931219AEF375213BDF2846140957C977), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0952675717000392). Its abstract, §§1–2, and conclusion identify relative consonantal duration, including the relation to the preceding vowel, as evidence for subsidiary rhythm. A failure to detect vowel-based cues does not alone settle its presence.

Zofia Malisz and Marzena Żygis (2018), “Lexical stress in Polish: evidence from focus and phrase-position differentiated production data”, *Speech Prosody 2018*, [proceedings record](https://www.isca-archive.org/speechprosody_2018/malisz18_speechprosody.html), [full article](https://www.isca-archive.org/speechprosody_2018/malisz18_speechprosody.pdf). Its abstract and analysis report primary-stress effects but no clear systematic acoustic evidence for secondary stress in their materials.

These results justify distinguishing metrical representation from its measured cues, without declaring Polish secondary stress either disproved or universally acoustically manifest. They do not license assigning any particular Polish cue to Iridian.

## Prosodic domains and boundary phenomena

René Schiering, Balthasar Bickel and Kristine A. Hildebrandt (2010), “The prosodic word is not universal, but emergent”, *Journal of Linguistics* 46(3): 657–709, [primary publisher abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-linguistics/article/abs/prosodic-word-is-not-universal-but-emergent1/7C57FC063CDE7BEDEF04FB442D8E11CB), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022226710000216). Their abstract reports Vietnamese and Limbu evidence against a universal clustering of rule domains into one hierarchy. It supports presenting the Iridian hierarchy as a language-specific analysis, with stress independently motivating its lexical domain; it does not support identifying every segmental process with that domain.

Sun-Ah Jun (1998), “The Accentual Phrase in the Korean prosodic hierarchy”, *Phonology* 15(2): 189–226, [publisher page](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/phonology/article/abs/accentual-phrase-in-the-korean-prosodic-hierarchy/B7FE580D038077DF51CD114868C2010E), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0952675798003571). The opening discussion distinguishes syntactically defined phrases from intonational units; its discussion around p. 193 distinguishes strengthening and tonal marking at smaller boundaries from substantial lengthening and possible pauses at intonational boundaries. The indexed author-text passage was inspected, although the old UCLA PDF link now returns an error. The source remains in this review; no Korean comparison was added to the grammar.

Jun's Korean results support distinguishing levels and diagnostic cues. They do not establish a universal package of lengthening, consonantal strengthening, pitch reset, and pause at every boundary. In Iridian these remain possible realisations; devoicing, vocalisation, assimilation, and resyllabification retain their independently described domains until clitic and cross-word distributions are specified.

## Autosegmental–metrical notation

D. Robert Ladd (2008), *Intonational Phonology*, 2nd edn, Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, [book DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511808814). The publisher's readable excerpts from [Chapter 2](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/intonational-phonology/fundamental-concepts-of-the-autosegmentalmetrical-theory/E6242BC87A7F98EF909FA6160E5C1F44) and [Chapter 3](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/intonational-phonology/analysis-and-transcription-of-intonation/EE7BBD3CE44D7646F72BC997B60AE2E2) establish the AM framework and explicitly distinguish notation from theoretical commitments. These excerpts were inspected; the full book text was not available through the publisher page. Cite the chapters broadly rather than assigning an unverified page to the precise notation.

The original primary statement is Janet Breckenridge Pierrehumbert (1980), *The Phonology and Phonetics of English Intonation*, PhD thesis, MIT, [MIT repository](https://dspace.mit.edu/entities/publication/362ae881-71c3-4189-bbf5-671b5c51b09d), [author-hosted thesis](https://www.phon.ox.ac.uk/jpierrehumbert/publications/Pierrehumbert_PhD.pdf). Its abstract explicitly separates accents associated with stressed syllables from phrase-edge tones independent of metrical position.

Iridian H*, H-, H% and L% labels may adopt these conventions without adopting English's tonal inventory or the same meanings. In particular, L% alone names a low boundary target rather than specifying a whole falling trajectory. The grammar must supply the preceding high target or prominence from which a default fall is realised. Rising polar contours are defaults, while falling interrogatives remain available; the interrogative particle and the tune have distinct functions. Post-focal compression is a chosen Iridian realisation, not a universal implication of focus.

## Outstanding descriptive limits

- Acoustic scaling, precise alignment, and register-dependent realisation remain unspecified rather than measured.
- Topic categories have no independently established tonal inventory.
- The minimal lexical stress domain does not settle segmental sandhi domains across clitics and words.
- The proposal's schematic forms must be checked against current morphology and syllabification before becoming IPA examples; comparative scholarship cannot validate their Iridian segments.

## Review of the implementation

The implemented Chapter 2 treatment was reviewed after its initial revision. Its retained Czech comparison, language-specific domain hierarchy, adopted footing rule, and distinction between representation and possible phonetic realisation remain within the verified sources' scope. The draft does not import Finnish quantity sensitivity, Estonian foot quantity, Polish bidirectionality, or Korean boundary cues into Iridian. Consequently, their sources remain in this review rather than expanding the grammar's bibliography.

One precision issue was corrected in the completed treatment: the L% and H% labels specify low and high endpoints, whereas a falling or rising trajectory additionally depends on the preceding contour. The added clarification preserves the adopted declarative and interrogative defaults without attributing a complete contour to a single boundary target. Exact segmental representations and the publication build are covered by the separate consistency pass.
