\chapter{Nominal morphology}\label{ch:nominal-morphology}

Iridian nouns distinguish three productive cases and two numbers through
class-selected endings and stem grades. Nominal reference and the internal
structure of the noun phrase are treated in Chapter~\ref{ch:nominal-syntax}.

In schematic examples, double brackets enclose a lexical meaning, while a
subscript specifies the complete inflectional cell of that slot
(§\,\ref{sec:lexical-slot-schemata}). A nominal cell specifies case and
number; a finite transitive perfective cell includes ordinary patient
orientation. The notation specifies grammatical
features without assigning a declension or stem grade to an unlisted lexeme.

\section{Case, number, and nominal stems}
\label{sec:nm-case}

\subsection{Case, \synhead{KP}, and the alignment-sensitive argument system}
\label{sec:nm-case-system}

The case-number endings realise the low inflectional domain of the noun
phrase, whose outer projection is \synhead{KP}. Modern Iridian has three productive
structural cases: \term{direct},
\term{transitive}, and \term{indirect}; a fourth case, the \term{vocative},
survives as a nonproductive form limited to direct address in lexically
specified items. The direct case supplies the citation form, with zero or a thematic ending
according to class. Case-number endings jointly realise
K\textsuperscript{0} and Num\textsuperscript{0}
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-num}), while the vocative is a specialised
speech-act-adjacent realisation of the same low nominal domain.

\begin{table}
  \footnotesize
  \caption{Case values in the Iridian nominal domain.}
  \label{tab:nm-cases}
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{l L L}
    \toprule
    {\sc case} & {\sc primary function} & {\sc structural interpretation} \\
    \midrule
    Direct & citation form; absolutive argument; zero-copula nominal predicate &
    unmarked case value; zero or thematic exponent by class \\
    Transitive & agent of perfective/retrospective transitives;
    directive agent with a distinct selected participant &
    ergative and directive-agent realisation of K \\
    Indirect & patient in nominative-alignment aspects; possessors;
    obliques; postpositional complements; nominal predicates with finite \ird{neh-} &
    general dependent K realisation; compatible with core argumenthood \\
    Vocative & direct address in lexically specified names, kin
    terms, and a small residue of nouns &
    speech-act linked case in the low nominal domain \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The clausal architecture selects the case values realised within
\synhead{KP}. In the perfective and retrospective aspects the patient
remains direct while the agent is transitive; in the nominative-alignment
aspects the agent remains direct while the patient appears in the indirect.
These are the ordinary active frames. A simple applicative selects its applied
participant as direct in either domain, assigning the agent indirect case in
the imperfective and transitive case in the perfective or retrospective,
while a retained theme is indirect. Causative and directive frames have their
own higher selection rules (§§\,\ref{sec:aspect-caus},
\ref{sec:mood-directive-case}).

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Marėk zemácime.//
\glb Marėk.\Dir{} run-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Marėk is running.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Trava Marku pjaštník.//
\glb bread.\Dir{} Marėk.\Trs{} eat-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Marėk ate the bread.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Marėk travě pjaštíme.//
\glb Marėk.\Dir{} bread.\Ind{} eat-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Marėk is eating bread.}//
\endgl
\xe

The direct supplies the unmarked absolutive case, while the transitive marks the
agent in perfective and retrospective transitives and in the directive frames
described above. The indirect marks patients in nominative-alignment clauses
as well as possessors, obliques, and postpositional complements. A nominal
predicate selected by finite \ird{neh-} also takes indirect case throughout
the aspectual paradigm, although it denotes a classification or role rather
than a patient; ordinary zero-copula nominal predication retains direct case
(§§\,\ref{sec:zero-copula}, \ref{sec:neh}). The vocative is confined to
lexically specified forms of direct address.

A pottery episode shows how these case values follow alignment rather than
remaining attached to one participant role throughout the verbal paradigm:

\pex\label{ex:nm-slot-case-alignment}
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[potter]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Sg{}} \textnormal{[[bowl]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}.\Sg{}} \textnormal{[[glaze]]}\textsubscript{\Prog{}}.//
\glb potter.\Dir{}.\Sg{} bowl.\Ind{}.\Sg{} glaze-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{A potter is glazing a bowl.} \lingcontext{ordinary active progressive; potter direct, bowl indirect}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[bowl]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Sg{}} \textnormal{[[potter]]}\textsubscript{\Trs{}.\Sg{}} \textnormal{[[glaze]]}\textsubscript{\Trs{}.\Pf{}}.//
\glb bowl.\Dir{}.\Sg{} potter.\Trs{}.\Sg{} glaze-\Trs{}-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{A potter glazed a bowl.} \lingcontext{ordinary active perfective; bowl direct, potter transitive}//
\endgl
\xe

The bowl is the patient in both clauses, although only the completed-event
clause assigns it direct case; the potter correspondingly changes from
direct to transitive. Neither the case contrast nor either participant role
determines whether the referent is already identifiable.

The same NOM-domain frame places the horse in indirect case when it is
the object of mounting:

\ex\label{ex:nm-mount-horse}%
\begingl
\gla Pres kazam radějša.//
\glb man.\Dir{} horse.\Ind{} mount-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{The man mounts a horse.}//
\endgl
\xe

\ird{Kazam} is the singular indirect of Class~I \ird{kaz}; context
identifies the man in this example, while the horse may be newly introduced.
Neither interpretation requires a distinct nominal form. The verb
\ird{radějš-} \trsl{mount; board a vehicle}
appears here in the continuous \ird{radějša}, with the horse as its
indirect-case patient.

Declension combines case-number exponence with stem selection: the same ending
may follow different stem grades, since each noun carries both a declension
class and the lexical information that licenses reduction, raising, or quantity
alternation (§§\,\ref{sec:nm-stemform}--\ref{sec:nm-declension}). The
morphological order is:

\medskip
\begin{center}
\textit{root} $\rightarrow$
\textit{class-selected stem grade} $\rightarrow$
[K\textsuperscript{0}$+$Num\textsuperscript{0}] exponent
\end{center}
\medskip

\noindent Case and number are fused at [K\textsuperscript{0}$+$Num\textsuperscript{0}];
the exponent selected for each of the six case-number cells belongs to the
noun's declension class, with no separate number morpheme in the surface string.
Contextual identification, discourse introduction and speaker-specific reference
do not select additional inflectional endings (Chapter~\ref{ch:nominal-syntax}).

\subsection{Number and countability}
\label{sec:nm-num}

Iridian expresses inflectional number through fused case-number endings.
Each declension class provides six case-number cells, covering the three
structural cases in both singular and plural values, although individual
forms may be syncretic. Within these endings, no independent substring
expresses plurality alone, and no independent plurality particle supplements
the paradigm. The number of a possessor and that of a possessed noun are
expressed by their respective case-number forms (§\,\ref{sec:nm-poss-number}).

The grammar analyses inflectional Num\textsuperscript{0} as a syntactically
projected head carrying [\textsc{+pl}] in the plural cells. NumP lies
between the lexical/possessive domain and KP, giving the hierarchy
\synhead{KP} $>$ \synhead{NumP} $>$ \synhead{PossP}/\synhead{NP}.
Num\textsuperscript{0} and K\textsuperscript{0} are adjacent heads on
this spine; at PF they are fused into a
single [K$+$Num] node. This fusion is an analytical assumption about Iridian;
Vocabulary Insertion supplies its phonological realisation within the
Distributed Morphology framework \cite[§\,2.3]{embicknoyer2007}. This node is
spelled out by a Vocabulary Item from the class-specific six-slot inventory.
Number semantics is recoverable from the inflectional form as a whole.
The special distribution of number under cardinal numerals is described in
§\,\ref{sec:nm-quant}.

Outside the cardinal context, plural morphology is most natural with count nouns.
It also serves collective or aggregate reference, as well as definite,
discourse-prominent, or generic reference to groups.
With mass nouns, plural forms select countable servings, varieties, or other
instances, as in Class~VIII \ird{piva} alongside mass \ird{pivo} `beer'.
A small set of pluralia-tantum nouns, especially names of
paired body parts and paired objects, resist ordinary pluralisation because
the lexical entry already denotes the pair; such items use plural morphology
as their citation form.

A maintenance report separates the number of objects from their discourse
status. The following schematic pair holds the predicate constant while
changing only the nominal case-number cell:

\pex\label{ex:nm-slot-number-beacons}
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[beacon]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Sg{}} \textnormal{[[flash]]}\textsubscript{\Prog{}}.//
\glb beacon.\Dir{}.\Sg{} flash-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{A beacon is flashing.} \lingcontext{one beacon is flashing during the inspection}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[beacon]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Pl{}} \textnormal{[[flash]]}\textsubscript{\Prog{}}.//
\glb beacon.\Dir{}.\Pl{} flash-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Beacons are flashing.} \lingcontext{several beacons are flashing during the inspection}//
\endgl
\xe

The plural identifies a plurality of beacons. Either expression can retrieve
objects already identifiable in the inspection context, or introduce previously
unmentioned objects when the context supports that reading. Its schematic
cell specifies number without predicting the ending
or stem grade of an unlisted noun.

Japanese and Korean distinguish number from reference through different
morphological resources. Kawahara analyses Japanese \textit{-tati} as an
optional associative marker, deriving additive-looking readings within that
analysis \cite[§§\,3--4]{kawahara2025}, whereas Park argues that Korean
\textit{-tul} has both additive and associative uses \cite{park2023plural}.
A student group illustrates the contrast with Iridian's ordinary inflection:

\pex\label{ex:nm-comparative-students}
\a
\begingl
\glpreamble \textit{Japanese} \cite[example~3b; §4]{kawahara2025}//
\gla gakusei-tati//
\glb student-\textsc{assoc}//
\glft \trsl{students}
\lingcontext{in Kawahara's analysis, a plurality represented by students; the gloss identifies that analysis}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\glpreamble \textit{Iridian}//
\gla študentí//
\glb student.\Dir{}.\Pl{}//
\glft \trsl{students}
\lingcontext{ordinary nominal plural; the members satisfy the student description}//
\endgl
\xe

The shared translation conceals a difference in the semantic contribution
assigned to the morphology. Kawahara's associative analysis permits a
group represented by students to include non-students, while Iridian
\ird{študentí} realises the ordinary direct plural of \ird{študent}
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-irregular}). Like the beacons in
(\ref{ex:nm-slot-number-beacons}), its plurality requires no representative
individual around whom associates are assembled. Whether particular students
are intended or jointly identifiable is resolved by context and the containing
construction, not by a second inflectional paradigm.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla byle//
\glb child.\Dir{}.\Pl{}//
\glft \trsl{children} \lingcontext{a plurality introduced into the discourse}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla byle//
\glb child.\Dir{}.\Pl{}//
\glft \trsl{the children} \lingcontext{the same form retrieves a previously established group}//
\endgl
\xe

The plural may also denote particular children intended by the speaker whose
identities are not yet shared. That specific interpretation requires no
additional particle and does not make the description definite for the addressee.

Mass and count reference can likewise be compared in a locative existential
frame, where the nominal remains direct in both number values:

\pex\label{ex:nm-slot-mass-count}
\a
\begingl
\gla Pivo \textnormal{[[cellar]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}.\Sg{}} na jeca.//
\glb beer.\Dir{}.\Sg{} cellar.\Ind{}.\Sg{} in \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is beer in the cellar.} \lingcontext{a quantity of the substance is stored there}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Piva \textnormal{[[cellar]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}.\Sg{}} na jeca.//
\glb beer.\Dir{}.\Pl{} cellar.\Ind{}.\Sg{} in \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There are beers in the cellar.} \lingcontext{separate servings are being counted}//
\endgl
\xe

The plural selects countable instances; it does not merely increase the
quantity of the mass substance. The existential predicate and locative
complement remain unchanged.

\subsection{Stem formation and allomorph selection}
\label{sec:nm-stemform}
\label{sec:nm-stem-grades}

Nominal paradigms select both a segmental stem and a quantitative grade.
\term{G0} preserves lexical quantity; \term{G1} selects its strong
correspondent, which is long where the vowel class has a distinct long member.
For \ird{y}, G0 and G1 both give /ɨ/, so strong selection preserves the
selected nucleus without redirecting the operation to another vowel.
\term{G2} raises a specified mid vowel while preserving quantity, and \term{G3} combines raising with
strength. The productive nominal selectors target the rightmost eligible
lexical-root nucleus, excluding fleeting vowels and all prefixal and suffixal
material; a lexically specified target takes precedence. These instructions
operate within the selected stem, independently of initial stress and
consonantal PAL (§\,\ref{sec:morphophon}).

A noun's declension class specifies its case-number endings and the grades
with which they combine. Class~II \ird{perėk} selects strong
\ird{pérk-} (G1), whereas \ird{lobėk} selects raised strong
\ird{lúbk-} (G3); both take transitive \ird{-u}. Neither choice follows
from the pronounced quality of that ending.

Fleeting-vowel reduction and quantitative selection are separate operations.
The final short unstressed \ird{ė} /ə/ is absent from \ird{pérk-} and
\ird{lúbk-}, but it is excluded as a quantity target rather than being
lengthened before deletion. The retained root nucleus bears the selected
grade, while every consonant remains available for syllabification and
mutation. Lexical reduced stems outside productive Class~II, including
\ird{Jank-} and Class~VII \ird{obl-}, retain the quantity assigned by
their own paradigms. Stable stems retain their vowels in these environments
(§\,\ref{sec:vowel-zero}).

The traditional grade correspondences distinguish raising from lengthening:
G2 relates short
\ird{e/ě} to \ird{i} and \ird{o} to \ird{u}, with corresponding
long-vowel pairs \ird{é}/\ird{í} and \ird{ó}/\ird{ú}; G3 additionally
selects long \ird{í} or \ird{ú} from the eligible short mid vowel.
Class~II \ird{lobėk}/\ird{lúbk-} selects G3, whereas
\ird{perėk}/\ird{pérk-} selects G1. In the principal variety,
G1 \ird{é, ó} has /eː, oː/, distinct from G3 \ird{í, ú}
/iː, uː/, while G2 retains short /ɪ, ʊ/. The long-vowel grade pairs
likewise preserve their height contrast (§\,\ref{sec:vowel-raising}).
Regional mergers can neutralise one or both contrasts without changing
the selected grade. Lexical \ird{uo} /uɔ/ is inherently long: G0 and
G1 retain it, while G2 and G3 require separately licensed stem allomorphy.
A class must license
raising independently: a suffix vowel alone does not establish that relation.
Consonantal palatality survives raising. Soft coronals are written
\ird{ť, ď, ň, ľ} before the raised vowel, while labial softness
coincides in spelling with contextual palatalisation before /ɪ, iː/
(§\,\ref{sec:orthographic-i}).

Case-number exponents that select the front grade independently
mutate the final consonant of the selected stem. Their front vocalism often
corresponds to that grade, but does not itself determine it. Ordinary nominal
mutation gives \ird{k $\rightarrow$ c} and coronal
/t, d, n, l/ $\rightarrow$ /c, ɟ, ɲ, ʎ/, written
\ird{tě, dě, ně, lě} before /ɛ/; \ird{perėk} therefore yields
\ird{pérk-} plus \ird{-e}, with velar mutation producing \ird{pérce}.
Lexical allomorphs such as \ird{red}/\ird{rez-} in \ird{rezy} are specified
with their class and do not redefine productive coronal softening. The same
front grade supplies postalveolar /ʃ, ʒ, tʃ/ from final /s, z, ts/ and
secondary palatalisation of labials; the sibilant outputs are written
\ird{š, ž, č}, independently of the following vowel
(§\,\ref{sec:palatal-sibilants}). Grade selection applies to the boundary
input once: inherited /k/ $\rightarrow$ /ts/ does not feed a second application
of /ts/ $\rightarrow$ /tʃ/. Thus \ird{pérce} retains its alveolar affricate
before /ɛ/, as does \ird{lúbcí} before /iː/; high front vowels do not
categorically change sibilant place of articulation.

The case ending and its grade specification must therefore be recorded
together. Singular indirect \ird{-e} selects PAL in Class~IV \ird{redě},
but the homophonous Class~V ending preserves /n/ in \ird{ráne}.
Class~VII makes the converse point: its direct-plural \ird{-a} selects
\ird{obľ-} or \ird{tězň-}, although its vowel is not front, whereas
indirect-plural \ird{-em} selects hard \ird{obl-} or \ird{tězn-}.
The complete paradigms below license these correspondences; a vowel's written
shape cannot supply a missing class or grade assignment.

Liquid-bearing stems require no separate syllabic grade. In \ird{uzėl}
$\rightarrow$ \ird{uzla}, \ird{l} begins the second syllable; in
\ird{perėk} $\rightarrow$ \ird{pérku}, \ird{r} closes the first.
If reduction would prevent syllabification around vowel nuclei without
unlicensed consonant loss, the vowel is retained
(§\,\ref{sec:vocalic-nuclei}; §\,\ref{sec:cluster-repair}).

Coda /l/ vocalisation follows stem selection, consonant mutation, and
suffixation. Thus \ird{byl} [bɯ̯ɨw] retains the same underlying lateral as
\ird{byla} [ˈbɯ̯ɨ.la], where the case ending places it in an onset; soft
\ird{ľ} retains its lateral realisation. Prevocalic /v/ surfaces as [ʋ],
while coda /v/ in zero-ended \ird{tráv} gives [traːw]. These positional
realisations leave the class-selected stems and case-number exponents intact
(§§\,\ref{sec:l-vocalisation} and~\ref{sec:v-realisation}).

Productive short native Class~I and regular Class~III nouns select G0 in the
singular and G1 throughout the plural. Lexically long stems retain their
specified quantity, and a loan or other lexeme assigned to an invariant
subclass does not acquire G1 merely through a plural ending. The restricted
Class~III \ird{stráz}/\ird{straz-} pattern preserves its listed distribution;
Class~V \ird{rán-} remains long throughout. These lexical specifications
coexist with productive grade selection rather than imposing a single
quantity pattern on every noun.

\subsection{Vocative and proper-name behaviour}
\label{sec:nm-vocative}

The vocative is singular and nonproductive in the modern language. Its
lexically specified forms select a basic or soft stem with \ird{-o},
\ird{-e}, or zero; the kinship terms in Table~\ref{tab:nm-vocative}
illustrate the \ird{-o} pattern. A marked vocative is viable only for
lexically specified items: inherited proper names, older integrated loan proper
names, kinship terms, and a restricted residue of concrete and abstract nouns.
The latter class is lexically determined item by item and does not follow
systematically from membership in the inherited vocabulary. Outside those
lexicalised items, direct address uses the unmarked direct case.
The simplest analysis is that the vocative realises the low nominal domain in
direct relation to the speech-act layer. Direct address identifies its
addressee through the speech situation; it does not introduce a productive
definiteness contrast into the nominal paradigm.

\begin{table}[ht]
  \footnotesize
  \caption{Kinship terms retaining lexicalised vocative forms.}
  \label{tab:nm-vocative}
  \begin{tabularx}{0.7\textwidth}{L l l}
    \toprule
    {\sc direct} & {\sc vocative} & {\sc meaning} \\
    \midrule
    \ird{papka}  & \ird{papko}  & father \\
    \ird{mamka}  & \ird{mamko}  & mother \\
    \ird{mlazka} & \ird{mlazko} & brother \\
    \ird{voska}  & \ird{vosko}  & sister \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The name \ird{Marėk} retains lexically specified singular doublets:
\ird{Marku}/\ird{Marka} in the transitive and
\ird{Marcí}/\ird{Markovi} in the indirect; these variants carry no assigned
register distinction. Ordinary plural forms are not specified for this name.

Proper names otherwise behave like ordinary DPs: they bear the ordinary
case-number suffixes and may participate in possessive and demonstrative
constructions. What is special about names is not their exemption from the
nominal spine, but their lexically strong identifiability, which makes
bare-name reference especially natural. No additional article or suffix is
required when the name identifies an established discourse participant.

\section{Declension classes and paradigms}
\label{sec:nm-declension}

Nine declension classes distinguish the endings and stem grades through which
nouns realise case and number. Classes~I and~III are the principal productive
classes, while Classes~II and~IV productively accommodate specified patterns of
stem alternation. Class~V is small and partly productive; Class~VIII admits
nouns assigned to its thematic pattern. Classes~VI, VII, and~IX have restricted
membership, although Class~VII permits limited analogical extension and
Class~IX is at most weakly productive. Membership is lexical and introduces no
grammatical gender or agreement category.

Within a uniform \synhead{KP}, each noun selects its case-number exponents and
the stem grades with which they combine (§\,\ref{sec:nm-stemform}). The complete
paradigms below give the singular and plural forms in all three structural
cases. These forms remain available for definite, indefinite and specific
reference where the context supports the corresponding interpretation.

\subsection{Classes I--III: consonant, reduced, and thematic stems}
\label{sec:nm-classes-i-iii}

The productive Class~I consonant-stem pattern represented by \ird{byl} `child' has
singular endings zero, \ird{-a}, and \ird{-am}, and plural endings
\ird{-e}, \ird{-ov}, and \ird{-ac}, in direct--transitive--indirect order.
G0 appears in the singular and G1 in all three plural cells. For \ird{byl-},
the two grades have the same segmental form because /ɨ/ has no distinct long
correspondent; the case-number endings continue to distinguish the cells.
Lexically specified subclasses vary in their oblique endings and stem grades.

\ird{Veň} \trsl{woman}, \ird{kaz} \trsl{horse} and \ird{parc}
\trsl{heart} follow this regular Class~I pattern, with plural stems
\ird{véň-}, \ird{káz-} and \ird{párc-}. Thus \ird{veň},
\ird{veňa}, \ird{veňam} correspond to plural \ird{véně},
\ird{véňov}, \ird{véňac}; the lexical palatal nasal is retained
throughout. Long-root \ird{hráť} \trsl{coat} likewise takes Class~I
endings, although its lexical \ird{á} remains long in the singular
and plural.

Class~II combines fleeting-vowel reduction with lexical differences in raising.
The designated stems have short unstressed \ird{ė} /ə/ in their citation
form and omit that lexically specified slot in the bound stem:
\ird{perėk}/\ird{pérk-} `body' represents the unraised subtype, and
\ird{lobėk}/\ird{lúbk-} `apple' the raised subtype. The G1 or G3 bound stem occurs before overt case-number
endings. The direct singular retains the independently specified full citation
stem. This quantitative selection is independent of
the plain or front consonant grade. Singular
transitive \ird{-u} selects the plain consonant grade, whereas indirect \ird{-e}
(unraised) or \ird{-í} (raised) selects the front grade. The regular plural
endings are \ird{-e}, \ird{-ov}, and \ird{-em}; direct and indirect select
the front consonant grade, while transitive selects the plain consonant
grade. Both retain the strong bound quantity, yielding
\ird{pérce}/\ird{pérkov}/\ird{pércem} and
\ird{lúbce}/\ird{lúbkov}/\ird{lúbcem}. The consonant \ird{c} belongs to
the mutated stem, with /k/ $\rightarrow$ /ts/.

The long indirect \ird{-í} is retained in its specified subclass:
\ird{lúbcí} is not shortened to \ird{*lúbci} merely because the root
is already strong. Neither raising nor quantity selection follows
automatically from the surface quality of a suffix vowel.

Class~II stem selection is visible when an apple is introduced as the sole
participant and is contrasted with an imperfective patient:

\pex\label{ex:nm-slot-reduced-case}
\a
\begingl
\gla Lobėk \textnormal{[[basket]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}.\Sg{}} cy \textnormal{[[roll]]}\textsubscript{\Pf{}}.//
\glb apple.\Dir{}.\Sg{} basket.\Ind{}.\Sg{} from roll-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{An apple rolled out of a basket.} \lingcontext{direct singular retains the full citation stem}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Marár lúbcí \textnormal{[[slice]]}\textsubscript{\Prog{}}.//
\glb cook.\Dir{}.\Sg{} apple.\Ind{}.\Sg{} slice-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{A cook is slicing an apple.} \lingcontext{indirect singular selects reduction, raising, strength, and the front consonant grade}//
\endgl
\xe

The surface contrast joins several independently specified choices: the
indirect cell selects reduced strong raised \ird{lúbk-}, its front
consonant grade supplies \ird{lúbc-}, and the long ending remains
\ird{-í}. The case contrast alone does not assign that pattern to every
noun ending in \ird{-ėk}.

\ird{Sviňėk} \trsl{girl; daughter} belongs to the unraised Class~II
subtype: loss of fleeting \ird{ė} leaves strong \ird{svíňk-},
with front-grade \ird{svíňc-} in indirect singular \ird{svíňce}
and direct plural \ird{svíňce}. The transitive singular
\ird{svíňku} retains /k/. Its kinship reading does not assign it
to the restricted Class~IX pattern.

The major productive Class~III \ird{-a} subtype has singular endings \ird{-a},
\ird{-y}, and \ird{-ě}, and plural endings \ird{-y}, zero, and \ird{-am}.
In \ird{trava} `bread', singular cells select G0 \ird{trav-}, while all
three plural cells select G1 \ird{tráv-}, including before overt endings.
The indirect-plural \ird{-am} is short in this productive pattern.
The quantity-alternating zero-ending subtype represented by \ird{stráz}
retains a long direct singular and transitive plural alongside short
\ird{straz-} elsewhere.

\ird{Buda} \trsl{mountain} follows the productive \ird{trava}
pattern, with singular \ird{buda}/\ird{budy}/\ird{budě}
and plural \ird{búdy}/\ird{búd}/\ird{búdam}. Its singular indirect

\subsection{Classes IV--VI: soft, bare-root, and relic stems}
\label{sec:nm-classes-iv-vi}

Class~IV \ird{red} `blade' combines singular zero, \ird{-y}, and \ird{-e} with
plural \ird{-e}, \ird{-óv}, and \ird{-ím}. Its singular transitive selects
\ird{rez-}, whereas singular indirect and plural direct select \ird{reď-}.
These latter two cells are syncretic in \ird{redě}; their front
consonant grade supplies /ɟ/.

Class~V \ird{rán} `hand' has singular endings zero, \ird{-ou}, and \ird{-e}, and
plural \ird{-a}, zero, and \ird{-om}. The root \ird{rán-} remains invariant
in quantity and consonant grade, including before indirect \ird{-e}, hence
\ird{ráne} with /n/. Direct singular and transitive plural coincide
in \ird{rán}. Thematic \ird{-o} nouns belong to Class~VIII.

The closed Class~VI pattern represented by \ird{pět} `sun' has singular zero,
\ird{-im}, and \ird{-y}, with plural \ird{-ove}, \ird{-óv}, and
\ird{-ovim}. Its plural direct \ird{pětove} and transitive
\ird{pětóv} differ in both their ending and the position of vowel
quantity, under unchanged initial stress.

\subsection{Classes VII--IX: alternating, thematic, and kinship stems}
\label{sec:nm-classes-vii-ix}

Class~VII \ird{tězėň} `lake' and \ird{obėl} `window' distinguish a full citation
stem, a hard bound stem (\ird{tězn-}, \ird{obl-}), and a palatal bound
stem (\ird{tězň-}, \ird{obľ-}). Singular transitive \ird{-u} and plural
transitive \ird{-óv} select the hard bound stem, as does indirect plural
\ird{-em}. Singular indirect \ird{-e} and direct plural \ird{-a} select
the palatal stem, yielding \ird{tězně}/\ird{tězňa} and
\ird{oblě}/\ird{obľa}. Direct singular retains full
\ird{tězėň} or \ird{obėl}. Lexical membership,
rather than a citation ending in \ird{-ėň} or \ird{-ėl} alone, distinguishes
this restricted pattern from Class~II.

Class~VIII \ird{pivo} `beer' combines \ird{piv-} with singular \ird{-o}, \ird{-a},
and \ird{-um}, and plural \ird{-a}, \ird{-óv}, and \ird{-em}.
Singular transitive and plural direct coincide in \ird{piva}; their case
and number values are determined by the construction. \ird{Bro} likewise
has singular \ird{bro}/\ird{bra}/\ird{brum} and plural
\ird{bra}/\ird{bróv}/\ird{brem}
(§\,\ref{sec:thematic-vowels}).

Class~IX contains the kinship nouns \ird{mamka} `mother', \ird{papka} `father', \ird{voska} `sister', and
\ird{mlazka} `brother'; its restricted lexical membership is
independent of the referent's sex. Singular \ird{-a}, \ird{-y}, and
\ird{-e} combine with plural \ird{-e}, \ird{-óv}, and \ird{-ám};
singular indirect, plural direct, and plural indirect select \ird{k}
$\rightarrow$ \ird{c}, while the other cells retain the hard grade.
The vocative \ird{mamko} and the corresponding forms of the other kin terms
remain lexically restricted (§\,\ref{sec:nm-vocative}). Ordinary nouns in
\ird{-ka} do not enter this class solely by virtue of their citation shape.

\subsection{Lexically irregular nouns}
\label{sec:nm-irregular}

The noun \ird{pres} `man' retains Class~I endings but selects
plural \ird{préš-}. Its plural stem selection is lexical:
although the consonantal correspondence coincides with PAL, ordinary Class~I
plural endings do not select that consonantal grade. The productive number
selector independently assigns G1, yielding long \ird{préš-}
in all plural cases. \ird{Tóm}
`book' retains its long root vowel only in the direct singular;
all other cells select \ird{tom-}, with
exceptional singular indirect \ird{tomem}. The long \ird{ó} of plural
transitive \ird{tomóv} belongs to the ending. This lexical quantity pattern
is an exception to productive Class~I G0/G1 selection.

The noun \ird{študent} `student' combines hard-stem direct singular and transitive
forms with affricated indirect \ird{študencí}/\ird{študencím}. Its direct
plural permits \ird{študentí} and \ird{študencí}; no register distinction is
assigned to these variants. Its listed quantities are retained; the mixed
paradigm is not assigned productive Class~I plural G1 by its citation shape.
This mixed inventory is stored with the lexeme
rather than constituting another productive declension class. In particular,
the /ts/ of \ird{študenc-} is a listed stem allomorph, not the [c] supplied
by PAL on /t/. Plain \ird{študentí} also retains /t/ before /iː/;
the vowel alone selects neither of those consonantal grades. The complete
irregular paradigms are given in Table~\ref{tab:nm-irregular-paradigms}.

A noun's lexical specification identifies its citation form and declension class;
Class~II and~VII entries additionally record their bound and front grades
where these cannot be recovered from the class designation. Irregular entries
specify the relevant plural or oblique grade and exceptional endings. These
lexical specifications supplement the class inventory without changing the
three structural case values or the singular--plural distinction.

\subsection{Syncretism across the paradigm}
\label{sec:nm-syncretism}

Syncretism is class-specific. Class~VIII singular transitive and plural direct
coincide in \ird{piva}, whereas Class~IX has singular indirect and plural
direct \ird{mamce}. Productive Class~III distinguishes singular transitive
\ird{travy} from plural direct \ird{trávy} through the G0/G1 opposition,
although the ending is the same in both cells.
These identities do not collapse the syntactic distinctions expressed by case
and number. Class~V additionally has singular direct and plural transitive
\ird{rán}. Neither case syncretism nor reference to a known individual
creates a second inflectional series.

The Class~IX identity of singular indirect and plural direct can be
resolved by the clause in which \ird{mamce} occurs:

\pex\label{ex:nm-slot-syncretism}
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[guide]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Sg{}} mamce \textnormal{[[greet]]}\textsubscript{\Prog{}}.//
\glb guide.\Dir{}.\Sg{} mother.\Ind{}.\Sg{} greet-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{A guide is greeting a mother.} \lingcontext{mamce is the singular patient in the ordinary active frame}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Mamce méžime.//
\glb mother.\Dir{}.\Pl{} wait-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Mothers are waiting.} \lingcontext{mamce is the plural sole participant}//
\endgl
\xe

The shared string therefore realises two cells whose case and number
features remain distinct. In the first context the verb licenses an indirect
patient, whereas the second requires a direct sole participant.

Plural indirects distinguish \ird{-ac}, \ird{-em}, productive \ird{-am},
restricted \ird{-ám}, \ird{-ím},
\ird{-om}, and \ird{-ovim}; the same ending may occur in more than one
class, whose identity consequently depends on the whole paradigm. In
Class~VI, direct and indirect plurals contain \ird{-ov-}, while transitive
\ird{pětóv} has the long-vowel exponent. Its contrast with direct plural
\ird{pětove} is preserved independently of the referents' contextual
identifiability.

\section{Reference and inflection}
\label{sec:nm-reference-inflection}

The case--number paradigms do not distinguish definite from indefinite
reference. A contextually identifiable hand takes the same indirect form
\ird{ráne} as a newly introduced hand; neither interpretation changes the
Class~V stem or licenses coronal palatalisation.

\pex\label{ex:nm-slot-contextual-patient}
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[nurse]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Sg{}} ráne \textnormal{[[examine]]}\textsubscript{\Prog{}}.//
\glb nurse.\Dir{}.\Sg{} hand.\Ind{}.\Sg{} examine-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{A nurse is examining a hand.} \lingcontext{the hand is newly introduced during a ward report}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[nurse]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Sg{}} ráne \textnormal{[[examine]]}\textsubscript{\Prog{}}.//
\glb nurse.\Dir{}.\Sg{} hand.\Ind{}.\Sg{} examine-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{A nurse is examining the hand.} \lingcontext{the interlocutors have already identified the injured hand}//
\endgl
\xe

Both clauses retain the indirect patient licensed by the progressive. Their
reference contrast belongs to the discourse contexts, not to the case ending.
An identifiable direct-case patient in a perfective construction likewise
retains its ordinary direct form; demonstratives, possessors and quantifiers
can further restrict a nominal description without introducing a separate
case--number paradigm (Chapter~\ref{ch:nominal-syntax}).
