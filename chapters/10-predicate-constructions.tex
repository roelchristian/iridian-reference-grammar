\chapter{Predicate Constructions}\label{ch:predicates}

\section{Stative verbs}\label{sec:stative}

Stative predicates constitute a morphosyntactic subclass of Iridian verbs
whose lexical meanings denote properties, conditions or relations holding of
participants. Property roots form stative stems through the productive
verbalising suffix \ird{-n-} (§\,\ref{sec:stative-derivation}), so that
\ird{muž} \trsl{beauty}, for example, yields \ird{mužn-}
\trsl{be beautiful}. Like other finite verbs, stative predicates require an
Asp.Align exponent in the indicative, but take no copula. The continuous
form \ird{mužna} \trsl{is beautiful} accordingly consists of the derived
stative stem and continuous \ird{-a}. The obligatory expression of Asp.Align
does not, however, entail that every aspectual value is compatible with a
stative predicate. In the analysis adopted here, \synhead{StateP} introduces
the state itself, while a distinct \synhead{BecomeP}, overtly realised by
the inchoative \ird{-el-}/\ird{-él-}
(§\,\ref{sec:stative-inchoative}), introduces a transition into that state.
A simple predication of tiredness or happiness, or of a window being open,
therefore describes an obtaining condition without asserting its inception.
The corresponding inchoative derivative explicitly expresses entry into that
condition.

The distinction between state predication and change of state has parallels
in Japanese and Korean, where rate adverbials do not independently license
change-of-state interpretations of simple property predicates
\cite[§1]{smithhopperdietzelkoontzgarboden2025}. The lexical classification
of these predicates nevertheless differs across languages. Kim
\cite{kimaeryung2024}, for example, argues that Korean adjectives constitute
a lexical class distinct from verbs despite their considerable inflectional
similarities, identifying differences in present-tense marking, adnominal
morphology and the distribution of the inchoative suffix \textit{-eci}.
Iridian retains the analysis of stative predicates as a subclass of verbs,
since their inflectional and derivational behaviour is accommodated within
the general verbal system. Their lexical category is nevertheless
independent of the structural distinction between \synhead{StateP} and
\synhead{BecomeP}, which determines whether a predicate describes a state
or an event involving its inception.

The aspectual distribution of stative predicates also requires distinctions
among lexical classes and the interpretations associated with particular
aspectual forms. Tham \cite[§3.3, pp.~668--670]{tham2013cos} distinguishes
Mandarin property predicates that systematically admit change-of-state
readings from lexical stative verbs such as \textit{xiāngxìn}
\trsl{believe}, whose perfective can locate a state of belief in an earlier
interval without asserting its inception. A comparable sensitivity to
aspectual interpretation is found in Nafsan, where experiential and
persistent-situation readings of the perfect admit stative predicates,
whereas the resultative reading requires a change-of-state interpretation
\cite[§4.1]{krajinovicvanderklokvonprince2025}. These distributions show
that aspectual compatibility cannot be determined from lexical stativity
alone, and that the relevant restrictions concern the interpretations
licensed by individual aspectual operators, independently of tense marking.
Iridian permits retrospective and contemplative predication of states while
excluding simple statives from progressive and perfective selection. The
latter forms require an independently licensed eventive derivation.

The presence of overt inchoative morphology does not, by itself, establish
such a restriction. Smith, Hopperdietzel and Koontz-Garboden
\cite[§§3.2--3.3]{smithhopperdietzelkoontzgarboden2025} discuss Washo,
where an overt inchoative formed on a stativised verb coexists with
change-of-state readings induced by aspectual morphology. The requirement
that an Iridian stative predicate undergo an independently licensed eventive
derivation before selecting progressive or perfective aspect must therefore
be understood as a language-specific restriction on the interaction between
lexical structure and aspectual selection. The distinction between
\synhead{StateP} and \synhead{BecomeP} provides the structural representation
of this restriction without presupposing that the same division is enforced
in other languages.

\subsection{State types and aspectual interpretation}\label{sec:stative-stability}

Four principal lexical-semantic groups are distinguished among Iridian
statives. Quality statives, such as \ird{mužn-} \trsl{be beautiful},
\ird{hradn-} \trsl{be good} and \ird{torban-} \trsl{be bad}, express aesthetic
or evaluative properties. Dimensional statives such as \ird{svorn-}
\trsl{be big} and \ird{četn-} \trsl{be tall} denote properties interpreted
along a scale of size, extent or another measurable dimension. Experiential
and condition statives, such as \ird{řejn-} \trsl{be tired}, \ird{sledn-}
\trsl{be sick} and \ird{všihn-} \trsl{be happy}, denote bodily conditions or
subjective experiences, with the interpretation of the participant as an
affected holder or experiencer determined by the lexical predicate. Relational
statives include \ird{tvírzn-} \trsl{be courtly} and \ird{barocezn-}
\trsl{be baroque in style}, derived from nominal bases through \ird{-(e)zn-}.
Positional, possessive and locative states may also be expressed by lexical
predicates outside productive \ird{-n-} formation
(§\,\ref{sec:stative-derivation}).

\a

\gla Nužo mužna.//
\glb city.\Dir{}.\Def{} be.beautiful-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{The city is beautiful.}//
\endgl

\endgl

\glb 1\Sg{}.\Dir{} be.tired-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{I am tired.}//
\endgl
\a

\gla Rěč tvírzna.//
\glb speech.\Dir{} be.courtly-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{The speech is in the courtly register.}//
\endgl

Quality and dimensional statives are both gradable, but differ in the semantic
dimensions along which gradation is interpreted. Quality statives attribute
properties such as beauty or goodness, whose evaluation depends on aesthetic
or normative criteria, whereas dimensional statives locate their holders along
scales such as size or height. This distinction is also reflected in emphatic
attribution: \ird{mužněská} \trsl{strikingly beautiful} occurs naturally in
literary usage, while \ird{svorněská} \trsl{strikingly big} is marked outside
elevated style (§\,\ref{sec:stative-degree}). The contrast therefore lies not
in whether a property admits gradation, but in the nature of the property
being graded and the interpretation of its emphatic expression.

Further gradable statives include \ird{veln-} \trsl{be old, aged},
\ird{mudn-} \trsl{be wise, judicious}, \ird{gremn-} \trsl{be heavy},
\ird{tevn-} \trsl{be expensive} and \ird{rošn-} \trsl{be rare, uncommon},
which describe properties of age, judgement, weight, price and frequency of
occurrence respectively.

Experiential and condition statives describe states experienced by or
affecting a participant. Depending on the lexical predicate, that participant
is interpreted as an experiencer or an affected holder. Where a predicate
permits perspective-sensitive readings, their interpretation further depends
on the relation between this participant and the perspective holder. Certain
psychological statives also license an additional stimulus relation. Thus
\ird{všihn-} \trsl{be happy} has a basic valency frame containing only an
experiencer, but may introduce a stimulus through the applicative suffix
\ird{-éb-} (§\,\ref{sec:stative-valency}). Neither the availability of this
extended frame nor perspective sensitivity is entailed by membership in the
broader experiential and condition group; both depend on the properties of
individual lexical entries.

Relational statives, by contrast, characterise a participant through its
association with the domain denoted by a nominal base. In
(\ref{ex:stative-semantic-classes}d), for example, \ird{tvírzn-} identifies
the speech as belonging to the courtly register. This relation forms part of
the predicate's lexical meaning rather than introducing a separate syntactic
argument: the clause retains a single direct-case holder. Unlike quality and
dimensional properties, relational properties need not be organised along a
naturally open scale. Consequently, an interpretation involving a transition
into the relevant state requires a context that establishes what constitutes
entry into the relation.

These lexical-semantic groupings cut across the morphological classes
described in §\,\ref{sec:stative-derivation}. The quality stative \ird{mužn-},
the dimensional stative \ird{svorn-} and the condition stative \ird{řejn-},
for example, all belong to root-strong finite Class~A despite their semantic
differences. Finite quantitative class membership, valency extensions
and licences for eventive derivation are therefore specified independently
for individual lexical entries. Membership in a verbal quantitative class
does not itself license every aspectual exponent.

The contrast between a state and entry into it is visible even when both
predicates bear continuous aspect: in (\ref{ex:predicate-everyday-states}),
\ird{řejna} reports tiredness, whereas \ird{řejnéla} presents its
development through overt inchoative \ird{-él-}.

\pex\label{ex:predicate-everyday-states}
\a
\begingl
\gla Dá řejna.//
\glb 1\Sg{}.\Dir{} be.tired-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{I am tired.}
\lingcontext{after a long day; the present condition is reported}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Dá řejnéla.//
\glb 1\Sg{}.\Dir{} be.tired-\textsc{inch}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{I am getting tired.}
\lingcontext{during a walk; the speaker describes a developing change}//
\endgl
\xe

The continuous form in (\ref{ex:predicate-everyday-states}a) denotes a state
holding at the reference time, although the context may suggest that it arose
from an earlier change; only the inchoative in (b) asserts a developing
transition. The availability of such a derivative is a property of the
individual predicate, independently of its semantic group or quantitative
shape. Both \ird{všihn-} \trsl{be happy} and \ird{řejn-} \trsl{be tired},
for example, are experiential or condition statives with short roots and the
same finite class, and both license inchoatives: \ird{Dá všihnéla}
\trsl{I am becoming happy} and \ird{Dá řejnéla} \trsl{I am getting tired}.
Their shared properties do not extend to the restricted manifestative
derivation (Manifest), which expresses outward display through \ird{-eš-}
(§\,\ref{sec:stative-manifest}): \ird{Dá všihneša}
\trsl{I am displaying happiness} has no counterpart in
\ird{*Dá řejneša}, intended as \trsl{I am displaying tiredness}.

The argument structure of psychological statives requires a further
distinction according to how the experiencer and the stimulus of an experience
are licensed. Some predicates select only an experiencer, while others
lexically select both an experiencer and a stimulus or content argument. A
third configuration arises where the stimulus is introduced by overt
applicative morphology, without forming part of the argument structure of the
underived predicate. Comparable distinctions between experiencer-only and
stimulus-bearing predicates have been proposed for Korean
\cite{kimyoonshin2026}. In Iridian, \ird{všihn-} \trsl{be happy} belongs
to the experiencer-only class, although the applicative \ird{-éb-} permits
the introduction of a further argument specifying the content to which the
experience relates. The simple predicate consequently does not license an
additional unmarked object. The stimulus must also be distinguished from the
cause of the experience, since the former need not have brought about the
psychological state, and any grammatically expressed causative relation must
receive independent licensing. These distinctions allow the grammar to
recognise both lexical and morphologically derived argument structures without
attributing otherwise unattested two-place frames to individual psychological
predicates.

The three configurations are represented schematically in
Figure~\ref{fig:psychological-argument-frames}. In the first, the predicate
selects an experiencer as its sole argument; in the second, the experiencer and
stimulus are both licensed by the lexical predicate. The applicative
configuration differs from these in retaining the experiencer argument of the
underlying stative predicate while introducing the stimulus through a separate
relation headed by Appl. Table~\ref{tab:psychological-argument-frames}
illustrates the relevant distinctions through comparative examples. The Korean
predicates exemplify the lexical argument-structure contrast described by Kim,
whereas the Japanese construction illustrates a stimulus-bearing adjectival
predicate whose additional argument is licensed without overt applicative
morphology.

Simple stative predicates admit three indicative values of Asp.Align:
continuous, retrospective and contemplative. The continuous presents a state
as holding at a reference interval, whose temporal location may be
established by discourse or an adverbial. This interpretation is available
to both characteristic properties and temporary conditions, as illustrated
by the city's beauty in (\ref{ex:stative-semantic-classes}a) and the
speaker's tiredness in (\ref{ex:predicate-everyday-states}a). The
retrospective locates the holding of a state in an interval anterior to the
reference point, without entailing that the state has ceased to obtain. The
contemplative, conversely, projects the state as holding at a later
interval, although the condition may already obtain when the expectation
is formed. Both forms therefore permit the temporal location of a state to
be specified without introducing a transition into or out of it. Neither
anteriority nor projection, in itself, licenses an inchoative interpretation.

Progressive and perfective Asp.Align, by contrast, require an eventive
predicate and cannot select a simple state directly. Neither the temporary
character of a condition nor the perceptual salience of a property supplies
the requisite eventive structure. The delimitation of an occasion likewise
establishes a temporal frame without changing the aspectual character of the
predicate it contains. Thus the intended expressive interpretation of the
simple progressive in (\ref{ex:stative-progressive-contrast}a) is excluded,
whereas the overt inchoative in (\ref{ex:stative-progressive-contrast}b)
introduces a developing transition that satisfies the selectional
requirements of progressive viewpoint. The same distinction accounts for
the contrast in (\ref{ex:stative-perfective-contrast}). The rest stop in
(\ref{ex:stative-perfective-contrast}a) provides a bounded occasion during
which tiredness obtains, but does not license perfective aspect over the
simple state. Continuous \ird{řejna} can describe that condition at a past
reference interval without requiring any change-of-state interpretation.
Perfective \ird{řejnélek} in (\ref{ex:stative-perfective-contrast}b), on the
other hand, presents the onset of tiredness as a bounded event, with the
inchoative derivation supplying the eventive structure required by
perfective viewpoint. The restriction thus concerns the aspectual
interpretation and structural licensing of the lower predicate, rather than
the morphological presence of stative \ird{-n-}. Once an independently
licensed eventive derivation has been formed, the resulting predicate
retains the ordinary aspectual possibilities of its eventive class,
notwithstanding the stative morphology contained within its stem.

The retrospective pair in (\ref{ex:stative-retrospective-contrast})
illustrates the distinction between anterior state predication and an
anterior change of state. The simple stative locates tiredness in an
interval preceding the reference point, whereas its inchoative derivative
locates the transition into tiredness within that earlier interval. Neither
construction entails that the resulting condition has ceased by the later
reference point. The contemplative pair in
(\ref{ex:stative-contemplative-contrast}) exhibits a corresponding
distinction between the projected holding of a state and the projected
onset of that state. In the simple stative, the expectation concerns an
interval during which tiredness will obtain, without specifying when the
condition begins. The projected interval lies ahead of the reference point,
although the state itself may already obtain at that point and need not be
confined to the anticipated interval. The inchoative counterpart, by
contrast, projects the transition into tiredness.

Relational statives admit the same distinction where the context supplies
criteria for acquiring the relevant relation. In
(\ref{ex:stative-relational-become}a), a speech remains within the courtly
register throughout the relevant interval, whereas the inchoative in
(\ref{ex:stative-relational-become}b) describes its entry into that register
as the speaker adopts its characteristic forms. The change-of-state
interpretation depends on contextually established criteria for recognising
that transition, since the relation itself does not encode an inherent open
scale along which change must proceed. The inchoative consequently
identifies the acquisition of the relation without requiring that the
transition be understood as gradual or measurable along a lexical scale.

\pex\label{ex:stative-relational-become}
\a
\begingl
\gla Rěč tvírzna.//
\glb speech.\Dir{} be.courtly-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{The speech is in the courtly register.}
\lingcontext{the register is maintained throughout; no adoption of it is asserted}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Rěč tvírznela.//
\glb speech.\Dir{} be.courtly-\textsc{inch}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{The speech is taking on the courtly register.}
\lingcontext{the speaker is adopting the prescribed courtly forms}//
\endgl
\xe

In \ird{Dá všihna} \trsl{I am happy}, uttered as a report of the speaker's
present feeling, the speaker is both the experiencer who bears the state and
the perspective holder whose internal access licenses its unmediated
presentation. Certain experiential statives require this relation between
experiencer and perspective holder on their unmediated internal reading,
although the latter need not always be the speaker. The independent
declarative in (\ref{ex:stative-perspective-contexts}a) has the default
speaker anchor, whereas the sincere enquiry in (b) invites the addressee's
own assessment and permits the addressee's experiential perspective. The
same interrogative \ird{by} occurs in (c), but the addressee and experiencer
differ, so interrogative force alone cannot establish the experiencer's
internal perspective (§\,\ref{sec:by-pol-head}). Narrative discourse may
likewise support the third-person state description in (d) where the passage
represents the child's own felt experience, although merely observing the
child's smile provides grounds for an inference rather than access to that
experience from within. Such readings depend upon an independently
established perspective relation; the acceptability of bare third-person
assertions outside a specified perspective or source context remains
unspecified.

Attitude complements distinguish the holder of a thought from the experiencer
within its content. In the constructed pair in
(\ref{ex:stative-attitude-perspective}), the anaphoric \ird{i} resumes the
same discourse-accessible Marek: (a) represents Marek's assessment of his own
feeling, whereas in (b) Janek forms a judgement from Marek's report. The
thinker and experiencer consequently differ in (b), and Janek's attitude
does not itself supply Marek's internal perspective. In both sentences,
\ird{všihne} is the linked continuous predicate, while the complement
precedes the direct-case thinker and continuous \ird{vada} \trsl{thinks}
(§\,\ref{sec:complementation}); neither \ird{po} nor the demonstrative
independently encodes a perspective shift. In the analysis adopted here,
these dependencies are interpreted through the lexical predicate and the
existing Mood, Evid and attitude structure, without positing an additional
perspective morpheme or projection. The lexical entry specifies whether
internal access is relevant, while clause type and the particular attitude
or discourse relation identify the available anchor; no general rule assigns
every embedded clause to its matrix subject's perspective
(§\,\ref{sec:evidential-embedded-perspective}). The distinction has
comparative support in Nam's Korean matrix and embedded contrasts, including
a first-person experiencer whose tiredness is described from a reported
speaker's perspective \cite[pp.~31--32, (1)--(4); p.~43, (25)]{nam2024}.
Sakaba's Japanese examples likewise show that emotion adjectives and verbs
are not categorically distributed by grammatical person
\cite[§1, (1a--b)]{sakaba2026}, while Park proposes Mood-based anchoring for
Korean perspective effects \cite[abstract]{park2026perspective}. These
comparisons support separating person from perspectival access without
determining Iridian licensing conditions or functional-head order.

\pex\label{ex:stative-attitude-perspective}
\a
\begingl
\glpreamble \lingcontext{Marek is already highly accessible; \ird{i} refers to him,
and the thought represents his assessment of his own feeling}//
\gla I všihne po Marek vada.//
\glb 3.\Dist{}.\Dir{} be.happy-\Cont{}-\Lnk{} \Compl{} Marek.\Dir{} think-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek thinks that he is happy.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\glpreamble \lingcontext{\ird{i} still refers to the discourse-accessible Marek;
Janek bases his judgement on Marek's report of his own feeling}//
\gla I všihne po Janek vada.//
\glb 3.\Dist{}.\Dir{} be.happy-\Cont{}-\Lnk{} \Compl{} Janek.\Dir{} think-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek thinks that he [Marek] is happy.}//
\endgl
\xe

The indirect evidential \ird{-al}/\ird{-l} marks indirect access to a
proposition independently of its predicate's lexical class. In \ird{Byl
všihnal} \trsl{The child is reportedly happy}, a report concerns the child's
feeling without describing any outward behaviour; the same suffix permits
an inferential interpretation when the speaker concludes from available
evidence that the child feels happy. Vowel-final \ird{všihna} takes
\ird{-l}, yielding \ird{všihnal}, and further linking gives
\ird{všihnalě}; Evid follows aspect and any Mood exponent and precedes the
linker (§\,\ref{sec:vm-evid}). Absence of this suffix leaves evidential
access unspecified, so bare \ird{všihna} does not itself encode direct
experience, nor does a third-person experiencer automatically require
\ird{-l}. Outward expression has the separate restricted Manifest
derivation: \ird{Byl všihneša} \trsl{The child displays happiness} can
describe smiling and laughter even when the child feels unhappy, whereas
\ird{Byl všihnal} presents the internal-state proposition on indirect
evidence (§\,\ref{sec:stative-manifest}, (\ref{ex:deriv-slot-display})).
Evidential source, attributed internal perspective and outward display
therefore make distinct contributions. Perspective sensitivity also depends
on the individual predicate and reading: \ird{Dá řejna} \trsl{I am tired}
can describe directly felt tiredness, while an externally observable bodily
reading does not acquire the same perspective restriction merely through its
stativity.
Ordinary quality and dimensional statives impose no corresponding
experiential requirement: \ird{Byl svorna} \trsl{The child is big}, for
example, attributes size without representing the child's own felt
experience.

\subsection{Valency operations with stative verbs}\label{sec:stative-valency}

Stative causatives describe the bringing about of a state or a change along
the scale expressed by the base. The progressive presents that change as
unfolding, whereas the perfective presents a bounded causing event; neither
viewpoint specifies whether the causer acts directly or through an intermediary.
Stative stems in \ird{-n-} select expanded causative \ird{-er-}, as in
\ird{svorn-ér-ime}. Overt outer Voice independently takes \ird{-an-}
after the causative, giving perfective \ird{svorn-ér-an-ik} and
retrospective \ird{svorn-ér-an-av}. The stem-final stative \ird{-n-},
the rhotic causative, and outer orientation therefore occupy distinct
positions, without metathesis or a joint causative--Voice exponent.

\pex\label{ex:val-stative-caus}
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek problemam svornérime.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} problem.\Ind{} be.big-\Caus{}-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek is making the problem bigger.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Problem Marku svornéranik.//
\glb problem.\Dir{} Marek.\Trs{} be.big-\Caus{}-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek made the problem bigger.}//
\endgl
\xe

Causative can also select an already inchoative predicate. Default compact
\ird{-r-} follows liquid-final Become without changing its protected quantity:
\ird{mužnél-r-ime} \trsl{is causing to become beautiful} and
\ird{mužnél-r-an-ik} \trsl{caused to become beautiful}, with the usual
NOM/ERG causee frames. The sequence /l.r/ permits a coda--onset division;
coda /l/ vocalises by the ordinary rule, without deletion or an invented
cluster repair. Protected \ird{tvírznel-r-ime} behaves likewise, where
change into the relevant relation is semantically licensed. Become introduces
the transition and Caus the causer; their separate exponents do not assert an
additional endpoint. This combination creates no new order of Appl, Caus or
orientation and no general licence for causativising every relational state.

Constructed continuous \ird{svornéra} \trsl{makes bigger} and
retrospective \ird{svornéranav} \trsl{has made bigger} follow the same
NOM/ERG case contrast. Their forms preserve the same expanded causative and the ordinary
conditioning of outer Voice.

Stative applicatives require a lexically licensed relation to the state.
The stimulus construction with \ird{všihn-} \trsl{be happy} relates an
experiencer to the news about which the state holds. Its applied stimulus is
direct and its experiencer transitive in the retrospective:

\ex
\begingl
\gla Senica Marku všihnébav.//
\glb news.\Dir{} Marek.\Trs{} be.happy-\Appl{}-\Ret{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek had been happy about the news.}
\lingcontext{the happiness held before the reference point; lexically restricted stimulus applicative}//
\endgl
\xe

The retrospective presents an earlier interval of happiness, while the
applied stimulus identifies what Marek is happy about; the state may continue
at the reference point. Applicative \ird{-éb-} retains its own long vowel
and takes short retrospective \ird{-av}. The stimulus relation adds an
argument without eventivising the state, so this construction admits
continuous, retrospective and contemplative aspect, with no progressive or
perfective counterpart. Neither the stimulus relation nor retrospective
anteriority asserts that the news caused the happiness to arise. A causal
relation requires independently licensed causative morphology or a lexical
causative; the stimulus extension supplies no general rule admitting a causer
with every stative applicative.

Underived stative verbs are intransitive and lack the lexical patient required
by the antipassive. Their derived causatives and applicatives license additional
participants through their respective argument-building heads; those
derivations do not establish a patient-bearing reading of the stative base.

Adnominal property modification uses the dedicated non-finite attributive
formation described in §§\,\ref{sec:stative-attr}, \ref{sec:nm-prop},
whose class is specified independently of the finite stative paradigm.

Property-root-based stative stems also feed the productive derived manner
adverbs in \ird{-ě} (§\,\ref{sec:stative-adv}), yielding lexical adverbial
adjuncts such as \ird{skorně} \trsl{quickly}. These belong to the clause's
preverbal adjunct structure, not to the attributive system proper.

\subsection{Syntactic analysis of stative verbs}
\label{sec:vm-tension-stative}

The lower stativising head projects \synhead{StateP}, whose holder or
experiencer is licensed by the predicate's lexical structure. Overt
\synhead{BecomeP} selects that state and introduces change, while restricted
\synhead{ManifestP} derives its outward display; both belong below inner
valency, Caus and orientation and require their separately listed input licences. The resulting predicate is selected by ordinary
Asp.Align, whose available values depend on that predicate's aspectual
type. A stative output admits continuous, retrospective and contemplative
indicative Asp.Align; where Become, Manifest or causative derivation supplies
an eventive output, ordinary eventive aspect is available under its own
licensing conditions. The schematic containment relation is
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{StateP}&\subset(\mathrm{BecomeP}/\mathrm{ManifestP})\subset
(\mathrm{ApplP}/\mathrm{VoiceP}_{\mathrm{AP}})\\
&\subset(\mathrm{CausP})\subset\mathrm{VoiceP}_{\mathrm{orient}}
\subset\mathrm{Asp.AlignP}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Parentheses mark independently licensed structure, not freely combinable
slots: in particular, underived states lack the patient frame required for
antipassive. The established actor-introducing layer and the restrictions on
inner Appl/Voice retain their positions in the general clause spine.

A lower \synhead{ResP} represents an event-derived state when the
construction independently entails a preceding event. Lexical
\ird{hvodna} \trsl{is open} entails openness without entailing an opening
event; a result-state interpretation of an independently licensed opening
predicate entails that event as well as its relevant state. ResP has no new
universal exponent here. Retrospective relevance alone does not force a
persisting result state, and a lexical state is not automatically reanalysed
as ResP. More specific target/resultant-state distinctions require separate
constructional evidence and are not assigned by terminology alone.

The distinction between a finite stative predicate and a nominal predicate is
also reflected in negation, even where neither clause contains an overt copula:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l l l}
\toprule
\textsc{predication type} & \textsc{affirmative} & \textsc{negative} \\
\midrule
Zero-copula equative & \ird{Marek doktor} & \ird{Marek doktor staly} \\
Stative verbal & \ird{Nužo mužna} & \ird{Nužo zamužna} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip
\noindent The nominal predicate \ird{doktor} takes clause-final
\ird{staly}, whereas \ird{mužna}, formed from \ird{mužn-} with continuous
\ird{-a}, takes \ird{za-}. Both clauses lack an overt copula, but the stative
predicate bears verbal and aspectual morphology.

Neg is analysed as selecting a nominal predication in the equative and an
aspectually inflected verbal predicate in the stative clause
(§\,\ref{sec:vm-neg}). Finite property predicates contain the verbalising
suffix \ird{-n-} and inflect for Asp.Align; predicative use of underived
property forms is undocumented.

The syntactic infrastructure of the stative domain therefore distinguishes
three outputs from the same lexical base. First, the finite stative verb heads
the clause's verbal predicate and requires no copula in ordinary property
predication. Second, a class-selected ordinary ATTR form is a non-finite derivative
inside the \synhead{DP}, corresponding to the adnominal strategy discussed in
Chapter~\ref{ch:nouns}; it is not a finite predicate and cannot bear aspect or
alignment. Third, the \ird{-ě} form is a lexical adverbial derivative that
functions as a clausal adjunct in the preverbal field, not as an inflectional
member of the verbal paradigm. The same lexical base thus feeds clause-level
predication, \synhead{DP}-internal attribution, and adverbial modification through three
distinct syntactic realisations.

Underived stative predicates retain a single direct-case participant in
continuous, retrospective and contemplative clauses. Alignment alternations
arise where valency-changing morphology licenses additional participants.
Causativised statives behave like other derived transitives, showing
nominative alignment in the continuous, progressive and contemplative and
ergative alignment in the perfective and retrospective. A single-participant
inchoative retains direct case in every licensed aspect, since Become
introduces no additional participant or transitive orientation.

\section{Copular and nominal predication}\label{sec:copula}

Iridian expresses present-time or atemporal nominal classification with a
zero copula, whose nominal predicate bears the direct case, whereas finite
\ird{neh-} selects an indirect-case nominal predicate. The latter construction
has a verbal aspectual spine and ordinarily presents entry into a classification
or role; retrospective \ird{nav} also expresses a classification holding in
a past frame, without entailing a transition. Genuine identity statements,
in which both nominals denote individuals, belong to the direct-case
zero-copula construction; the indirect-case rule is restricted to nominal
predication selected by finite \ird{neh-}.

Property predication --- the expression of adjectival meanings such as \trsl{be
beautiful} or \trsl{be good} --- belongs to the stative verb system
described in §\,\ref{sec:stative}. Existential
predication through the \ird{jec-}/\ird{zad-} pair is structurally distinct and
treated separately in §\,\ref{sec:existential-constructions}. The nominal
predication strategies, their structural type, negation, and temporal range are summarised
in Table~\ref{tab:copula-overview}.

\section{Zero-copula predication}%
\label{sec:zero-copula}

\subsection{The construction and its distribution}

The default pattern for equative clauses --- those asserting identity, role, or
class membership --- is zero copula. The topic \synhead{DP} occupies the initial
position and the predicative complement appears in the direct case in
clause-final position; no overt verb is present. This is the stylistically
neutral, unmarked construction.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek doktor.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} doctor.\Dir{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek is a doctor.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Janek \textnormal{[[neighbour]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}}.//
\glb Janek.\Dir{} neighbour.\Dir{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek is a neighbour.}
\lingcontext{introducing someone at the door}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Báh němscvení kompozitor.//
\glb Bach.\Dir{} German.\textsc{rel} composer.\Dir{}//
\glft \trsl{Bach is a German composer.}
\lingcontext{talking about the composer whose music is playing}//
\endgl
\xe

Classification of a place has the same direct--direct frame as
classification of a person:

\ex\label{ex:predicate-place-classification}%
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[this-building]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}} \textnormal{[[school]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}}.//
\glb this.building.\Dir{} school.\Dir{}//
\glft \trsl{This building is a school.}
\lingcontext{pointing out a familiar place to a visitor}//
\endgl
\xe

Inclusive and class-membership predications follow the same zero-copula pattern,
with the nominal predicate in the direct case. The same case value occurs in
genuine identity statements, although identity between individuals differs
semantically from attribution of a class or role; this distinction corresponds
to the separation of referential equation and property-denoting predication
in copular analysis \cite[§2.1]{djarv2021}. The term \term{equative}
and the label \synhead{EquP} cover these zero-copula constructions here;
they do not require every nominal predicate to denote an individual.

\subsection{Structural analysis: \synhead{EquP} and the absence of AspP}

The zero copula is analysed as the phonologically null exponent of
Cop\textsuperscript{0}, which projects an \term{equative phrase}
(\synhead{EquP}) licensing the relation between the topic \synhead{DP} and
the predicative \synhead{DP}. In the unmarked present or atemporal
construction, \synhead{EquP} contains no aspectual projection and is selected
by \synhead{NegP}, when negation is present, and then by any selected
phasal or comment-domain layers before the higher \synhead{IP} layer. With
no such operators, \synhead{IP} selects the equative predication directly. Transitional interpretations instead employ \ird{neh-},
whose event structure supports the verbal aspect paradigm
(§\,\ref{sec:neh}); property predication likewise belongs to the stative
verbal system. The affirmative zero-copula structure is represented below:

\begin{center}
\begin{forest}
  syntax tree
  [\synhead{EquP}
    [{\synhead{DP}\\\textit{Marek}\\{\footnotesize\textsc{[dir]}}\\{\scriptsize topic}}]
    [{Eq$'$}
      [{\synhead{DP}\\\textit{doktor}\\{\footnotesize\textsc{[dir]}}\\{\scriptsize pred.}}]
      [{Eq$^{0}$\\{$\varnothing$}}]
    ]
  ]
\end{forest}
\end{center}

Both DPs bear default direct case within the nonverbal predication domain.
The predicate's case belongs to this construction and is independent of the
case frame selected by finite \ird{neh-}.

\subsection{Temporal restriction}

The temporal corollary of the absence of \synhead{AspP} is that the zero-copula
construction is restricted to \term{present-time} and \term{atemporal} or
\term{generic} predications. A sentence like \ird{Marek doktor} is interpreted
as holding at the moment of utterance or as a classification that transcends any
particular time. It cannot, without additional context, be interpreted as a past
or future statement. This restriction follows directly from the lack of any
tense or aspect projection: there is no functional head in the clause that could
anchor the predication to a non-present time.

Past nominal classification employs retrospective \ird{nav}, with an
indirect-case predicate (§\,\ref{sec:neh-past}), while contemplative
\ird{náž} projects entry into a classification. Neither form establishes a
general temporal paradigm for identity between two referential nominals:
the expression of past or future genuine identity remains unspecified.

Negation of zero-copula predication uses clause-final \ird{staly}; its
structure and interaction with phasal particles are described in
§\,\ref{sec:negative-equatives}.

\section{Identificational equatives and participant nominals}%
\label{sec:ident-equative}\label{sec:nmlz-predicative}

When the predicative complement of a zero-copula equative clause is a
\term{participant nominal} (a nominalised verb form,
§\,\ref{sec:nmlz-predicative}), the construction is
\term{identificational}: the topic \synhead{DP} is identified with the referent
of the nominal description, which is interpreted as definite in the following example.
The English definite descriptions here and in the related existential examples
presume contextually recoverable referents; neither unanchored indirect
\ird{lúbcí} nor participant \ird{-ou} itself encodes definiteness or uniqueness:

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Lobek Janku pjaštník.//
\glb apple.\Dir{} Janek.\Trs{} eat-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek ate the apple.} \lingcontext{verbal clause: propositional assertion}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Lobek Jancí pjaštníkou.//
\glb apple.\Dir{} Janek.\Ind{} eat-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{}//
\glft \trsl{The apple is what Janek ate.} \lingcontext{identificational equative: patient nominal}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent In both clauses, bare \ird{lobek} permits the contextually
recoverable reading expressed by English \trsl{the apple}; shared-anchored
\ird{lúbko} would explicitly mark retrieval through the common discourse
model (§\,\ref{sec:nm-def}). That anchoring contrast leaves the predication
type unchanged: transitive \ird{Janku} with finite \ird{pjaštník} forms the
verbal clause, whereas indirect \ird{Jancí} with participant nominal
\ird{pjaštníkou} forms the equative. In (b), the apple is identified with the
referent of the nominalised description. An
agent-nominal equative identifies the topic as the agent of the nominalised
event:

\ex
\begingl
\gla Janek lúbcí pjáščikou.//
\glb Janek.\Dir{} apple.\Ind{} eat-\Antip{}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek is the one who ate the apple.}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent The indirect dependents belong to the nominal case domain:
\ird{Jancí} specifies the agent of the patient description, while
\ird{lúbcí} specifies the theme associated with the antipassive-based agent
description. The latter is licensed by the nominal relational head described
in §\,\ref{sec:nominalised}; it is not an overt patient argument inside
antipassive \synhead{VP}. The same nominal derivation appears in existential
pivots, independently of matrix case or predication type.

Identificational negation rejects the proposed participant identification;
its interpretation and its contrast with finite verbal negation are illustrated
in §\,\ref{sec:negative-identificational-constructions}.

\section{The overt copular verb \ird{neh-}: transition and inchoative
predication}\label{sec:neh}

\subsection{Status and structural contrast with the zero copula}

Finite \ird{neh-} takes a direct-case subject and an indirect-case nominal
predicate in every aspect, including the perfective and retrospective.
Its ordinary transitional interpretation presents the subject as entering
the classification or role denoted by that predicate, while retrospective
\ird{nav} also permits a stative past classification. Thus
\ird{Marek študent} \trsl{Marek is a student} contrasts with
\ird{Marek študencí neha} \trsl{Marek is becoming a student}.
The indirect \ird{študencí} is the listed singular form of mixed-declension
\ird{študent} (§\,\ref{sec:nm-declension}); the case alternation does not
require a new essive or translative ending.

The indirect is analysed here as constructionally licensed predicative case:
it marks the nominal dependent of finite \ird{neh-}, whose contribution is a
property or role predicated of the subject. It introduces no additional event
participant, so its occurrence does not make the clause transitive or trigger
a transitive-case subject in perfective or retrospective aspect. Case remains
constant under negation and aspectual alternation, independently of whether
the verbal form describes a transition or a state. The rule follows the
nominal's predicative function rather than its linear position; topic or focus
placement does not reassign it.

Zero-copula \synhead{EquP} lacks \synhead{AspP}, whereas finite \ird{neh-}
projects a verbal \synhead{VP} selected by Asp\textsuperscript{0}; a negative
clause projects \synhead{NegP} above that aspectual predicate.

Polish provides a comparative instance of construction-sensitive predicate
case: an instrumental nominal predicate occurs with verbal \textit{jest},
whereas the \textit{to (jest)} construction takes a nominative predicate,
although both can describe a present state \cite[§2.2.1]{djarv2021}.
Such a distribution shows that marked predicate case need not itself encode
change. For Iridian, the case licence is attributed to the finite copular
construction as a whole, without assigning a universal case-licensing function
to a separate predicational head. The following head-final structures represent
the direct predicate of \synhead{EquP} and the indirect nominal complement of
the verbal construction:

\end{minipage}%
\hfill

whose nominal predicate remains direct (§\,\ref{sec:negative-equatives}).

\subsection{Aspectual semantics}

The continuous, progressive, contemplative and perfective forms of
\ird{neh-} characterise different perspectives on a transition into the
nominal predicate's classification. Retrospective \ird{nav} additionally
permits a past state (§\,\ref{sec:neh-past}); neither interpretation changes
the indirect case of the nominal predicate.

The \term{continuous} (\ird{neha}) presents the transition as ongoing and
unbounded: the subject is in the process of moving into the new classification.
The inchoative flavour is inherent to \ird{neh-} itself, not to the continuous
aspect.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek študencí neha.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} student.\Ind{} \Cop{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek is becoming a student.}
\lingcontext{enrolment is under way}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek študencí zaneha.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} student.\Ind{} \Neg{}-\Cop{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek is not becoming a student.}
\lingcontext{correcting the assumption that he is enrolling}//
\endgl
\xe

The \term{progressive} (\ird{nejme}) adds an agentive or effortful dimension:
the subject is actively engaged in the process of transition. Where the
continuous reports a change in progress, the progressive foregrounds the subject's
directed activity toward the new state.

\ex
\begingl
\gla Janek študencí nejme.//
\glb Janek.\Dir{} student.\Ind{} \Cop{}-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek is working toward becoming a student.}
\lingcontext{preparing to gain admission}//
\endgl
\xe

The \Ctpl{} (\ird{náž}) locates the transition in prospective
time: the subject will enter the new classification. It is neutral between
imminent and more remote futures.

\ex
\begingl
\gla Anka mamce náž.//
\glb Anka.\Dir{} mother.\Ind{} \Cop{}-\Ctpl{}//
\glft \trsl{Anka will become a mother.}
\lingcontext{sharing news of an expected baby}//
\endgl
\xe

The \term{perfective} (\ird{nek}) presents the transition as a completed
event: the subject has crossed the threshold and entered the new state. The
resulting state is typically understood as still holding unless contextually
cancelled.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Janek papce nek.//
\glb Janek.\Dir{} father.\Ind{} \Cop{}-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek became a father.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Janek papce zanek.//
\glb Janek.\Dir{} father.\Ind{} \Neg{}-\Cop{}-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek did not become a father.}//
\endgl
\xe

\subsection{The retrospective and past nominal classification}%
\label{sec:neh-past}

The \term{retrospective} (\ird{nav}) places the predication in a past
frame viewed from the current moment. It permits both a transitional reading,
in which the subject entered the state at some earlier point, and a stative
reading, in which the classification held during the relevant past period.
The indirect-case nominal predicate is licensed under either reading:

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Dá študencí nav.//
\glb 1\Sg{}.\Dir{} student.\Ind{} \Cop{}-\Ret{}//
\glft \trsl{I was a student. / I had been a student.}
\lingcontext{recalling the period when the speaker lived in another town}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Dá študencí zanav.//
\glb 1\Sg{}.\Dir{} student.\Ind{} \Neg{}-\Cop{}-\Ret{}//
\glft \trsl{I was not a student.}//
\endgl
\xe

In its stative use, \ird{nav} supplies the overt verbal predication
required for a past classification, since the zero copula is restricted to
present-time and atemporal contexts. \ird{Dá študencí nav} therefore
need not describe a process of becoming a student. The indirect marks the
predicate's dependence on the finite copular construction; it contributes no
independent meaning of change, temporariness or acquisition. Whether the past
classification still holds is likewise unencoded: the sentence is compatible
both with the speaker's remaining a student and with their having ceased to be one.

The finite case frame is limited to classificatory and transitional nominal
predication. It does not assign indirect case to either referential nominal
in a genuine identity statement, nor does it determine the case of secondary
predicates or the distribution of nominal state-frame \ird{-eň}
(§\,\ref{sec:state-frame}). A proper name used as a role description
would require an independently licensed predicative interpretation; literal
identity or transformation into a named individual is not established by the
finite \ird{neh-} frame.

The suppletive sequential converb \ird{nesu} is established independently
of the regular aspect paradigm (§\,\ref{sec:seq-copula}). Its colloquial
reduced continuation \ird{nu} has the distinct discourse function described
in §\,\ref{sec:spoken-nu}; it does not supply an additional productive
converb ending.

\section{Existential constructions}
\label{sec:existential-constructions}

\subsection{Overview}
\label{sec:existential-overview}%
\label{sec:jec}% retained for cross-reference compatibility
\label{sec:zad}% retained for cross-reference compatibility
\label{sec:jec-zad}% retained for cross-reference compatibility

Iridian expresses existential predication through a pair of suppletive verbal
roots that together constitute the affirmative/negative alternation of a single
functional head, Exist\textsuperscript{0}. The affirmative root \ird{jec-}
asserts the existence or presence of a referent; the negative root \ird{zad-}
denies it. Both inflect for the full aspect system; both are morphologically
irregular and suppletive, with the perfective and retrospective built on
distinct suppletive stems. The two paradigms are given together in
Table~\ref{tab:jec-zad-paradigm}. The contemplatives select
\ird{jec-} and \ird{zad-} with long \ird{-áž}; their irregularity in
other aspectual cells does not require a separate contemplative ending.

The affirmative and negative roots are analysed as suppletive allomorphs of
Exist\textsuperscript{0}, with \ird{jec-} in affirmative clauses and
\ird{zad-} in the environment of Neg. Neg\textsuperscript{0} receives a zero
exponent while remaining present as Neg\textsuperscript{0}$[u\textsc{neg}]$ in the syntax;
the silent operator in Spec,\synhead{NegP} contributes semantic negation
(§\,\ref{sec:neg-exst-alternation}; §\,\ref{sec:vm-jec-zad}).

Iridian has three main productive uses of the existential construction: to
assert existence or presence, including location in a particular place
(\S\,\ref{sec:existence-and-presence}), to express possession
(\S\,\ref{sec:existence-possession}), and event-modified participant-introducing
constructions (\S\,\ref{sec:existential-presentatives}).

\subsection{Existence and presence}\label{sec:existence-and-presence}

The primary use of \ird{jec-} is to assert that a referent exists or is present
at the moment of utterance or within a contextually given frame. The entity in
question stands in the direct case. \ird{Zad-} forms the direct negative
counterpart, asserting non-existence or absence under the same structural
conditions.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Byl jeca.//
\glb child.\Dir{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is a child. / A child is present.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Byl zada.//
\glb child.\Dir{} \NegExst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is no child. / No child is present.}//
\endgl
\xe

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Kroumaště na pivo so jeca.//
\glb refrigerator.\Ind{} in beer.\Dir{} still \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is still (some) beer left in the refrigerator.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Kroumaště na pivo zada.//
\glb refrigerator.\Ind{} in beer.\Dir{} \NegExst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is no beer in the refrigerator.}//
\endgl
\xe

Availability within a household can also be the point of the existential
assertion. The item and its location remain distinct constituents:

\pex\label{ex:predicate-household-availability}
\a
\begingl
\gla Kroumaště na \textnormal{[[milk]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}} jeca.//
\glb refrigerator.\Ind{} in milk.\Dir{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is milk in the refrigerator.}
\lingcontext{answering someone who is making breakfast}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Kroumaště na \textnormal{[[milk]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}} zada.//
\glb refrigerator.\Ind{} in milk.\Dir{} \NegExst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is no milk in the refrigerator.}
\lingcontext{someone will need to buy some for breakfast}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[bag]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}} na hráť jeca.//
\glb bag.\Ind{} in coat.\Dir{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is a coat in the bag.}
\lingcontext{checking what has been packed before leaving home}//
\endgl
\xe

The locative phrases restrict where presence is asserted or denied;
they do not identify a possessor. A bare noun such as \ird{hráť}
can introduce an item in this context without an overt anchor.

Within the Minimalist framework, \ird{jec-} is the phonological exponent of a
dedicated functional head, \term{Exist}\textsuperscript{0}, which projects
\term{ExistP} as the innermost stratum of the clause spine --- the slot occupied
by root \synhead{VP} in ordinary verbal clauses. Its sole argument is the \term{existential pivot}, the direct-case
\synhead{DP} whose existence or presence is asserted. ExistP is then
selected by Asp\textsuperscript{0}, which contributes the aspect suffix, and
feeds the higher functional sequence including \synhead{NegP}. When
\synhead{NegP} is absent, Exist\textsuperscript{0} is spelled out as \ird{jec-};
when \synhead{NegP} is present, suppletive Vocabulary Insertion replaces
\ird{jec-} with \ird{zad-}, leaving Neg\textsuperscript{0} with no independent
PF exponent. The following diagrams show only the existential/aspectual constituent and,
in the negative, its negation layer. Finite inflection and optional higher
operators and ordinary placement are omitted; the illustrated pivot
positions are lower chain occurrences when the full finite placement field
is projected. The diagrams do not place existential Asp above those operators. The affirmative and negative structures of \ird{Byl jeca} and
\ird{Byl zada} are shown below:

\noindent\textit{Location.}\enspace \ird{Jec-} is also used to locate an entity: the entity
appears in the direct case and the location is expressed by an indirect noun
phrase followed by a postposed clitic adposition. \ird{Zad-} negates location,
asserting that the referent is not present at the named location.

\pex
\a
\begingl

\glb Marek.\Dir{} house.\Ind{} in \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek is at home.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek dumě na zada.//

\glft \trsl{Marek is not at home. / Marek is absent.}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent The locative phrase is merged as a PP adjunct to ExistP, introducing
the spatial domain within which the existential relation holds, without altering
the valency of Exist\textsuperscript{0}. The pivot remains the sole direct
argument; the structures parallel those for bare existentials above, with the PP
adjoined at the ExistP periphery in both affirmative and negative derivations.

\section{Possessive predication}\label{sec:existence-possession}

Iridian has no separate verb meaning \trsl{have}. What English presents as
\trsl{I have X} is in Iridian construed as \trsl{there is X at me}: the
possessor stands in the indirect case, the possessed thing in the direct, and
the construction is a special case of existential predication. \ird{Zad-}
produces the negative possessive, asserting that the possessed item does not
exist within the possessor's sphere.

The ordinary arrangement of the clause places the possessor phrase first, the
possessed noun next, and the existential verb last:

\begin{center}
[\emph{possessor}.\Ind{} \ \ird{na}] \ \ [\emph{possessee}.\Dir{}] \ \ \ird{jec-} / \ird{zad-}
\end{center}

\noindent The possessed noun is the thing whose existence is asserted or denied
relative to the possessor as reference point. In grammatical terms it is the
existential pivot --- the direct argument of Exist\textsuperscript{0} --- while
the possessor is an oblique locative domain, a PP adjunct to ExistP, within
which the existential relation holds.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Bylam na tóm jeca.//
\glb child.\Ind{} in/on book.\Dir{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{The child has a book.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Bylam na tóm zada.//
\glb child.\Ind{} in/on book.\Dir{} \NegExst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{The child does not have a book.}//
\endgl
\xe

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Tóm bylam na jeca.//
\glb book.\Dir{} child.\Ind{} in/on \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{As for the book, it is with the child.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Tóm bylam na zada.//
\glb book.\Dir{} child.\Ind{} in/on \NegExst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{As for the book, the child does not have it.}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent The second pair illustrates that topic-initial order is fully
available with \ird{zad-} on the same terms as with \ird{jec-}: the alternation
belongs to the preverbal field and is independent of the affirmative/negative
opposition.

Possession and temporary custody are readily distinguished by the situation
in which the same existential frame is used:

\pex\label{ex:predicate-everyday-possession}
\a
\begingl
\gla Že na hráť jeca.//
\glb 1\Sg{}.\Ind{} in/on coat.\Dir{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{I have a coat.}
\lingcontext{someone asks whether the speaker needs to borrow one}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Že na \textnormal{[[key]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}} zada.//
\glb 1\Sg{}.\Ind{} in/on key.\Dir{} \NegExst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{I do not have a key.}
\lingcontext{the speaker cannot open the front door}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Tómo Marcí na jeca.//
\glb book.\Dir{}.\Def{} Marek.\Ind{} in/on \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek has the book.}
\lingcontext{locating a shared book that Marek has borrowed}//
\endgl
\xe

The shared anchor on \ird{tómo} in (c) identifies the book being sought,
while its initial position makes that book the aboutness topic. Marek's
custody of it comes from the supplied borrowing context; the construction
itself does not encode a contrast between borrowing and ownership.

Following Freeze (1992) and the localist tradition, possession is treated here
as a structural subtype of location: \trsl{the book exists at the child}. The
possessor phrase [\textit{possessor}.\Ind{} \ird{na}] and a plain locative
phrase [\textit{location}.\Ind{} \ird{na}] are structurally parallel: both are
oblique PPs that modify ExistP without altering the valency of
Exist\textsuperscript{0}. The affirmative and negative possessive structures
are:

\begin{center}
\begin{forest}
  syntax tree
  [ExistP
    [{PP\\\ird{bylam na}\\{\footnotesize possessor}}]
    [ExistP
      [{\synhead{DP}\\\textit{tóm}\\{\footnotesize book.\Dir{}}\\{\scriptsize pivot}}]
      [{Exist$'$}
        [{Exist\\\ird{jec-}}]
      ]
    ]
  ]
\end{forest}
\hspace{2em}
\begin{forest}
  syntax tree
  [\synhead{NegP}
    [{$\mathrm{Op}_{\neg}$\\{\footnotesize$[i\textsc{neg}]$}}]
    [{Neg$'$}
      [\synhead{AspP}
        [{Asp$'$}
          [ExistP
            [{PP\\\ird{bylam na}\\{\footnotesize possessor}}]
            [ExistP
              [{\synhead{DP}\\\textit{tóm}\\{\footnotesize book.\Dir{}}\\{\scriptsize pivot}}]
              [{Exist$'$}
                [{Exist\\\ird{zad-}\\{\scriptsize suppletive}}]
              ]
            ]
          ]
          [{Asp\\\ird{-a}\\{\footnotesize\textsc{[cont]}}}]
        ]
      ]
      [{Neg\\$\varnothing$\\{\footnotesize$[u\textsc{neg}]$}}]
    ]
  ]
\end{forest}
\end{center}

\noindent The structural parallelism between locative and possessive
existentials is a direct consequence of this unified analysis: both
constructions merge a PP at the ExistP periphery, and the only grammatical
difference is the semantic content of the oblique phrase itself. The suppletive
readjustment applies identically in both frames; the PP adjunct is unaffected by
the presence or absence of \synhead{NegP}.

Since possession is expressed through the existential verb, possessive clauses
naturally inflect across the full aspectual system, and \ird{jec-} and
\ird{zad-} participate symmetrically in these aspectual contrasts. The
continuous is the common unmarked form: \ird{Bylam na tóm jeca} \trsl{the child
has a book}; \ird{Bylam na tóm zada} \trsl{the child does not have a book}. The
progressive conveys a relation in the course of arising: \ird{Bylam na tóm
jecime} \trsl{the child is getting a book}. The contemplative projects into
prospect: \ird{Bylam na tóm jecáž} \trsl{the child will have a book}. The
perfective of this lexically alternating existential permits an acquisition
reading:
\ird{Bylam na tóm jak} \trsl{the child got a book}. The retrospective places the
relation in a prior frame: \ird{Bylam na tóm ján} \trsl{the child had a book}.
In all these forms the construction remains fundamentally existential: the
possessed noun remains the direct pivot, the possessor remains oblique, and the
aspect construes the lexically available relation or acquisition event.
This existential alternation does not make perfective aspect a general
inchoative operator on productive property statives
(§\,\ref{sec:stative-inchoative}).

Since the possessed noun is the existential pivot, indefinite possessees are
especially natural. Definite possessees are not excluded, but with them the
clause more readily shades toward a meaning of location, availability, or
custody rather than settled ownership --- a consequence of the existential
source of the construction.

\section{Event-modified existential pivots and participant introduction}\label{sec:existential-presentatives}

The existential predicate in Iridian consistently selects a nominal pivot: the
entity asserted to exist stands as the direct-case nominal argument of
Exist\textsuperscript{0}. The nominal pivots admitted by this selectional requirement fall into
three subtypes according to the internal structure of the pivot \synhead{DP}:

\begin{enumerate}[wide]
\item a lexical nominal, illustrated by unanchored \ird{byl}, whose extended
  projection has a silent Anch head (plain existential;
  §\,\ref{sec:existence-and-presence}),
\item a complex \synhead{DP} with an overt lexical head modified by a
  participant-oriented attributive (overt-head presentational;
  §\,\ref{sec:exst-overt-head}),
\item a headless \synhead{DP} whose sole content is a participant nominal
  derived from verbal structure by Iridian's participant-nominal morphology
  (headless participant-nominal; §\,\ref{sec:exst-headless-nmlz}).
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The three subtypes share a single structural identity as the nominal
argument of Exist\textsuperscript{0}, originating in Spec,\synhead{ExistP}
and appearing in the low Spec,\synhead{AspP} in the bare and presentational
aspectual structures illustrated here; the PP-initial possessive retains the
pivot in the lower existential domain. They differ in the internal architecture of the pivot
\synhead{DP}; neither low position confers aboutness topichood, and both fall
within the scope of $\mathrm{Op}_{\neg}$ in a negative existential.
Ordinary finite pronunciation precedes the particle field through the
placement dependency; PP-initial possessives retain their independently
specified PP--pivot order rather than acquiring a new aboutness topic.

The category label \synhead{DP} covers the extended nominal argument in
all three subtypes, following §\,\ref{sec:nm-root}. In the plain lexical
pivot, absence of an overt anchor leaves the Anch head unpronounced rather
than reducing the argument to its lexical \synhead{NP}. The nominal
description remains property-denoting until it composes with the existential
predicate (§\,\ref{sec:nm-anchor-types}); the DP label supplies neither
existential force nor a silent definite interpretation. The diagrams
abbreviate the pivot's internal inflectional layers: a displayed NP identifies
the lexical head constituent, and the nP in a headless pivot identifies the
participant nominal, without representing either as the whole argument.

\subsection{The participant-nominal pivot}
\label{sec:exst-pivot-arch}

An unidentified event participant may be introduced by a participant nominal,
which combines nominal external distribution with a verbal event description
(§\,\ref{sec:nmlz-predicative}). In the analysis
adopted here, $n_{\mathrm{part}}$ supplies nominal category and participant
abstraction directly, without an intervening null lexical noun. Negative
indefinites such as \ird{zide} \trsl{no one} and
\ird{deno} \trsl{nothing} provide a distinct lexical strategy under strict
negative concord (§\,\ref{sec:nm-negative-concord}); their availability does not
require a silent noun within participant-nominal pivots.

The pivot's orientation follows from the same embedded aspect and Voice
configuration as in other participant nominals
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-derived-participant}). With transitive bases, perfective
and retrospective agents require antipassive Voice, whereas active continuous,
progressive, and contemplative forms already privilege the agent. An
intransitive nominal selects its sole participant without antipassive
morphology. Patient-oriented Voice, realised as \ird{-n-} or as
\ird{-an-} after a rhotic causative, and applicative promotion retain their
ordinary selectional roles, subject to the higher causative configuration where present. Existential
predication introduces no additional operation selecting an event participant.

\subsection{Individual quantification, event entailment, and reference frames}
\label{sec:exst-quantification}

Three questions must be distinguished: what the syntax selects, what the
sentence entails, and what the discourse foregrounds. The syntax selects a
nominal individual property. An actual perfective property entails an event
when instantiated. The resulting assertion can pragmatically report that
event, even though Exist\textsuperscript{0} selects no event-denoting or
clausal complement.

At a fixed evaluation situation, let $P$ be the individual property supplied
by the pivot, after any anchoring restriction. Let $C_R(x)$ restrict the
matrix existential domain to individuals relevant in a contextual frame $R$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \mathrm{Exist}^{R}(P)&=\exists x[P(x)\land C_R(x)],\\
 \mathrm{NegExist}^{R}(P)&=\neg\exists x[P(x)\land C_R(x)].
\end{aligned}
\]
Here $R$ may supply a temporal, spatial, or discourse domain, inheriting the
existence/presence alternation of §\,\ref{sec:existence-and-presence}.
Discourse availability is not an assertion that a historical participant is
physically present now. Conversely, a contextually located existential may
assert presence at a particular place and interval. Which restriction is
intended must be recoverable from the context; it is not erased by the
presentational use of continuous \ird{jeca}/\ird{zada}.

For \ird{lúbcí pjáščikou}, let $a$ be the contextually relevant apple and
$R_e$ the embedded event reference frame. Following the participant
abstraction of Chapter~\ref{ch:derivational}, the actual perfective gives:
\[
\begin{aligned}
 P_{\mathrm{PF}}(x)&=\exists e[\operatorname{eat}(e)
 \land\operatorname{Agent}(e,x)\\
 &\qquad\land\operatorname{Theme}(e,a)\land\operatorname{PF}(e,R_e)],\\
 \mathrm{Exist}^{R}(P_{\mathrm{PF}})&=\exists x\exists e[
 C_R(x)\land\operatorname{eat}(e)\\
 &\qquad\land\operatorname{Agent}(e,x)\land\operatorname{Theme}(e,a)\\
 &\qquad\land\operatorname{PF}(e,R_e)].
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus \trsl{Someone ate the apple} entails an appropriate actual eating event.
The nominal status of the pivot does not weaken this to existence without
participation. The indirect apple dependent restricts the associated event
through the nominal relational head (§\,\ref{sec:nm-derived-participant}).
The expository shorthand $n_{\mathrm{part}}(Q)=Q$ is appropriate only
\emph{after} Chapter~8's voice--aspect configuration and participant
abstraction have yielded an individual property $Q$; it does not replace
that derivation with an identity operation on a verbal event predicate.

Contemplative \ird{pjaštážou} instead characterises a prospective or intended
eater (§\,\ref{sec:nominalised}). Write this as
$P_{\mathrm{Ctpl}}(x)=\mathrm{ProspectiveEater}(x,R_e)$, with the projected
relation interpreted in the relevant intended or expected situations, rather
than by existential closure over an actual-world eating event. Then
$\mathrm{Exist}^{R}(P_{\mathrm{Ctpl}})$ asserts a domain-relevant individual
with that prospective property; it does not entail actual eating.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Pjáščikou jeca.//
\glb eat-\Antip{}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is someone who ate.} \lingcontext{an actual completed eating event; the participant is available in the relevant domain}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Pjaštážou jeca.//
\glb eat-\Ctpl{}-\Nmlz{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is someone who intends or is expected to eat.} \lingcontext{an existing prospective eater; eating need not occur}//
\endgl
\xe

The matrix frame $R$ and the embedded frame $R_e$ are independent contextual
parameters. For example, \ird{Védnikový ryc jeca} may introduce, in a current
search, a cat seen during an earlier walk. With a past narrative frame it
may present a cat available then, without committing to its survival now.
With a discourse domain it may introduce a remembered cat without asserting
its current physical presence. In each case the perfective seeing remains
completed relative to $R_e$. These are explicitly supplied contexts for the
same sentence, not distinct tense forms or a rule forcing the two intervals
to coincide. Replacing embedded perfective by contemplative changes the
participant's event relation; changing matrix viewpoint in an independently
licensed locative or possessive frame changes the existential predication.

\subsection{Anchoring and contextual availability}
\label{sec:exst-anchoring}

The three pivot structures do not impose a general prohibition on anchored
nominals. Under §\,\ref{sec:nm-anchor-types}, bare, speaker-selected, and
shared-retrieval nominals all supply properties; existential closure is
external. Anchoring restricts which individual can satisfy the pivot, while
$C_R$ specifies the domain in which its presence or availability is asserted.
A speaker-selected participant can be new to the addressee; a shared one is
already accessible, so its presentation normally supplies new availability
or location information rather than introducing a new discourse identity.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Ščénikový byl jeca.//
\glb arrive-\Pf{}-\textsc{rel} child.\Dir{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is a child who arrived.} \lingcontext{a child is introduced without overt anchoring}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Si ščénikový byl jeca.//
\glb \textsc{spkr.anch} arrive-\Pf{}-\textsc{rel} child.\Dir{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is a particular child who arrived.} \lingcontext{the speaker selects a child; identity need not be shared}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Ščénikový bylo jeca.//
\glb arrive-\Pf{}-\textsc{rel} child.\Dir{}.\Def{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{The child who arrived is available.} \lingcontext{in an established attendance domain, the shared child is available for the next task}//
\endgl
\xe

The last reading requires a domain in which availability is informative.
Shared retrieval does not itself assert the restricted property or the
individual's availability, and speaker selection does not make it shared.
These contrasts preserve the left-edge position of \ird{si} and the
incompatibility of speaker and shared anchoring on one nominal spine
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-spec}). They do not establish a fixed scope
for \ird{si}, especially under negation or intensional embedding.

\subsection{Overt-head presentational pivots}
\label{sec:exst-overt-head}

The overt-head presentational pivot consists of a lexical head noun in the direct
case modified by a participant-oriented attributive. Its canonical shape is:

\begin{center}
[\textit{(nominal dependent.\Ind{})} \ \textit{participant-oriented-relative} \ \textit{head-NP}.\Dir{}] \ \ \ird{jeca}
\end{center}

The modifier selects the participant privileged by embedded Voice and aspect.
An overt head may classify an agent, patient, sole participant, or applied
participant. Its suitability for presentation depends on the discourse and
the participant requested, rather than on an agentive/non-agentive division.
Relative \ird{-ový} modifies this lexical head; participant \ird{-ou}
supplies a headless nominal instead (§\,\ref{sec:th-relativization}).

The presentational frame uses continuous \ird{jeca}; its negative counterpart
uses continuous \ird{zada}. Matrix viewpoint concerns the contextual domain
in which a participant is present or available, while embedded viewpoint
characterises that participant's relation to an event. The attributive may be
perfective, retrospective, continuous, progressive, or contemplative. These
two reference frames need not coincide (§\,\ref{sec:exst-quantification}).

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Kářnikový senica jeca.//
\glb read-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}-\textsc{rel} news.\Dir{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is some news that (I) read.} \lingcontext{patient/theme introduced}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Že dbórnikový kíl jeca.//
\glb \textsc{1sg}.\Ind{} hear-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}-\textsc{rel} song.\Dir{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is a song that I heard.} \lingcontext{stimulus introduced via perception event}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Védnikový ryc jeca.//
\glb see-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}-\textsc{rel} cat.\Dir{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is a cat that (I) saw.} \lingcontext{perceived referent introduced via completed event}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Staženě marébikový byl jeca.//
\glb dinner.\Ind{} cook-\Appl{}-\Pf{}-\textsc{rel} child.\Dir{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is a child for whom someone cooked dinner.} \lingcontext{promoted participant introduced}//
\endgl
\xe

In the cooking example, indirect \ird{staženě} restricts the theme
within the nominal description, while the relative selects the child as
beneficiary. The attributive is an adjunct within the pivot \synhead{DP},
rather than a separate matrix predicate. Exist\textsuperscript{0} asserts that an individual
satisfies the complex description within the contextual domain. With an
actual perfective relative, the assertion consequently entails the relevant
event; nominal embedding does not remove that entailment. Fixed continuous
\ird{jeca} in this presentational frame does not eliminate matrix viewpoint.
The broader existence, locative and possessive frames retain the aspectual
alternations of Table~\ref{tab:jec-zad-paradigm}.

overt patient inside antipassive \synhead{VP}. Neither affirmative nor
negative presentation imposes an additional ban on agentive heads.

The negative counterpart of the overt-head presentational is formed with

the contextual domain of the matrix existential.

Only singular participant-nominal inflection is specified here. The absence

\subsection{Headless participant-nominal pivots}
\label{sec:exst-headless-nmlz}

The headless participant-nominal construction employs a pivot \synhead{DP} that
contains no lexical head noun. The pivot's entire content is a participant
nominal derived from verbal structure, whose voice--aspect configuration determines
participant orientation. This construction is the default means of expressing
indefinite participant reference in Iridian.

The participant nominal has the same $n_{\mathrm{part}}$--Asp--Voice
structure as the identificational predicates of §\,\ref{sec:nmlz-predicative}, with construction-conditioned exponence and an
independent nominal case domain. The participant is selected by the complete
voice--aspect configuration (Table~\ref{tab:participant-nominals}); the
nominaliser neither assumes finite absolutive assignment nor promotes an
otherwise unprivileged argument.

\noindent When such a nominal occupies the existential pivot position,
Exist\textsuperscript{0} asserts the existence of an individual satisfying the
participant description. The event description is internal to the nominalised
pivot. With contemplative aspect, the existence of a participant does not
assert realisation of the projected event. Matrix existential closure applies
to the participant property within its contextual domain; embedded aspect and
Voice determine the event-related property (§\,\ref{sec:exst-quantification}).

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Ščénikou jeca.//
\glb arrive-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is someone who arrived.} \lingcontext{sole-participant indefinite: intransitive nominal}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Lúbcí pjáščikou jeca.//
\glb apple.\Ind{} eat-\Antip{}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is someone who ate the apple.} \lingcontext{agentive indefinite: antipassive agent-nominal}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Védnikou jeca.//
\glb see-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is something (I) saw.} \lingcontext{patient indefinite: transitive patient-nominal}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Staženě marébikou jeca.//
\glb dinner.\Ind{} cook-\Appl{}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is someone for whom dinner was cooked.} \lingcontext{promoted participant: applicative nominal}//
\endgl
\xe

The formal structure of the headless participant-nominal existential differs
from the overt-head construction only in the internal architecture of the pivot.
The pivot contains \synhead{AspP} above \synhead{VoiceP}, with the root
and its verbal categoriser in the lowest constituent.
The head $n_{\mathrm{part}}$ introduces nominal category and participant
abstraction; no lexical noun is present. Optional causative structure lies
below the outer patient-selecting Voice head and above the lower predicate,
which may itself contain applicative or antipassive morphology
(§\,\ref{sec:morph-template}). A simple applicative privileges its applied
participant; a higher causative instead determines the selected participant
according to its derived frame. Existential predication contributes no
additional event-participant selection.

\noindent The semantics is parallel to the plain and overt-head cases. The nP
denotes a property of individuals, event-modified by the verbal material it
encodes; Exist\textsuperscript{0} introduces a witness individual satisfying
that property in the contextual domain. The event description is internal to
the nominal pivot, but its actual-perfective content is part of the truth
conditions of the whole assertion. An event-reporting use therefore requires
no separate clausal complement of Exist\textsuperscript{0}.

The contrast between the two constructional subtypes is already visible in the
grammar's existential question--answer paradigm:

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Védnikou by jeca?//
\glb see-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \textsc{q} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Did you see anything?} \lingcontext{headless NMLZ pivot; referent as yet unidentified}//
\endgl
\a

\glb see-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}-\textsc{rel} cat.\Dir{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//

\endgl
\xe

\noindent In (a), headless \ird{védnikou} supplies the participant property
whose satisfaction is questioned. In (b), \ird{ryc} additionally classifies
the participant as a cat, while \ird{védnikový} supplies the event-related
restriction. The question leaves the participant unidentified; the answer
introduces a referent under a lexical description. The presence of the lexical
noun does not itself encode specificity.

\subsection{Indefinite participant introduction}
\label{sec:exst-indef-pos}

The headless participant-nominal construction is the primary means by which
Iridian expresses what English achieves with the free indefinite pronouns
\trsl{somebody}, \trsl{someone}, \trsl{something}, and \trsl{anything}. The
existential pivot is a participant nominal whose voice--aspect configuration selects the
relevant participant role.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Ščénikou jeca.//
\glb arrive-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Someone arrived.} \lingcontext{lit.\ there is one-who-arrived; sole-participant indefinite}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Lúbcí pjáščikou jeca.//
\glb apple.\Ind{} eat-\Antip{}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Someone ate the apple.} \lingcontext{lit.\ there is one-who-ate-it; agentive transitive indefinite}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Védnikou jeca.//
\glb see-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{(I) saw something.} \lingcontext{lit.\ there is a seen-thing; patient indefinite}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Staženě marébikou jeca.//
\glb dinner.\Ind{} cook-\Appl{}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Dinner was cooked for someone.} \lingcontext{lit.\ there is one-for-whose-benefit-someone-cooked-dinner; applied participant}//
\endgl
\xe

The headless nominals in (a) and (b) freely introduce the sole participant
of arriving and the actor of eating, respectively. The same roles can occur
with an overt lexical head; its presence adds a classification rather than a
restriction to non-agentive participants.

In these existential clauses, the participant nominal constitutes the pivot
without an unexpressed lexical head noun, while embedded Voice and aspect
specify its participant role.

\subsection{Negative existential indefinites}
\label{sec:exst-indef-neg}

Negative existential clauses with participant-nominal pivots express the
meanings rendered in English by \trsl{nobody}, \trsl{no one}, and
\trsl{nothing}. The derivation is structurally parallel to the affirmative,
with \synhead{NegP} added above the aspectual predicate. Its silent operator
$\mathrm{Op}_{\neg}$$[i\textsc{neg}]$ takes scope over the existential predication, while
Neg\textsuperscript{0}$[u\textsc{neg}]$ conditions suppletive Vocabulary Insertion at
Exist\textsuperscript{0}. The resulting clause denies that any individual
satisfies the nominal description. A participant nominal formed from
affirmative verbal material does not acquire a negative-concord feature merely
by serving as the pivot: its negative indefinite interpretation follows from
existential predication within the scope of negation.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Ščénikou zada.//
\glb arrive-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \NegExst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Nobody arrived.} \lingcontext{lit.\ there is no one-who-arrived}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Lúbcí pjáščikou zada.//
\glb apple.\Ind{} eat-\Antip{}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \NegExst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Nobody ate the apple.} \lingcontext{lit.\ there is no one-who-ate-it}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Védnikou zada.//
\glb see-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \NegExst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Nothing was seen. / (I) saw nothing.} \lingcontext{lit.\ there is no seen-thing}//
\endgl
\xe

The negative existential denies that any individual satisfies the participant
description, without presupposing an established event. Identificational
negation, by comparison, rejects a participant identification against a
contextually given event (§\,\ref{sec:exst-vs-ident}).

Neither negative existential \ird{zada} nor identificational \ird{staly}
negates the embedded verbal description. Participant nominalisation selects
\synhead{AspP}, including inner valency, causative and orientation
structure, and hence excludes internal verbal \ird{za-} as well as higher
Phas, Comm, Mood and clausal Evid structure (§\,\ref{sec:nominalised}).
The matrix structure $[\mathrm{NegP}\ [\mathrm{AspP}_{\mathrm{matrix}}\ [\mathrm{ExistP}\
[n_{\mathrm{part}}\ [\mathrm{AspP}\ \ldots]]]]]$ thus differs from excluded
$*[n_{\mathrm{part}}\ [\mathrm{NegP}\ \ldots]]$. The same distinction
separates a matrix phasal/comment operator over an existential or equative
predication from an operator inside its participant description. A negative
existential with
\ird{pjáščikou} denies the existence of an eater satisfying the description;
it does not introduce someone characterised as not having eaten.

\subsection{Relation to identificational equatives}
\label{sec:exst-vs-ident}

The same participant nominals that serve as existential pivots with
\ird{jec-}/\ird{zad-} also serve as predicates in identificational equatives
(§\,\ref{sec:ident-equative}). As an existential pivot, the nominal supplies the description whose
satisfiers are asserted to exist; as an equative predicate, it supplies the
description with which the topic is identified.

In an identificational equative, the participant nominal is the predicative
complement of a zero-copula clause, and the topic is identified with its referent.
In an identificational reading with a contextually established event,
negation with \ird{staly} rejects the topic as the relevant participant
while retaining the event as background:

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Janek lúbcí pjáščikou.//
\glb Janek.\Dir{} apple.\Ind{} eat-\Antip{}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek is the one who ate the apple.} \lingcontext{identificational context: eating already established; Janek identified as agent}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Janek lúbcí pjáščikou staly.//
\glb Janek.\Dir{} apple.\Ind{} eat-\Antip{}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \Neg{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek is not the one who ate the apple.} \lingcontext{same established-event context: identification of Janek rejected}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent The suggestion that someone else ate the apple depends on the
continued assumption that there was an eater, together with the rejection of
Janek as that person (§\,\ref{sec:ident-equative}). Neither \ird{-ou} nor
$n_{\mathrm{part}}$ imposes uniqueness or definiteness. The established-event
background belongs to this discourse reading, not to an unconditional
presupposition of the nominaliser. With $P$ the eater property, the negative
identification rejects $P(j)$ for Janek $j$; it does not by itself entail
$\exists x\,P(x)$ or an alternative eater. By contrast, negative existential
closure denies $\exists x[P(x)\land C_R(x)]$.

With the same nominal as an existential pivot, the affirmative asserts the
existence of an eater, while the negative denies it:

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Lúbcí pjáščikou jeca.//
\glb apple.\Ind{} eat-\Antip{}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Someone ate the apple.} \lingcontext{existential: asserts existence of an agent; no event presupposition}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Lúbcí pjáščikou zada.//
\glb apple.\Ind{} eat-\Antip{}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \NegExst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Nobody ate the apple.} \lingcontext{negative existential: no domain-relevant eater; event not presupposed}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent In the existential construction, the participant nominal supplies the
pivot, and the negative clause denies that any individual satisfies its
description, without requiring the event to be established in advance.
The existential assertion thus differs from an identification whose event and
participant description are already given in the discourse.

To assert that no participant satisfies a description, the existential
construction with \ird{zad-} supplies the relevant reading; the identificational
construction with \ird{staly} instead disputes the proposed identity of a
participant. In contexts where the event is already established, the latter
naturally preserves that background and suggests a different participant,
whereas the negative existential makes no such commitment.

Lexical negative indefinites such as \ird{deno} \trsl{nothing} and
\ird{zide} \trsl{no one} participate in strict negative concord with both
existential and equative negation (§\,\ref{sec:nm-negative-concord}). Their $[u\textsc{neg}]$ feature requires the local negative
operator, whose presence is expressed by \ird{zad-} or \ird{staly} according
to predicate type; neither the indefinite nor the overt negative exponent
introduces another semantic negation. This lexical strategy coexists with the
participant-nominal existential, whose pivot need not contain a concord item.

\subsection{Discourse profile of the two constructional subtypes}
\label{sec:exst-discourse}

The overt-head presentational introduces a referent under both a lexical
category and an event-related description. The headless construction supplies
the participant description alone, making it suitable when the participant is
otherwise unidentified. Both admit the roles licensed by embedded Voice and
aspect. Referent presentation and event reporting are discourse profiles of
these nominal structures, not additional participant-selection mechanisms.

The two constructions may therefore form a question--answer sequence:
\ird{Védnikou by jeca?} \trsl{Did you see anything?} asks whether an
individual satisfies the participant description, and \ird{Védnikový ryc
jeca} \trsl{There is a cat that I saw} introduces such an individual as a
cat. The same answer is poorly suited to \trsl{Who saw the cat?}, which asks
for the agent of an already specified event rather than for the patient
introduced by this pivot. The contrast follows from the participant selected
by the relative and the information requested in the question; it does not
make agents generally incompatible with existential predication.

An event-reporting construal foregrounds the event entailed by an instantiated
actual perfective property. It is especially useful when both participant and
event are new information. Compare the finite arrival report with its
participant-nominal existential:

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Byl ščének.//
\glb child.\Dir{} arrive-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{A child arrived.} \lingcontext{finite event report; the subject is classified as a child}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Ščénikou jeca.//
\glb arrive-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Someone arrived.} \lingcontext{an arriving participant is introduced; the arrival can be the main news}//
\endgl
\xe

Both can report an arrival in a suitable context; the second does so through
an existential participant description. Its English translation is not a
diagnostic for a different complement category. The following discourse
comparisons keep syntax and information requested separate:

\begin{itemize}[wide]
\item After \trsl{What happened?}, \ird{Ščénikou jeca} can foreground the
  arrival, and \ird{Lúbcí pjáščikou jeca} can report an eating event in a
  context where the apple is relevant. Neither needs an event already given.
\item After \trsl{Did you see anything?}, \ird{Védnikový ryc jeca}
  confirms a seen participant and classifies it as a cat; \ird{Védnikou
  jeca} confirms satisfaction of the participant description without that
  classification.
\item After \trsl{What did you see?}, the overt-head cat pivot supplies
  the requested classification. The headless affirmative alone supplies no
  further answer identifying what was seen.
\item After \trsl{Who ate the apple?}, \ird{Lúbcí pjáščikou jeca}
  asserts an eater without identifying them. \ird{Janek lúbcí pjáščikou}
  supplies the identification in the established-event context of
  §\,\ref{sec:exst-vs-ident}.
\end{itemize}

These constructed contexts illustrate the two discourse profiles. They do not
establish a separate conventional event-existential syntax or unrestricted
extraction from nominal descriptions. Evidence from independent temporal
scope, matrix case/voice relations, and extraction would be needed to
motivate that stronger analysis.

\section{Comparative and superlative constructions}\label{sec:comparison}

Comparison in Iridian is expressed analytically using particles that combine
with stative verbs. The subject of comparison stands in the direct case; the
standard of comparison takes the indirect case. Two particles are
distinguished: \ird{stá} for direct comparison (\textit{more\ldots than},
\textit{-er than}) and \ird{zrc} for equative comparison (\textit{as\ldots
as}).

\subsection{Direct comparison}

Direct comparison is formed with the particle \ird{stá} placed between the
standard of comparison and the stative verb. A comparative state admits
continuous, retrospective and contemplative aspect, while an overt
\ird{-él-}/\ird{-el-} transition also permits progressive and perfective
viewpoint where its temporal structure licenses them. Comparison specifies
the scalar standard separately from this state/change contrast:

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Janek Marcí stá četna.//
\glb Janek.\Dir{} Marek.\Ind{} \textsc{comp} be.tall-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek is taller than Marek.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Janek Marcí stá četnélime.//
\glb Janek.\Dir{} Marek.\Ind{} \textsc{comp} be.tall-\textsc{inch}-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek is becoming taller than Marek.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Janek Marcí stá četnélek.//
\glb Janek.\Dir{} Marek.\Ind{} \textsc{comp} be.tall-\textsc{inch}-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek became taller than Marek.}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent where \ird{četn-} is \trsl{be tall}. The particle \ird{stá} may also
combine with the \ird{-á} attributive form (§\ref{sec:stative-attr}) to form
an attributive comparative. The particle precedes the \ird{-á} form
prenominally:

\pex
\a \ird{stá četná pres} \trsl{the taller man}
\a \ird{Marcí stá četná pres} \trsl{the man who is taller than Marek}
\xe

Different nominal standards make the same construction useful beyond
personal height comparisons. In these schematic examples, the property
predicates retain their established forms while the noun slots specify
complete case cells:

\pex\label{ex:comparison-slot-dimensions}
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[tower]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}} \textnormal{[[tree]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}} stá četna.//
\glb tower.\Dir{} tree.\Ind{} \textsc{comp} be.tall-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{The tower is taller than the tree.}
\lingcontext{two landmarks; no growth or construction event is asserted}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[parcel]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}} \textnormal{[[box]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}} stá svorna.//
\glb parcel.\Dir{} box.\Ind{} \textsc{comp} big-\textsc{state}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{The parcel is bigger than the box.}
\lingcontext{a packaging decision; the relevant dimension is supplied by context}//
\endgl
\xe

The comparison orders the referents on the relevant scale without asserting
that either meets the positive standard for \trsl{tall} or \trsl{big}.
Neither example entails that its subject exceeds every contextually relevant
alternative; the comparative particle alone supplies no superlative reading.

A nominal standard can participate in more than one structural analysis.
Kim and Sells analyse Korean phrasal comparatives without clausal reduction,
and treat apparently clausal standards as \textit{kes}-headed nominals
\cite{kimsells2010comparatives}. Tamura instead derives Japanese phrasal
comparatives from structures containing ellipsis and argues for degree
abstraction \cite[ch.~2, especially §2.6]{tamura2026comparison}. His robot
example shows why the interpretation of the standard matters alongside its
pronounced form:

\ex\label{ex:comparison-japanese-robot}%
\begingl
\gla Taro-ga Jiro-yori ookii robotto-o motteiru.//
\glb Taro-\textsc{nom} Jiro-than big robot-\textsc{acc} have//
\glft \trsl{Taro has a robot that is bigger than Jiro.}
\lingcontext{Japanese; the narrow reading in Tamura's example~41, p.~43}//
\endgl
\xe

Tamura also gives a wider reading in which Taro's and Jiro's robots are
compared in size, rather than the robot with Jiro. That
ambiguity belongs to the Japanese construction, whereas the following
Iridian nominal schemata illustrate explicit standards in an everyday
comparison between coats:

\pex\label{ex:comparison-iridian-robot-standards}
\a
\begingl
\gla Bylam stá svorná hráť//
\glb child.\Ind{} \textsc{comp} big-\textsc{state}-\textsc{attr} coat.\Dir{}//
\glft \trsl{a coat bigger than the child}
\lingcontext{an oversized coat offered to a small child}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Hráťam stá svorná hráť//
\glb coat.\Ind{} \textsc{comp} big-\textsc{state}-\textsc{attr} coat.\Dir{}//
\glft \trsl{a coat bigger than the other coat}
\lingcontext{comparing two coats before choosing one; the indirect nominal supplies the standard}//
\endgl
\xe

The two occurrences of the coat noun in
(\ref{ex:comparison-iridian-robot-standards}b) share a lexical meaning while
identifying different coats. The established attributive construction
places its indirect standard before \ird{stá svorná}; whether an Iridian
possessive clause with a personal standard also permits the Japanese wider
reading remains unresolved. Clausal-standard formation, differential
measure phrases and degree abstraction likewise require diagnostics beyond
these nominal comparisons.

\subsection{Equative comparison}

Equative comparison is formed with the particle \ird{zrc}. The structure is
otherwise identical to the direct comparative, with the subject in the direct
case and the standard in the indirect. Negation of the equative falls on the
stative verb itself, which takes the regular negative prefix \ird{za-}
(§\ref{sec:negation}):

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Janek Marcí zrc četna.//
\glb Janek.\Dir{} Marek.\Ind{} \textsc{eq} be.tall-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek is as tall as Marek.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Janek Marcí zrc začetna.//
\glb Janek.\Dir{} Marek.\Ind{} \textsc{eq} \Neg{}-be.tall-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek is not as tall as Marek.}//
\endgl
\xe

The equative construction can likewise guide an ordinary choice between
objects, with the context fixing the dimension of size under discussion:

\pex\label{ex:predicate-everyday-equative}
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[this-bag]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}} \textnormal{[[that-bag]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}} zrc svorna.//
\glb this.bag.\Dir{} that.bag.\Ind{} \textsc{eq} be.big-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{This bag is as big as that one.}
\lingcontext{choosing a bag for the same shopping trip}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[this-bag]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}} \textnormal{[[that-bag]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}} zrc zasvorna.//
\glb this.bag.\Dir{} that.bag.\Ind{} \textsc{eq} \Neg{}-be.big-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{This bag is not as big as that one.}
\lingcontext{the smaller bag may not hold all the shopping}//
\endgl
\xe

The comparison concerns the contextually relevant size dimension; neither
sentence asserts a change in either bag's size.
