\chapter{Phonology}\label{ch:phon}

\section{Introduction}

This chapter describes the phonology of standard Iridian as spoken in Roubže and the
surrounding region. The language shows a distinctly West Slavic profile: an
opposition between hard and soft consonant stems, productive coronal softening,
velar fronting, coda vocalisation of /l/ and /v/, prevocalic approximant realisation
of /v/, final devoicing, cluster voicing assimilation, and a system of
full and bound stems distinguished by vowel presence, height, or quantity.

The description distinguishes lexical contrasts, morphologically selected stem
grades, and contextual pronunciation. The alveolar /s\,z\,ts/ and
postalveolar /ʃ\,ʒ\,tʃ/ series contrast lexically, including before high front vowels. The palatal grade derives
/ʃ\,ʒ\,tʃ/ from plain sibilants, just as it derives the soft coronals
\ird{ť\,ď\,ň\,ľ} from /t\,d\,n\,l/. Phonemic contrast and participation
in an alternation are compatible properties.

Constructions select their consonantal grades independently of the written vowel
of an ending. The velar alternants /ts/ and /tʃ/, conventionally written
\ird{c} and \ird{č}, belong to specified grades; neither should be confused
with either lexical /kʲ/, realised as a secondarily palatalised velar in
\ird{kěda}, or the palatal stop [c] in \ird{ťeda}. Antipassive
\ird{-aš-} selects a separate system of \term{sibilant replacement}
(§\,\ref{sec:sibilant-replacement}). Orthography represents the resulting
consonants rather than causing their alternations.

The distinction between a change of primary articulation and secondary
palatalisation follows Bateman’s terminology \cite{bateman2007}: the standard
realisations [c\,ɟ\,ɲ\,ʎ] differ in primary constriction from their plain
coronal counterparts, whereas [pʲ\,bʲ\,mʲ\,ʋʲ] retain a labial constriction
and lexical [kʲ] retains a velar closure.
The synchronic description identifies the stem and suffix classes selecting
these alternants; possible historical sources of the consonantal and vocalic
grades are considered separately in §\,\ref{sec:historical-note}.

\section{Vowels}

\subsection{Vowel inventory}

Standard Iridian distinguishes twelve monophthongal vowel phonemes, organised
into six short--long pairs: /a\,aː/, /ɛ\,eː/, /ɪ\,iː/, /ɨ\,ɨː/,
/ɔ\,oː/, and /ʊ\,uː/. Quantity is contrastive independently of lexical
stress, although paired vowels may differ in quality as well as duration.
The short high and mid vowels are generally more open or centralised than
their long counterparts, whereas /a\,aː/ differ chiefly in quantity.
Orthographic \orth{i, í} represent the high front series
/ɪ,\,iː/, while \orth{y, ý} represent the non-palatalising central
series /ɨ,\,ɨː/. Slashes identify phonemic categories throughout this
chapter; square brackets give their phonetic realisations, with optional
contextual detail omitted in broad pronunciations.

Orthographic \ird{ě} represents the same vowel /ɛ/ as plain \ird{e},
while also marking palatal quality on a preceding consonant
(§\,\ref{sec:orthographic-e-hook}).

Short \ird{y} /ɨ/ ordinarily has the lowered central realisation [ɨ̞],
with [ɨ] as a raised variant and further lowering towards [ɘ] in unstressed
and prosodically weak positions. The symbol [ɨ̞] specifies a target below
close [ɨ] rather than a fixed degree of lowering; it remains distinct from
front \ird{i} [ɪ] in careful standard speech. Long \ird{ý} /ɨː/ has the
higher target [ɨː], beside secondary [ɘː] in some dialects. Regional fronting
of the central series may produce short [ɨ̟] or [ɪ̠] and long [ɨ̟ː].
Such fronting need not eliminate the lexical opposition to \ird{i, í}.
The casual urban merger of \ird{ý} with \ird{í} instead neutralises the
long-vowel contrast at [iː], even where short \ird{y} remains distinct
from \ird{i}; it lies outside the standard twelve-vowel system.

Adjacent palatal and palatalised consonants favour vowel fronting, most
readily represented for /a/ by [a̟]. The ordinary low-vowel target is [a],
including in stressed syllables, while central [ɐ] belongs to the reduction
patterns described below. These contextual modifications affect phonetic
quality without adding vowel phonemes or determining the consonantal grades
selected by morphology
(§§\,\ref{sec:soft-coronals} and~\ref{sec:velar-fronting}).

A close comparison for the arrangement of vowel qualities is Northern
Welsh, whose stressed monophthongs comprise six pairs
/ɐ,\,ɐː/, /ɛ,\,eː/, /ɪ,\,iː/, /ɨ,\,ɨː/, /ɔ,\,oː/,
and /ʊ,\,uː/, together with /ə/. Its long vowels are restricted to
stressed final syllables, so the resemblance concerns inventory structure
rather than the distribution of quantity \cite{bell2023}.
Czech supplies a comparison for quantity independent of initial stress
and for phonemic /ou̯/, although its mid-vowel pairs show less qualitative
differentiation than the Iridian pairs
\cite{simackova2012}.

\subsection{Vowel quantity}\label{sec:vowel-quantity}

Vowel quantity is independent of lexical stress, so every short--long pair
remains phonologically contrastive in unstressed as well as stressed
syllables. Under comparable conditions, unstressed /aː/ is ordinarily longer
than unstressed /a/, although a stressed short vowel need not be shorter
than an unstressed long vowel. Speech rate and phonetic context also affect
duration: substantial phonetic shortening in rapid speech does not by
itself change a vowel's lexical quantity category. The contrast therefore
requires neither a fixed duration nor an invariant long-to-short ratio.

Stress is conveyed by a combination of prominence, pitch alignment, and
articulatory strength, rather than simply by increasing vowel duration. The
acute accent in vowel spellings marks length, not lexical stress. The stress
rule is given in §\,\ref{sec:stress}.

The pairs \ird{bil} [bʲɪw] `hook' and \ird{byl} [bɨ̞w] `child',
and \ird{bíl} [bʲiːw] `whip' and \ird{býl} [bɨːw] `stump', differ
in consonantal palatalisation as well as vowel quality at the phonetic level.
Since labial palatalisation before pronounced high front vowels is predictable and
non-phonemic (§\,\ref{sec:orthographic-i}), the twelve-vowel analysis
represents these pairs as /bɪl/--/bɨl/ and /biːl/--/bɨːl/:
they are phonemically minimal despite being phonetically near-minimal.
Their surface forms alone do not, however, determine whether the lexical
distinction belongs to the vowels or to consonantal softness.

Under the twelve-vowel analysis, both series are also licensed after the
unchanged postalveolars /ʃ,\,ʒ,\,tʃ/ (§\,\ref{sec:postalveolars}).
Schematic /ʃɪr/--/ʃɨr/ and /ʃiːr/--/ʃɨːr/, pronounced
[ʃɪr]--[ʃɨ̞r] and [ʃiːr]--[ʃɨːr], isolate the vowel oppositions
without an accompanying consonantal difference; these strings have no
assigned lexical meanings. A lexical pair in this environment would provide
additional evidence for the vowel analysis, although no such pair is
specified here.

\subsection{Vowel reduction}

Ordinary unstressed lexical vowels in standard speech generally retain
their characteristic qualities. Short /ɛ,\,ɪ,\,ɔ,\,ʊ/ ordinarily remain
[ɛ,\,ɪ,\,ɔ,\,ʊ], while /a/ permits mild centralisation towards [ɐ]
and /ɨ/ ranges from [ɨ̞] towards [ɘ]. Neither lowering of central \ird{y}
nor the absence of stress imposes a general merger of /ɛ/, /ɪ/, and /ɨ/.
Broad careful-speech pronunciations use [a] in ordinary unstressed syllables
and [ɨ̞] for short \ird{y}; a narrower transcription can record the
contextual variants.

Grammatical clitics and other prosodically weak material admit greater
centralisation, especially in rapid connected speech. Short /a/ may then
surface as [ɐ], /ɨ/ as [ɘ], and /ɛ/ and /ɪ/ may move towards more central
targets. These are gradient possibilities whose extent depends on prosodic
weakness and register; an unstressed suffix does not automatically undergo
the stronger reduction. Final /ɛ/ retains [ɛ] before a pause. Qualitative
reduction is compatible with the lexical quantity distinctions described
in §\,\ref{sec:vowel-quantity}.

The fleeting-\ird{e} alternation in \ird{perek}/\ird{perku} belongs to
lexically and morphologically restricted stem selection
(§\,\ref{sec:vowel-zero}). Although stable /ɛ/ survives in comparable
unstressed positions, fleeting \ird{e} deletes only in specified stem
classes; its loss is a morphophonological alternation rather than the
automatic endpoint of ordinary phonetic reduction.

\subsection{Diphthongs}

Standard Iridian has one phonemic diphthong, \ird{ou}\,/ou̯/, whose
careful-speech realisation [ou̯] begins with a closer vowel target [o]
than the [ɔ] of short /ɔ/. Coda /l/ and /v/ vocalisation also produces
surface vowel--glide sequences, as in \ird{sel} [sɛw] and \ird{tráv} [traːw]
(§§\,\ref{sec:l-vocalisation} and~\ref{sec:v-realisation}). These retain
the analysis of a vowel nucleus followed by a consonantal coda, even where
the vocalised consonant gives the sequence a diphthongal phonetic shape.

The following schematic strings illustrate the distinction without assigning
lexical meanings:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
{\sc underlying structure} & {\sc phonetic output} \\
\ipagloss{/tou̯/} & \ipagloss{[tou̯]} \\
\ipagloss{/tɔl/, /tɔv/} & \ipagloss{[tɔw]} \\
\ipagloss{/toːl/} & \ipagloss{[toːw]}
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Coda vocalisation neutralises /l/ and /v/ in the second row, whereas the
closer initial vowel of /ou̯/ and the long vowel of /oːl/ preserve the other
careful-standard distinctions. The symbols [u̯] and [w] need not denote
different glide articulations for these contrasts to hold. Regional or casual
raising of [ɔ] could bring [ɔw] towards [ou̯], although phonetic proximity need not entail lexical merger or a change in the
syllabic analysis of the underlying vowel--consonant sequence.

Other falling vowel sequences, such as [eɪ̯] in interjections and affective
expressions, belong to formulaic exclamations rather than to the ordinary
inflectional system. Such expressions introduce no additional phonemic
diphthong contrast. Sequences ending in [w] from /l/ or /v/ occur in
ordinary lexical and inflected forms wherever either segment occupies a coda.

\subsection{Orthographic palatalisation markers: \orth{i} and \orth{ě}}\label{sec:orthographic-i}\label{sec:orthographic-e-hook}

Standard Iridian uses two orthographic devices to signal that a consonant bears
palatal quality before a following vowel: the letter \orth{i} and the hook-e
\orth{ě}. The two are in complementary distribution by vowel environment and
apply to overlapping but distinct consonant classes.

\subsubsection*{The \orth{i} device}

The letter \orth{i} serves two distinct functions. Its primary role is as a full
vowel letter representing the short high front vowel /ɪ/ (or, with the acute accent,
the long /iː/; see Table~\ref{tab:vowels}). This is its value whenever it stands
alone as the nucleus of a syllable or immediately before a consonant.

A secondary, purely orthographic function operates when \orth{i} appears between a
consonant and a following back vowel letter (one of \orth{a, á, o, ó, u, ú}).
In lexically or morphologically licensed labial sequences, \orth{i} carries
no vocalic value and contributes no syllable; it marks the preceding labial
as palatalised. The vowel of the syllable is supplied by the following
letter, as in \ird{piaštou} [ˈpʲaʃ.tou̯] `food'. This silent-marker
reading is restricted to \orth{p, b, m, v}. Sibilants use their dedicated
letters directly before vowels: \ird{šarc} [ˈʃarts] `door' and
\ird{ža} [ˈʒa] `bell' contain neither a silent marker nor a high front vowel.

Licensed native labial \orth{CiV} spellings of this kind do not encode hiatus /ɪ.V/ or
/i.V/. A loan requiring such hiatus must have an explicitly specified
pronunciation; the silent-marker reading cannot itself establish an
additional vowel nucleus. No additional hiatus diacritic is needed for the
examples described here.

\subsubsection*{The \orth{ě} device}

Before /ɛ/, labial softness is written with \orth{ě}. The letter \orth{ě}
thus functions simultaneously as a full vowel letter, representing the same
/ɛ/ as plain \orth{e}, and
as a diacritical signal that the immediately preceding consonant is palatal. This
device applies to the same consonant classes that use \orth{i} before non-front
vowels, to lexical \ird{kě} /kʲɛ/, and to \ird{hě} [çɛ].
Dictionary-specified coronal spellings such as \ird{tězeň} belong to the
restricted lexical layer described in §\,\ref{sec:e-hook}; productive PAL
coronals take explicit soft letters.

The two entries \ird{šebec} `soil' and \ird{šebec} `spear' are
homonyms, with the postalveolar /ʃ/ encoded directly in the dedicated
háček letter before plain \orth{e}. The soft coronal in \ird{ťeda}
also carries its articulation in its consonant letter and takes plain
\orth{e}. In \ird{kěda}, by contrast, lexical /kʲ/ retains posterior
velar closure alongside secondary palatal articulation; \orth{ě} identifies
that lexical specification without changing the vowel /ɛ/.
The contrast between \ird{kě} [kʲɛ] and \ird{ťe} [cɛ]
therefore concerns consonantal articulation as well as spelling.

\subsubsection*{Consonants outside the two devices}

The soft coronals \ird{ť\,ď\,ň\,ľ} carry palatality in their own letters
and stand directly before vowels; before /ɛ/ they take plain \orth{e},
since \orth{ě} would repeat information supplied by the consonant letter.
Learned \ird{ř} spells /ʒ/ and belongs to the lexical conventions of
§\,\ref{sec:r-hook}. The postalveolars \ird{š\,ž\,č} are phonemes with both lexical and
morphologically derived occurrences (Section~\ref{sec:postalveolars}); they
do not participate in either device, and inserting \orth{i} or writing \orth{ě}
after them would be orthographically anomalous. Labial consonants use the
\orth{i} device before back vowels but take plain \orth{e} or \orth{ě}
according to their lexical or morphological specification before /ɛ/.
Before pronounced /ɪ, iː/, their secondary palatalisation is predictable;
before /ɛ/ or a back vowel, softness must instead be supplied by the
lexical stem or the construction.

\subsubsection*{The orthographic glide \orth{j}}

A final distinction concerns the orthographic glide \orth{j}. Unlike \orth{i} in
its palatalising function, \orth{j} represents the palatal semivowel /j/, an
independent phoneme listed in the consonant inventory (Table~\ref{tab:consonants}).
It is a segment in its own right and is always pronounced, whether in onset, coda,
or intervocalic position.

	\footnotesize

	\begin{tabularx}{0.75\textwidth}{L L L}
		\toprule
		{\sc spelling} & {\sc pronunciation} & {\sc gloss} \\
		\midrule
		\ird{daj} & [daj] & sky (direct) \\
		\ird{daja} & \ipagloss{[ˈda.ja]} & sky (transitive) \\
		\bottomrule
	\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

In \ird{daj} [daj] the semivowel /j/ occupies the syllabic coda as a full
consonantal segment. In the transitive form \ird{daja} [ˈda.ja] the same /j/ appears
in the onset of the second syllable, without any orthographic
\orth{i} being involved. A single intervocalic /j/ is syllabified with the
following vowel, as are single intervocalic /l/ and /v/
(§§\,\ref{sec:l-vocalisation} and~\ref{sec:v-realisation}). The contrast is
categorical: \orth{j} spells a phoneme that is present in the pronunciation;
orthographic \orth{i} in consonant-palatalising position contributes no segment of
its own.


\section{Consonants}

\subsection{Consonant inventory}\label{sec:consonant-inventory}

The core consonant inventory of standard Iridian is set out in
Table~\ref{tab:consonants}; restricted lexical palatality is specified
separately below. Surface [g] and [f] are not primary phonemes. The former occurs in careful
loan pronunciation and through voicing assimilation of /k/, while [f] arises
by voiceless assimilation of onset /v/
(§\,\ref{sec:lenition}). Native phonology accordingly lacks phonemic /g/ and
/f/.

The inventory classifies /l/ as a liquid and places /v/ with the fricatives
on the basis of its onset behaviour in voicing assimilation. Both segments
have coda [w], while prevocalic /v/ has [ʋ]
(§§\,\ref{sec:l-vocalisation} and~\ref{sec:v-realisation}). Their outputs
coincide in codas, but onset [l] and [ʋ] preserve the distinction; neither
[w] nor [ʋ] adds a contrastive member to Table~\ref{tab:consonants}.

The letters \orth{č}, \orth{š}, and \orth{ž} represent /tʃ/, /ʃ/, and
/ʒ/. Bare \orth{c} denotes /ts/, including before pronounced /ɪ\,iː/;
\orth{č} denotes /tʃ/. Neither letter represents the palatal stop [c],
which is written \orth{ť}. The postalveolars occur lexically and as
outputs of productive PAL applied to /s, z, ts/.

The palatal nasal \ird{ň} /ɲ/ belongs to the core inventory, with lexical
occurrences before back vowels and word-finally as well as derived occurrences
in the palatal grade. The other soft coronals cannot all be treated as
predictable variants of plain consonants either: \ird{ťeda} [ˈcɛ.da]
and \ird{hťe} [çcɛ] contain lexical /c/, while \ird{zďec} [zɟɛts]
`room' contains /ɟ/ without a suffixal PAL boundary.
Lexical \ird{kěda} [ˈkʲɛ.da] instead contains a secondarily palatalised
velar, distinct from /c/ in \ird{ťeda}. The analysis therefore admits
restricted lexical /c, ɟ, kʲ/ alongside the core inventory.
Lexically specified \ird{ľ} retains its soft quality,
although its productive occurrence in PAL paradigms alone does not establish
an independent lexical /ʎ/ contrast.

Labial softness also has a lexical source. The initial [pʲ] in
\ird{piašt-} precedes /a/, while silent \orth{i} contributes neither
a high front vowel nor /j/. This stem accordingly stores palatality on
its initial /p/, independently of the grade selected at its right edge.
Lexical palatality is represented here as a consonantal specification,
without extending a contrastive hard--soft pairing to every consonant.
The lexical spellings \ird{kaměn} `stone' and \ird{vravět} `letter'
likewise specify soft /m/ and /v/ before /ɛ/, realised [mʲ] and [ʋʲ].
These forms establish stored palatality without supplying a full set of
hard--soft minimal pairs; the independent lexical distribution of soft /b/
remains unspecified. Membership in the productive PAL mapping alone does
not establish such a lexical opposition.
Predictable secondary palatalisation before /ɪ, iː/ remains distinct from
this stored property.
Lithuanian provides a comparison for contrastive consonantal palatality
before back vowels and for silent orthographic \orth{i} marking that
quality \cite{baksiene2024}; the comparison does not imply an equally
extensive set of hard--soft pairs in Iridian.

The letter \ird{h} represents the velar fricative /x/, not a glottal [h]. Before
/ɪ, iː/ and in the specified \orth{hě} sequences it is fronted to [ç], and the
same fronted value appears by local assimilation before a following palatal
consonant in the onset. Compare \phon{hěma}{ˈçɛ.ma}{flag},
\phon{hťe}{çcɛ}{time}, and \phon{hěř}{çɛʃ}{brow}. The spelling
\orth{hě} marks [çɛ]; no silent \orth{i} is inserted before \orth{e}.
This fronting is purely
allophonic in this analysis and does not create a separate phoneme. Plain
\orth{he} also occurs, as in relational \ird{ščehezna}
(Table~\ref{tab:relstatives}); its consonantal realisation is not specified.
The examples with \orth{hě} therefore do not establish a general rule for
every /xɛ/ sequence or a contrast between [xɛ] and [çɛ].

\subsection{Coda lateral vocalisation}\label{sec:l-vocalisation}

Hard /l/ is realised as [l] in syllable onsets and as the labial--velar glide
[w] in codas, both word-medially and word-finally. The rule applies after
stem selection, consonant mutation, suffixation, and syllabification, within
the phonological word. A vowel-initial suffix can therefore place an underlying
/l/ in an onset, where it retains lateral articulation. Compare the existing
nominal forms \ird{byl} [bɨ̞w] and \ird{byla} [ˈbɨ̞.la]: the stem retains
/l/ throughout, although the bare form has a glide at the surface.

Soft \ird{ľ}, realised as [ʎ], retains lateral articulation in all
syllable positions. In the schematic softening pair \ird{ral}/\ird{raľe}
of Table~\ref{tab:soft-coronals}, the outputs are [raw] and [ˈra.ʎɛ].
Morphology selects the soft lateral before vocalisation can apply, so the
alternation continues to relate hard /l/ to its soft counterpart. Neither
liquid is syllabic, and vocalisation supplies no new nucleus
(§\,\ref{sec:vocalic-nuclei}).

The following syllabified strings schematise the positional rule; the dot
marks a syllable boundary:

\begin{center}
\ipagloss{/al.ta/} $\rightarrow$ \ipagloss{[aw.ta]}\qquad
\ipagloss{/a.la/} $\rightarrow$ \ipagloss{[a.la]}
\end{center}

Orthographic \ird{l} is retained for both [l] and [w], while \ird{ľ}
continues to mark the soft lateral. The synchronic rule maps /l/ directly
to [w], with no independent /w/ or /ɫ/ in the inventory. Coda-conditioned /l/ to [w]
vocalisation has a descriptive parallel in Brazilian Portuguese, where
Battisti and Moras document variation between lateral and vocalised outputs
\cite{battistimoras2016}; in standard Iridian the coda rule is
categorical.

\subsection{Positional realisation of /v/}\label{sec:v-realisation}

/v/ is realised as the labiodental approximant [ʋ] in prevocalic onsets and
as the labial--velar glide [w] in codas, including word-final position.
Thus the nominal forms \ird{trava} and \ird{tráv} have [ˈtra.ʋa] and
[traːw], respectively; their vowel quantities belong to the lexical
case-number grades, independently of the consonant's realisation. Both
outputs remain non-syllabic, and orthographic \ird{v} is retained.

Within the phonological word, postvocalic /v/ before a consonant is assigned
to the coda: a sequence /VvC/ is syllabified /Vv.C/, even where /vC/ is
possible as a word-initial onset. Vocalisation applies to this coda before
final devoicing and voicing assimilation, so /v/ yields [w] regardless of
the following consonant's voicing. A following vowel-initial suffix can
instead place /v/ in an onset. The schematic strings /av.ta/ and /a.va/
therefore have [aw.ta] and [a.ʋa].

Preconsonantal onset /v/ retains fricative articulation: it surfaces as [v]
before a voiced consonant and as [f] when assimilated to a following
voiceless obstruent. Word-initial /vta/, for example, schematically yields
[fta], whereas the coda /v/ of /av.ta/ remains [w]. The lexical preverb
\ird{vra-} has the licensed obstruent--liquid onset /vr/, realised [vr]
before its vowel: \ird{vranočk-} \trsl{run around an obstacle} begins
[vra], with neither coda vocalisation nor an additional vowel
(§\,\ref{sec:lexical-preverbs}).

In palatal-grade environments, secondary palatal articulation is retained
on the approximant, giving [ʋʲ] before the following vowel
(§\,\ref{sec:front-softening}). This is a conditioned realisation of /v/,
whose palatality is supplied by the construction. Any lexical palatality
belongs to the stem specification described in §\,\ref{sec:consonant-inventory};
the positional realisation of /v/ does not decide its lexical distribution.

The analysis retains onset /v/ as a voiced obstruent for cluster licensing
and voicing assimilation. It both undergoes assimilation and determines the
voicing of preceding obstruents when it is the rightmost obstruent in the
cluster, even if it subsequently surfaces as [ʋ]. The schematic sequence
/tva/ consequently yields [dʋa], with voicing assimilation preceding
prevocalic approximant realisation. Coda [w] from /v/ has sonorant behaviour:
it neither triggers voicing nor undergoes devoicing, although it is
transparent to assimilation between other obstruents. The onset and coda
distributions therefore differ in their participation in the voicing system.

The distribution has a positional parallel in Slovene, for which Unuk
describes prevocalic [ʋ] and non-syllabic [u̯] in codas
\cite{unuk2023}. Iridian's onset behaviour remains independently
specified: the voicing triggered by onset /v/ in the analysis here differs
from Unuk's account of Slovene /v/ as a non-trigger of obstruent voicing
\cite{unuk2023}.

\subsection{Lenition, final devoicing, and voicing assimilation}\label{sec:lenition}

\subsubsection*{Velar lenition}

The velar stop /k/ is frequently realised as [k͡x] after a pause and lenites to
[x] or [ɣ] intervocalically and in certain clusters. The voiced fricative
[ɣ] is distinct from the stop [g] supplied by regressive voicing assimilation
of /k/ in an obstruent cluster. Loanwords may preserve [g] in
careful pronunciation, but in ordinary speech they are commonly adapted with
\orth{h} or with the native lenited series.

\subsubsection*{Final devoicing}

Word-final obstruents are categorically devoiced in standard Iridian. Voiced
stops, fricatives, and affricates surface as their voiceless counterparts, for example:
/b\,d\,ɟ/ $\rightarrow$ [p\,t\,c], /z\,ʒ/ $\rightarrow$ [s\,ʃ].
Voiceless obstruents and sonorants are unaffected. Coda /v/ has already
vocalised to sonorant [w] when final devoicing applies, so \ird{tráv}
surfaces as [traːw]; the [f] allophone belongs to preconsonantal onsets
(§\,\ref{sec:v-realisation}). In the phasal proclitic
\ird{vy}, /v/ precedes the particle's own vowel and therefore has its
prevocalic realisation (§\,\ref{sec:class1-particles}).

\subsubsection*{Regressive voicing assimilation}

Within an obstruent cluster---a sequence of adjacent obstruents uninterrupted by a
vowel---voicing is determined regressively: the voicing specification of the
rightmost obstruent spreads leftward to all preceding obstruents in the cluster.
Sonorants (liquids, nasals, and the glide \ird{j}) are transparent to the process:
they neither block the spread nor change their own voicing value.
Onset /v/ participates as an obstruent before its prevocalic output is
realised as [ʋ]. Coda /l/ and /v/ have already vocalised to [w], which
has the voicing behaviour of a sonorant (§§\,\ref{sec:v-realisation}
and~\ref{sec:l-vocalisation}).

In \ird{nazd} [nast], the coda cluster /zd/ contains two adjacent obstruents.
Word-final devoicing converts /d/ to [t], and the regressive rule then applies

mismatch arises and no assimilation takes place. The coda /ts/ is
underlyingly voiceless and surfaces unchanged in word-final position.

The same rules apply across morpheme boundaries and therefore form part of the
regular morphophonemic surface cleanup described in

The grade is a property of the morphological exponent, not a consequence of
reading a front-vowel letter. It specifies a set of segment-dependent
correspondences, including changes of primary articulation, secondary
palatalisation, and the class-assigned velar affrication; it does not denote
one uniform articulatory operation or the insertion of /j/.
In a Distributed Morphology analysis,
\textsc{pal} licenses readjustment at the relevant stem--suffix boundary.
The input may be an already inflected complex: linker \ird{-e}, for example,
targets the end of the finite verb rather than necessarily the lexical Root.
Once that boundary has been interpreted, the output is not fed back into the
same grade operation. Mutation targets the final consonant of the selected
input, including a complex stem or inflected word; it does not search
leftwards through a cluster for another eligible consonant. Construction-specific
cluster alternants are listed separately (§\,\ref{sec:velar-fronting}).

The standard soft-stop and lateral realisations are [c\,ɟ\,ʎ], rather than
merely [tʲ\,dʲ\,lʲ]. Their occurrence in these alternations does not by itself
establish independent phonemic contrasts; lexical softness, where specified,
is retained in the stem. The phonemic nasal /ɲ/ and postalveolar sibilants
have lexical distributions as well as derived occurrences. Labials retain
their primary articulation and acquire secondary palatalisation
[pʲ\,bʲ\,mʲ\,ʋʲ], with /v/ receiving its prevocalic approximant realisation
(§\,\ref{sec:v-realisation}). Rhotic /r/ has no categorical PAL alternant.
Any secondary [rʲ] realisation is noncontrastive phonetic variation, whose
conditioning is not specified here; PAL alone does not require it.
No productive rule replaces /r/ with \ird{ř} or /ʒ/.

\subsection{Postalveolars as independent phonemes}\label{sec:postalveolars}

silent \orth{i} nor \orth{ě} as an additional palatality marker.

no assigned lexical meanings. A high front vowel supplies no categorical sibilant mutation: \ird{Marcí} [ˈmar.tsiː]

postalveolar articulation without creating an additional phonemic series
or a categorical neutralisation rule.

a postalveolar output from the same input. Antipassive \ird{-aš-} has
its own replacement classes, additive formations, and quantity alternations
(§\,\ref{sec:sibilant-replacement}). Likewise, PAL /k/ $\rightarrow$ /ts/
and Yod /k/ $\rightarrow$ /tʃ/ retain different outputs, while underlying
/ts/ gives /tʃ/ under PAL. Mutation applies once at a given boundary, so the
/ts/ produced from /k/ does not re-enter the PAL mapping at that boundary
(§\,\ref{sec:velar-fronting}).


\section{Phonotactics}

\subsection{Syllable structure}

The basic syllable structure is CV(C). The maximum is CCCCVCCC, though most syllables
fall into one of CV, CVC, CCV, CCVC, or CVCC.

\subsection{Vocalic nuclei and liquid clusters}\label{sec:vocalic-nuclei}

Every syllable in Iridian has a vowel nucleus. The rhotic, the lateral, and
coda [w] from /l/ or /v/ remain non-syllabic in careful and rapid speech
alike, whether they occur next to a lexical vowel or in a cluster created by
weak-\ird{e} deletion (§§\,\ref{sec:l-vocalisation}
and~\ref{sec:v-realisation}). A liquid
therefore contributes no syllable of its own, and its presence cannot license
a consonant sequence that would otherwise violate the cluster restrictions
in Section~\ref{sec:sonority}.

In the fleeting-vowel class, short unstressed \ird{e} deletes where the
remaining consonants can be syllabified around the surviving vowels without
unlicensed consonant loss. Thus \ird{obel} yields \ird{oblu}, with the
liquid in the onset of the second syllable, while \ird{perek} yields
\ird{perku}, with \ird{r} closing the first syllable and \ird{k} beginning
the second. The first \ird{e} of \ird{perek} is retained; deletion of both
vowels would leave an unlicensed initial cluster. Where deletion would remove
a necessary nucleus, the vowel is retained; an illicit cluster arising later
in the derivation is subject to the repair rules in
Section~\ref{sec:cluster-repair}.

\subsection{Stress}\label{sec:stress}

Primary lexical stress falls on the first syllable of the minimal lexical
prosodic word. This domain comprises the lexical stem, integrated derivational
prefixes, and inflectional suffixes. Stem-grade selection,
vowel deletion or retention, and cluster repair establish the surface syllables
before stress is assigned. Thus \ird{perek} and \ird{perku} both have initial
stress and two vocalic nuclei; neither contains a syllabic liquid. A silent
palatality-marking \orth{i} contributes no nucleus, while \ird{ou} forms one
diphthongal nucleus (§\,\ref{sec:orthographic-i}).

Integrated prefixes belong to this domain: the stressed syllable of
\ird{premrocna} is \ird{pre-}. Suffixation, including the conjunctive linker,
leaves stress on the first syllable of the resulting word. These are statements
about the left edge of the derived domain, rather than an accent permanently
attached to the bare root.

Lexical preverbs follow the same rule: \ird{mněktařime} \trsl{is reading
through} bears primary stress on \ird{mně-}, as does its indicative negative
\ird{zamněktařime}. The preverb belongs to the lexical domain, whereas
\ird{za-} attaches outside it (§\,\ref{sec:lexical-preverbs}). The same
domain includes \ird{le-}, \ird{ra-}, \ird{vra-}, \ird{ša-},
\ird{na-}, and \ird{ru-}: progressive \ird{lenočkime}
\trsl{is running out}, \ird{ranočkime} \trsl{is running into an enclosure},
\ird{vranočkime} \trsl{is running around an obstacle}, and
\ird{rustojime} \trsl{is retracing a route} bear stress on their respective
preverbs. Negative \ird{zalenočkime} \trsl{is not running out} retains
stress on \ird{le-}; its initial \ird{za-} is unstressed. In \ird{ša-},
\orth{š} represents the independent postalveolar /ʃ/, so [ʃa] requires
neither \orth{i} nor \orth{ě} as a palatality marker
(§\,\ref{sec:postalveolars}). Ordinary orthography joins a lexical preverb
to its root; a hyphen identifies a bound formative or bare stem only.

pronunciations abstract away from optional reduction and connected-speech
assimilation.

Dependent clitics attach outside the minimal lexical prosodic word. Proclitics
lean on following material and enclitic-like postpositions on the preceding
complement, without assigning a new primary stress to the extended group.
Here \term{group} describes a host and its dependants, without requiring a
separate universal prosodic category; clitic attachment and syntactic constituency
need not coincide \cite{booij1996}. Orthographic fusion likewise does not
determine the stress domain. The negative proclitic \ird{za-} $\sim$ \ird{z-}
therefore leaves stress on its host: \ird{zapiaštnik} is stressed on
\ird{pia-}, whereas \ird{zapremrocna} is stressed on \ird{pre-}.
The marker's short /a/ may reduce to [ɐ]; its vowel-initial allomorph
\ird{z-} reflects morphologically restricted deletion
(§\,\ref{sec:neg-allomorphy}).

Vowel quantity remains contrastive independently of stress, and the acute accent
marks length throughout (§\,\ref{sec:vowel-quantity}). Edge-based stress of this
kind is typologically familiar \cite{goedemansvanderhulst2013}; Czech supplies
a comparison for the independence of initial stress and quantity
\cite{simackova2012}, though its treatment of prepositions does not
establish the Iridian clitic boundary. Lexical stress must also be distinguished
from phrasal prominence: focus, contrast, and intonational phrasing may strengthen
or weaken the audible prominence of a word without relocating its stressed
syllable. The distinction is particularly useful where fixed stress has weak
acoustic correlates, as in Czech \cite{skarnitzleriksson2017}; it does not
prescribe an identical phonetic realisation for Iridian.

\subsection{Sonority and cluster licensing}\label{sec:sonority}

Iridian cluster phonotactics are described by the following sonority hierarchy,
ranked from lowest to highest:

\begin{center}
voiceless obstruents $<$ voiced obstruents $<$ nasals $<$ liquids (\ird{r, l}) $<$
glides (\ird{j}) $<$ vowels
\end{center}

This hierarchy describes the segment classes at syllabification, before
coda vocalisation. Derived [w] has glide articulation but occupies the
coda already assigned to /l/ or /v/; it introduces no additional onset
type. Postvocalic /v/ before a consonant belongs to the coda, as in
schematic /av.ra/ [aw.ra]. Onset /v/ is licensed as an obstruent, with [ʋ]
arising before the nuclear vowel after voicing assimilation. A preconsonantal
onset retains fricative [v] or assimilated [f], preserving the existing
onset patterns (§§\,\ref{sec:l-vocalisation} and~\ref{sec:v-realisation}).

The general requirement for syllable-initial clusters is non-decreasing sonority
from the cluster edge to the nuclear vowel. This licences the most common Iridian
onset types: obstruent plus liquid (\ird{tr-, dr-, kl-}), obstruent plus nasal, and
obstruent plus glide. Sequences of two obstruents in onset position are also
permitted. For syllabification, adjacent onset obstruents count as a plateau
irrespective of their underlying voicing; subsequent assimilation makes their
voicing uniform (Section~\ref{sec:lenition}). Such plateaux are tolerated
at the left syllable edge, while falling sonority between other consonant
classes remains disfavoured.

Three- and four-consonant onsets arise primarily where a prefix adjoins a complex
stem onset. They conform to the same principle: the segment immediately before the
vowel must be at least as sonorous as the one preceding it.

Coda clusters show the complementary pattern. They fall in sonority away from the
syllable peak, so that the most peripheral coda segment is typically an obstruent.
Sequences of obstruent followed by sonorant are disfavoured in coda position and
are usually resolved either by resyllabification across a following vowel-initial
suffix or by vowel retention and epenthesis
(Sections~\ref{sec:vowel-zero} and~\ref{sec:cluster-repair}).

Liquid-bearing clusters obey the same requirement for a vocalic nucleus as
other clusters. Weak-\ird{e} deletion is blocked where the remaining consonants
cannot be distributed among licensed onsets and codas around the surviving
vowels. Sonority ranks the consonants within these margins; it does not confer
syllabicity on \ird{r} or \ird{l}.


\section{Suprasegmentals}

\subsection{Intonation}

Declaratives are characterised by a falling contour, yes-no questions by a final
rise, and focused contrastive phrases by an expanded pitch excursion on the focused
constituent. The information-structural uses are discussed in
 Chapter~\ref{ch:infstr}. A tonal inventory distinguishing continuing,
 shifted, and contrastive topics from narrow focus is not specified;
 neither a particular pitch rise nor an obligatory boundary follows from
 the lexical stress rule.


\section{Morphophonemic processes}\label{sec:morphophon}

Morphological constructions select vocalic and consonantal grades at the
boundary where their exponents attach. Palatal-grade selection supplies the
coronal and labial correspondences of §\,\ref{sec:soft-coronals};
participating velars have the front- and yod-grade alternants described in
§\,\ref{sec:velar-fronting}. Antipassive sibilant replacement is a separate
operation. High front vowels do not impose a further categorical
sibilant mutation after morphology has selected the consonantal grade.

The nominal pair \ird{perek}/\ird{perk-} differs in the presence of an
unstressed vowel, whereas \ird{lobek}/\ird{lubk-} additionally differs in
root-vowel height. The irrealis \ird{zdičev}, from \ird{zděk-}, has a raised
root vowel without vowel loss. These distributions distinguish three independent
properties: membership in the fleeting-vowel class, selection of a raised grade,
and selection of a quantity grade. Each belongs to the lexeme and its
morphological construction; none adds a vowel phoneme to the inventory.

Within each construction, the full or bound stem is selected before its
exponent is attached and the associated consonant mutation applies:

\begin{center}
\textsc{construction and stem class} $\rightarrow$ \textsc{stem grade}
$\rightarrow$ \textsc{suffixation and consonant mutation} $\rightarrow$
\textsc{syllabification and licensed repair} $\rightarrow$
\textsc{coda vocalisation} $\rightarrow$ \textsc{voicing}
$\rightarrow$ \textsc{phonetic and orthographic realisation}
\end{center}

Lexical preverbs select no independent consonantal mutation, vowel-height
change, or quantity grade. Their derivatives retain the base's thematic class
and undergo the construction-selected alternations at subsequent suffixal
boundaries. Productive examples of \ird{le-}, \ird{ra-}, \ird{vra-},
\ird{ša-}, \ird{na-}, and \ird{ru-} are confined here to
consonant-initial bases, since the present description establishes no general
resolution of hiatus at these prefix--root boundaries. Combinations with
vowel-initial roots require individual lexical specification; automatic
contraction, deletion, epenthesis, and glide insertion cannot be inferred from
the prefix alone.

Consonant mutation and syllabification precede coda /l/ and /v/
vocalisation. The resulting [w] has sonorant behaviour when final devoicing
and cluster voicing apply to the remaining obstruents. Prevocalic onset /v/
is subsequently realised as [ʋ] or, where secondary palatal articulation is
licensed, [ʋʲ] (§§\,\ref{sec:l-vocalisation} and~\ref{sec:v-realisation}).

Thus \ird{lobek} selects \ird{lubk-} before transitive \ird{-u}, while
\ird{perek} selects \ird{perk-}; a front-vocalic ending subsequently yields
\ird{lubcí} or \ird{perce}. The stem \ird{zděk-} selects its raised modal
grade before \ird{-ěv}, whose velar mutation gives \ird{zdičev}. Outer
anchoring morphology has its own stem-selection conditions
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-def-allomorph}).

Sibilant replacement belongs to the antipassive construction and may itself
select a lexical quantity grade. Its output can undergo subsequent suffixation
and the ordinary phonology. Analogical levelling supplies the stored stem
relations of modern lexemes; it is not a final operation applied after spelling.
The relative order of two independent changes is fixed only where their
interaction makes different predictions.

\subsection{Suffix classes and their mutation triggers}\label{sec:suffix-classes}

A construction selects both a vocalic stem and a consonantal grade. These
choices are independent: the same palatal-grade ending may follow a stable,
reduced, or raised bound stem. Its surface vowel neither predicts stem raising
nor determines mutation. Comparable suffix-specific patterns in Czech include
different velar outcomes before similar front vowels
\cite{caviranivandenwyngaerd2026}; the comparison supports the
separation of morphology from phonetic context, without supplying an Iridian
historical derivation.

\footnotesize
\caption{Consonantal grades selected by morphological constructions.}\label{tab:morph-trigger}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{L L L}
\toprule

\midrule
Plain & conditional \ird{-ic}; nonmutating case exponents, including Class~V \ird{-e}; ordinary nonmutating aspect and converb suffixes & no categorical mutation; contextual phonetics still applies \\

\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The labels identify synchronic classes; in particular, \term{yod grade} does
not posit a pronounced or underlying /j/ in the modern suffix. Membership is
specified for an exponent in its construction, so homophonous nominal and verbal
\ird{-ení}, or nominal \ird{-e} in different declensions, need not select the
same grade. Sequential converb \ird{-ení}, for example, is plain-grade, while
front-coded nominal \ird{-ení} is palatal-grade. Class~I plural \ird{-e}, as
in \ird{byle}, and Class~V \ird{-e}, as in \ird{ráne}, do not acquire mutation
merely from their spelling.

Full or bound stem selection, height grades, and quantity grades are recorded
separately. Thus nominal \ird{perek} selects \ird{perk-}, \ird{lobek}
selects \ird{lubk-}, and the irrealis of \ird{zděk-} selects a short raised
vowel independently of its yod-grade consonant. Linker \ird{-e} and
participant nominaliser \ird{-ou} select perfective bound \ird{-ik-};
only the former additionally mutates the final velar. Antipassive
\ird{-aš-} has its own replacement classes rather than a palatal-grade
specification.

Grade selection is productive within an assigned morphological class. New
roots may enter a productive nominal or verbal class, but a front vowel alone
does not assign an unfamiliar root a raised grade, a lexical velar alternant,
or an exceptional cluster stem.

\subsection{Thematic nominal endings}\label{sec:thematic-vowels}

The Class~VIII forms \ird{bro}, \ird{bra}, and \ird{brum} contain a constant consonantal
root \ird{br-} and the class-specific endings \ird{-o}, \ird{-a}, and
\ird{-um}. Their terminal vowels realise the case paradigm rather than
root-vowel ablaut:

\pex
\a \ird{br-} + \ird{-o} $\rightarrow$ \ird{bro}
\a \ird{br-} + \ird{-a} $\rightarrow$ \ird{bra}
\a \ird{br-} + \ird{-um} $\rightarrow$ \ird{brum}
\xe

In the bare-root Class~V represented by \ird{rán}, the singular endings are
zero, \ird{-ou}, and \ird{-e}. The long root vowel remains constant in
\ird{rán}, \ird{ránou}, and \ird{ráne}. The ending alternation leaves the root vowel and its quantity unchanged.

The anchored direct forms of Class~VIII, such as singular \ird{bró}
and plural \ird{brá}, select cumulative case-number--anchoring endings.
Their long vowels belong to those endings, while the root \ird{br-}
remains constant (§\,\ref{sec:nm-def-allomorph}).

\subsection{Fleeting \ird{e} and reduced stems}\label{sec:vowel-zero}

Alternating nouns such as \ird{Janek}, \ird{perek}, and \ird{obel} have
bound stems \ird{Jank-}, \ird{perk-}, and \ird{obl-}. Across these alternating classes one
short unstressed \ird{e} in the final stem syllable alternates with zero.
The stressed vowel and the consonant sequence are preserved:

\pex
\a \ird{Janek} + \ird{-u} $\rightarrow$ \ird{Janku}
\a \ird{perek} + \ird{-u} $\rightarrow$ \ird{perku}
\a \ird{obel} + \ird{-u} $\rightarrow$ \ird{oblu}
\a \ird{Merek} + \ird{-í} $\rightarrow$ \ird{Mercí}
\xe

The term \term{fleeting} or \term{weak e} names this alternation, not an
additional phoneme or a reduced vowel obligatorily distinct in pronunciation.
Stable stems retain ordinary \ird{e} in the same morphological environment.
Membership in the alternating class must therefore be acquired with the lexeme;
within that class the relation between full and bound stems is regular. The
analysis locates lexical exceptionality on the morpheme rather than on an
otherwise inaudible vowel distinction, following the representational option
developed for Russian by Gouskova \cite{gouskova2012}. The Iridian class has its own distribution, which is specified by the
paradigms below.

Bound-stem morphology selects the reduced stem before the licensed case-number
endings and, in alternating nouns, before direct anchoring. Selected verbal and
nominalising constructions also admit this relation. The purposive form
\ird{raznit}, for example, selects the reduced stem \ird{razn-} of
\ird{razeň-}; the hard nasal belongs to that lexical bound stem. Vowel loss
alone does not predict the loss of softness.

Deletion is blocked if the remaining consonants cannot be syllabified around
vocalic nuclei without unlicensed consonant loss. Obstruent--sonorant onsets
and sonorant--obstruent coda--onset sequences provide different syllabifications:
\ird{oblu} has \ird{ob.lu}, while \ird{perku} has \ird{per.ku}. The first
vowel of \ird{perek} remains stressed and cannot delete. Liquids are always
non-syllabic (§\,\ref{sec:vocalic-nuclei}). Ordinary voicing assimilation can
apply to the retained cluster, but consonant deletion is not a productive means
of enabling vowel loss.

New lexical roots and loans have stable vocalism unless assigned to an
alternating class. Native nominal formations with alternating \ird{-ek} can
productively assign the fleeting-vowel pattern to the derived noun; this nominal
suffix is distinct from verbal perfective \ird{-ek}. In longer derivatives,
stem selection applies in the domain of the relevant construction, so an outer
suffix does not automatically delete a vowel inside an already formed stem.

\subsection{Raised grades and vowel quantity}\label{sec:vowel-raising}

The noun \ird{lobek} selects \ird{lubk-} throughout its bound paradigm,
including \ird{lubku}, \ird{lubcí}, and \ird{lubce}. By contrast,
\ird{perek} retains the initial mid vowel in \ird{perk-}, despite sharing
\ird{-u} with \ird{lobek}. A raised bound grade is consequently a property of
a lexical subclass, independent of the surface vowel of the ending.

The height correspondences preserve phonemic quantity:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
short: & \ird{e/ě} $\leftrightarrow$ \ird{i}, \quad \ird{o} $\leftrightarrow$ \ird{u} \\
long: & \ird{é} $\leftrightarrow$ \ird{í}, \quad \ird{ó} $\leftrightarrow$ \ird{ú}
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The short high vowels have their ordinary lax realisations [ɪ, ʊ]; the long
vowels remain [iː, uː]. Since \ird{ě} and \ird{e} represent the same vowel
phoneme, they do not define different height grades. Raising does not lengthen a
short root vowel or supply a nucleus to a consonant cluster.

\pex
\a \ird{lobek} + \ird{-u} $\rightarrow$ \ird{lubku} \lingcontext{reduced and back-raised bound stem}
\a \ird{pěštel} + \ird{-í} $\rightarrow$ \ird{pištlí} \lingcontext{reduced stem; short root vowel raises to short i}
\a \ird{zděk-} + \ird{-ěv} $\rightarrow$ \ird{zdičev} \lingcontext{short raised modal grade; k $\rightarrow$ č; no vowel loss}
\xe

Verbal raising belongs to specified stem--construction combinations, especially
the irrealis. It does not follow from front-vocalic suffixation as such:
\ird{zděcir}, with negative irrealis \ird{-ir}, retains its mid root vowel.
The linker-selected \ird{-ik-} grade of the perfective is suffixal allomorphy,
not an instruction to raise every preceding root vowel
(§\,\ref{sec:pf-dissim}).

The inherited nominal raised grades are distinct from the productive assignment
of fleeting-vowel membership in native formations. An unfamiliar noun with a
mid vowel and ending \ird{-u} does not automatically join the raised class.
Quantity grades, including \ird{stráz}/\ird{straz-}, are specified separately
(§\,\ref{sec:comp-lengthening}).

\subsection{Soft-stem selection}\label{sec:front-softening}

The palatal grade replaces /t\,d\,n\,l/ with [c\,ɟ\,ɲ\,ʎ] and /s\,z\,ts/
with /ʃ\,ʒ\,tʃ/, while labials acquire secondary palatalisation. The standard
spelling writes \ird{ť\,ď\,ň\,ľ} and \ird{š\,ž\,č} explicitly in
every vowel environment. A rhotic
retains /r/ without obligatory secondary palatalisation
(§\,\ref{sec:soft-coronals}).

Palatal-grade selection precedes syllabification and coda lateral vocalisation,
so its lateral output [ʎ] remains unaffected by vocalisation of hard /l/.
The secondary palatal articulation of /v/ is realised on prevocalic [ʋʲ]
(§§\,\ref{sec:l-vocalisation} and~\ref{sec:v-realisation}).

\pex
\a \ird{rat} + \ird{-e} $\rightarrow$ \ird{raťe}
\lingcontext{PAL linker selects [c]}
\a \ird{rad} + \ird{-í} $\rightarrow$ \ird{raďí}
\lingcontext{PAL nominal ending selects [ɟ]}
\a \ird{ran} + \ird{-ě} $\rightarrow$ \ird{raňe}
\lingcontext{PAL selects /ɲ/; plain e follows explicit ň}
\a \ird{ral} + \ird{-e} $\rightarrow$ \ird{raľe}
\lingcontext{PAL linker selects [ʎ]}
\a \ird{les} + \ird{-ě} $\rightarrow$ \ird{leše}
\lingcontext{indirect nominal ending; PAL s $\rightarrow$ š}
\a \ird{stráz} + \ird{-ě} $\rightarrow$ \ird{straže}
\lingcontext{short nominal stem grade; PAL z $\rightarrow$ ž}
\a \ird{rac} + \ird{-e} $\rightarrow$ \ird{rače}
\lingcontext{schematic /ts/-final input; PAL selects /tʃ/}
\xe

Already palatal consonants retain their quality. The postalveolars
/ʃ\,ʒ\,tʃ/ retain their articulation, including when selected as outputs
of this productive mapping.
Sibilant replacement in antipassives is governed by its own construction.

Mutation operates on the selected bound stem, so vocalic and consonantal
changes must be distinguished:

\pex
\a \ird{lobek} + \ird{-í} $\rightarrow$ \ird{lubcí}
\lingcontext{reduced raised stem; front-grade k $\rightarrow$ ts}
\a \ird{Janek} + \ird{-í} $\rightarrow$ \ird{Jancí}
\lingcontext{reduced stem; front-grade k $\rightarrow$ ts}
\a \ird{perek} + \ird{-e} $\rightarrow$ \ird{perce}
\lingcontext{first vowel retained; second e deletes; k $\rightarrow$ ts}
\xe

The /ts/ in \ird{lubcí} and \ird{Jancí} remains [ts] before /iː/.
Negative irrealis \ird{-ir} selects
only the restricted velar-front grade; conditional \ird{-ic} selects the
plain grade. Neither is an exception to an automatic front-vowel mutation
rule, since no such rule governs morphological grade selection.

\subsection{Velar fronting and affrication}\label{sec:velar-fronting}

Participating /k/-final stems select /ts/ in the front grade and /tʃ/ in the
yod grade. These are affricates, written \ird{c} and \ird{č}, rather than
the secondarily palatalised velar [kʲ] of lexical \ird{kě} or the palatal
stop [c] of \ird{ťe}. The distinction between secondary
velar palatalisation and affrication is also necessary in descriptions of
Polish \cite{rubach2019}, although the Iridian classes have their own
membership and outputs.

	\footnotesize
	\caption{Class-selected velar alternants.}\label{tab:velar-fronting}
	\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{L L L}
		\toprule
		{\sc grade} & {\sc output} & {\sc derivation} \\
		\midrule
		Palatal/front & /k/ $\rightarrow$ /ts/ & \ird{Marek} + \ird{-í} $\rightarrow$ \ird{Marcí} \\
		& & \ird{ruk} + \ird{-ě} $\rightarrow$ \ird{ruce} \\
		Restricted velar-front & /k/ $\rightarrow$ /ts/ & \ird{zděk-} + \ird{-ir} $\rightarrow$ \ird{zděcir} \\

	\end{tabularx}

At a single boundary the input /k/ selects its velar alternant once.
The resulting /ts/ does not re-enter the coronal PAL mapping at that same
boundary. Thus \ird{ruk} plus front-grade \ird{-ě} gives /rʊtsɛ/,
spelled \ird{ruce}, while input /ts/ with PAL before /ɛ/ gives /tʃɛ/,
spelled \ird{če}. The spellings \ird{ce} and \ird{če} record the
different consonants selected from /k/ and /ts/. An additional outer palatal-grade
suffix constitutes a new boundary and may select a further alternation.

The yod-grade \ird{piašč-} of \ird{piašt-} is a listed cluster alternant;
it does not establish a productive /t/ $\rightarrow$ /tʃ/ mapping.
Likewise, the short raised vowel in \ird{zdičev} belongs to the lexical
modal grade, not to the affrication rule. The contrast with \ird{zděcir}
therefore involves independently selected consonantal and vocalic grades.

\subsubsection{Treatment of h}

The fricative /x/ does not undergo productive categorical mutation to a
sibilant. It has [ç] before /ɪ, iː/, in the specified \orth{hě} sequences,
and before a palatal consonant in the onset, as in \ird{hťe} [çcɛ]. This phonetic
fronting preserves /x/ rather than selecting a /ʃ/ stem.
Any exceptional lexical alternant must be specified independently.

\subsection{Sibilant replacement}\label{sec:sibilant-replacement}

The antipassive \ird{-aš-} selects a sibilant replacement stem whose final
consonant may be \ird{č}, \ird{š}, or \ird{ž}. Since the trigger is the
antipassive construction, these outputs are independent of the front-vocalic
softening classes.

The mutation targets the thematic consonant only and follows the classes in
Table~\ref{tab:sibilant-mutation}:

	\footnotesize

	\begin{tabularx}{0.9\textwidth}{l L L L}
		\toprule
		{\sc class} & {\sc thematic consonants} & {\sc sibilant output} & {\sc example} \\

		I    & -t, -k, -c, -č & $\rightarrow$ -č   & \ird{piašt-} $\rightarrow$ \ird{piašč-} \\

		III  & -d, -h, -j, -z, -ž & $\rightarrow$ -ž & \ird{vad-} $\rightarrow$ \ird{váž-} \\

In Classes I and III the thematic consonant is replaced; in Classes II-A and
III-A the sibilant is added after the preserved consonant. Surface [g]-bearing
loans, where they participate in this pattern at all, follow the voiced
velar/fricative series and are assigned analogically to Class~III. Once replacement
has applied, later voicing cleanup and cluster repair may still affect the output
if the following suffix requires it.

Sibilant replacement and softening thus identify different stem relations:
the former belongs to the antipassive, whereas the latter is selected by the
ordinary front-coded suffix classes.

\subsection{Cluster repair, epenthesis, and voicing cleanup}\label{sec:cluster-repair}

Derived consonant clusters are retained wherever they can be syllabified around
vocalic nuclei. Weak-\ird{e} is retained when deletion would prevent this
syllabification without unlicensed consonant loss; clusters created by subsequent
morphological operations undergo assimilation or class-licensed simplification, with vowel epenthesis supplying
a nucleus when consonantal repair is insufficient. Liquids remain non-syllabic
throughout these operations (Section~\ref{sec:vocalic-nuclei}).

\begin{table}[ht]
	\footnotesize
	\caption{Cluster retention and repair at morpheme boundaries.}\label{tab:cluster-repair}
	\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{l L L}
		\toprule
		{\sc stage} & {\sc strategy} & {\sc effect} \\
		\midrule
		1 & deletion or retention & delete weak \ird{e} only if the remaining vowels support a licit syllabification; otherwise retain it \\
		2 & cluster retention & preserve derived consonant sequences that are already licit \\
		3 & assimilation or simplification & apply ordinary assimilation or a lexically licensed simplification; do not delete consonants to enable vowel loss \\
		4 & vowel epenthesis & insert a short vowel if a later cluster cannot be repaired by consonantal means \\
		\bottomrule
	\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

Four repairs are central in the modern language. First, obstruent clusters obey
regressive voicing assimilation, including across morpheme boundaries:

\pex
\a \ird{obez + -ba} $\rightarrow$ \ird{obezba}, phonetically [obezba]
\a \ird{ot + -ba} $\rightarrow$ [odba]
\a \ird{vrad + -ka} $\rightarrow$ [vratka]
\xe

Second, the obstruents remaining after coda vocalisation undergo final
devoicing, while [w] from /l/ or /v/ remains voiced
(§\,\ref{sec:lenition}). Third, weak-vowel stems retain enough
vocalism for their liquids to occupy syllable margins. In the following
constructed derivations, only the second stem vowel deletes:

\pex
\a \ird{berel} + \ird{-a} $\rightarrow$ \ird{berla}
\a \ird{perek} + \ird{-u} $\rightarrow$ \ird{perku}
\xe

Both outputs contain two syllables, each with a vowel nucleus. The sequences
\ird{rl} and \ird{rk} span coda--onset boundaries, with \ird{r} in the coda
of the first syllable.

The same syllabification precedes coda /l/ vocalisation. An onset lateral
in \ird{berla} or \ird{oblu} remains [l], whereas a hard lateral assigned
to a coda surfaces as [w]. Vocalisation does not license additional fleeting-vowel
deletion, consonant loss, or syllabic consonants
(§\,\ref{sec:l-vocalisation}).

Fourth, a cluster introduced by later consonantal morphology may require
assimilation or class-licensed simplification before vowel epenthesis. Such
repairs do not license an otherwise blocked fleeting-vowel deletion.
Anticipatory palatal coarticulation may affect the consonant immediately
preceding a soft consonant within an onset, especially a coronal. This
phonetic tendency supplies no categorical PAL mapping or additional stem
allomorph. Productive mutation targets the consonant at the stem's right
edge; listed cluster alternants such as \ird{piašt-}/\ird{piašč-}
belong to their construction and do not establish unrestricted leftward
spreading. If no consonantal repair succeeds, a short
\ird{e} /ɛ/ is inserted and written as an ordinary vowel. This vowel supplies
a syllable nucleus and is subject to the regular stress rule. It introduces no
morphological softening trigger, since soft-stem selection is conditioned by
suffix class rather than by an inserted vowel.

Vowel retention preserves a segment already present in the stem, whereas
epenthesis adds a vowel to repair a derived cluster. The latter is analysed
here as a phonological segment with nuclear status, following Hall's distinction
between epenthetic vowels and non-nuclear phonetic vowel intrusion
\cite{hall2006}. Neither process licenses a syllabic
liquid, and neither entails a new vowel phoneme beyond the inventory in
Table~\ref{tab:vowels}.

\subsection{Quantity grades}\label{sec:comp-lengthening}

The nominal pairs \ird{stráz}/\ird{straz-} and \ird{tráv}/\ird{trav-}
illustrate a quantity relation independent of vowel height. In the alternating
Class~III subtype, specified zero-ended case-number cells select a long grade,
while overt case-number endings select a short grade: \ird{stráz},
\ird{strazy}, \ird{strazám}; \ird{trava}, \ird{tráv}, \ird{travám}.
Outer anchoring attaches to the selected case-number form and does not recompute
its root quantity. Other roots, such as \ird{rán-}, retain a lexical long
vowel throughout their case paradigms.

Some antipassive replacement stems likewise select a long vowel, as in
\ird{vad-}/\ird{váž-}, whereas additive stems such as \ird{dal-}/\ird{dalš-}
retain their quantity. The long grade is lexically specified in the replacement
class; replacing one consonant by another does not itself identify a lost mora.

\pex
\a \ird{vad-} + \ird{-aš-} $\rightarrow$ \ird{váž-} \lingcontext{lexical long replacement grade}
\a \ird{dal-} + \ird{-aš-} $\rightarrow$ \ird{dalš-} \lingcontext{additive grade; quantity preserved}
\xe

Height raising preserves length, whereas a quantity grade changes it. Neither
relation is a general phonological response to a dense consonant cluster.

\subsection{Orthography and the soft consonant letters}\label{sec:orthography}

Standard Iridian writes productive soft-coronal outputs with explicit
\ird{ť\,ď\,ň\,ľ}. Restricted lexical spellings are listed separately in
§\,\ref{sec:e-hook}. Conventional spellings before pronounced \ird{i, í},
such as \ird{pištlí}, likewise retain their dictionary-specified consonantal
readings. Such spellings do not make every high-front-vowel suffix a PAL
trigger. The principal conventions are:

\begin{itemize}
	\item Productive soft-coronal outputs use \ird{ť}, \ird{ď}, \ird{ľ},
	and \ird{ň}; lexical soft letters are retained in the stem.
	\item \ird{č}, \ird{š}, \ird{ž} spell postalveolar phonemes, whether
	lexical or derived through PAL, Yod, or antipassive replacement.
	\item Bare \ird{c} spells /ts/, including before high front vowels;
	\ird{č} spells /tʃ/. Neither letter denotes the palatal stop [c].
	\item The phoneme /x/ is written \ird{h} in initial and medial position, but
	word-finally the spelling \ird{ch} is retained. Orthographic \ird{ch} is
	therefore a positional spelling convention, not a separate consonant cluster.
\end{itemize}

These spellings distinguish the soft coronals from the postalveolar consonants.

\subsection{The letter ě and orthographic normalisation}\label{sec:e-hook}

Orthographic normalisation represents the consonantal output selected by the
lexicon and morphology. It does not apply a second mutation. Before /ɛ/,
explicit soft coronals and both sibilant series take plain \ird{e}.
Labial secondary palatalisation is marked by \ird{ě}; lexical \ird{kě} represents /kʲɛ/,
realised [kʲɛ], and \ird{hě} represents the contextual sequence [çɛ].

make a written vowel a mutation trigger. Further suffixation follows the

Redundant \ird{*ťě, *ďě, *ňě, *ľě} and \ird{*čě, *šě, *žě} are excluded.

softened sibilants are written directly as \ird{še, že, če}.
These syllables illustrate spelling correspondences and are not additional
lexical entries.

\subsection{The letter ř}\label{sec:r-hook}

The letter \ird{ř} is a lexical spelling of /ʒ/ in learned and
etymological vocabulary. It introduces no contrast with \ird{ž}, has the
same devoiced realisation [ʃ] word-finally, and does not represent either
a palatalised rhotic or Czech /r̝/. It is preserved where specified by a
lexical entry, as in \ird{knář-}, but no productive native inflection
changes /r/ to \ird{ř}. A phonetic [rʲ] retains rhotic articulation and
does not receive this spelling.

\subsection{Lexical class membership}

A lexeme's stored stem relations and its declension class jointly determine the
form selected by each construction. Expanded endings such as \ird{-ení}
and animate-like \ird{-ovi} do not collapse this distinction: their
consonantal grades and the stem's vocalic grade remain separate
specifications. Listed irregular forms supplement the productive mappings;
they do not supply a general phonological rule for unfamiliar words.

\subsection{Perfective suffix grades}\label{sec:pf-dissim}

Ordinary free perfective \ird{-ek} corresponds to bound \ird{-ik-} before
the linker \ird{-e} and participant nominaliser \ird{-ou}:
\ird{piašček}, \ird{piaščice}, \ird{piaščikou}. The linker also triggers
\ird{k $\rightarrow$ c}, while \ird{-ou} preserves the velar. These
constructions select the suffixal grade without raising the lexical root.
Thus ordinary \ird{kělek} and \ird{ščenek} retain \ird{-ek} even after a
root containing \ird{e/ě}.

Ergative transitive \ird{-nik} and perfective \ird{-ik} selected by the stative
verbaliser \ird{-n-} are inherited construction-specific forms. Their vowel
remains high when further morphology follows. These specifications do not make
\ird{-ik} the default perfective of every stem with a front mid vowel.

\section{Historical note}\label{sec:historical-note}

The modern consonantal grades combine changes of primary articulation,
secondary palatalisation, and affrication. A possible history distinguishes
their sources rather than deriving all three from one sound change.
The developments below are hypotheses, with relative ordering specified
only where one development supplies the input to another. Modern contrasts
do not alone establish an ancestral hard--soft inventory; Wandl and
Kavitskaya's reconstruction of Common Slavic palatalisation illustrates the
need to reconstruct individual contrasts separately \cite{wandlkavitskaya2023}.

\paragraph{Glide absorption and lexical softness.}
Earlier consonant--glide sequences could have yielded secondary palatality
on labials and changes of primary articulation on coronals, with different
segments reaching their modern outputs at different stages. Absorption of
the glide would precede the establishment of softness independent of an
adjacent /j/. Such a development could supply lexical palatality before
back vowels, as in modern \ird{piašt-}, without requiring the silent
orthographic \orth{i} to represent a synchronically deleted glide.
The restricted lexical /kʲ/ of \ird{kěda} does not establish a former
/k/--/kʲ/ alternation or a historical source for its secondary palatality.
Its contrast with /c/ in \ird{ťeda} is synchronic; the two articulations
imply no particular ordering of earlier changes.

\paragraph{Velar grades and morphological retention.}
The front-grade /k/--/ts/ and yod-grade /k/--/tʃ/ relations could descend
from changes in different front-vocalic and consonant--glide environments.
Neither the order of these changes nor their intermediate articulations
is established by the modern alternants. Once changes to vowels or glides
obscured their conditioning, the resulting stem relations could have been
retained by particular suffix classes and extended analogically within
those classes. This would permit a later or differently inherited
front-vocalic exponent to remain plain-grade, as conditional \ird{-ic}
does, while other exponents retained mutation. The suffix-specific Czech
patterns provide a synchronic comparison for such differentiation
\cite{caviranivandenwyngaerd2026}, without establishing this Iridian chronology.

\paragraph{Convergent sibilant outputs.}
The shared postalveolar outputs of modern sibilant PAL, Yod, and antipassive
replacement are compatible with convergence of earlier articulations, but
the modern correspondences establish neither an earlier third sibilant
series nor the chronology of a merger.

\paragraph{Lateral vocalisation.}
Earlier coda /l/ could have developed a dorsal articulation before losing
its lateral constriction and yielding [w], while a palatal counterpart
retained lateral articulation as [ʎ]. An intermediate velarised lateral is
a possible phonetic stage, not an independently reconstructed Iridian
segment. The modern [w]--[ʎ] distribution is compatible with this pathway,
but does not fix the historical order of vocalisation and the creation of
palatal laterals. Synchronically, stem selection and mutation precede
coda vocalisation (§\,\ref{sec:l-vocalisation}).

\paragraph{Construction-specific replacement.}
Earlier fusion with an antipassive formative, followed by morphological
reanalysis and analogy, could have produced the replacement and additive
classes now associated with \ird{-aš-}. Their modern distribution does
not establish the original form of the formative or a single historical
palatalisation rule. Learned \ird{ř} likewise supplies no evidence for
a former productive rhotic mutation. These histories must remain separate
from the productive mappings until lexical correspondences establish
their connections.

\subsection{Vowel quality and the diphthong}

The high and mid short--long pairs differ in quality as well as quantity, although
their earlier phonetic values are not independently established. One possible
history would derive /ɪ, iː/, /ɛ, eː/, /ɔ, oː/, and /ʊ, uː/ from earlier
*/i, iː/, */e, eː/, */o, oː/, and */u, uː/, respectively, through lowering
of the short members, while */a, aː/ retained its quality. An earlier back
unrounded */ɯ, ɯː/ series could have centralised to /ɨ, ɨː/, with subsequent
lowering of short /ɨ/ yielding its modern [ɨ̞] target. These correspondences
constitute a hypothesis about the sources of the inventory; they do not
establish that the changes occurred together or determine their ordering
relative to the stem alternations below. Lexical correspondences, the
distribution of stable and alternating vowels, and the chronology of loans
would have to distinguish this history from other possible sources of the
same modern qualities.

The origin of /ou̯/ is likewise unresolved. An earlier vowel sequence and
diphthongisation of an earlier long vowel are possible sources, but neither
is established by the modern spelling \ird{ou}. Any derivation from an earlier
long /oː/ would also have to explain the distribution of the surviving
monophthong /oː/. Modern coda vocalisation supplies a separate, synchronically
productive source of vowel--[w] sequences and does not by itself account for
the phonemic diphthong.

\subsection{Old Iridian stem vocalism}\label{sec:old-iridian-vocalism}

The following tentative reconstruction explores how ordinary Old Iridian
short and long vowels, initial stress, and suffixal inflection might have
yielded the vocalic stem classes. Starred forms are hypothetical stages,
not independently attested forms; the proposed chronology remains to be
tested against the full lexical distribution. The reconstruction requires no front
and back yers and no phonologically empty vowels in modern representations.

\paragraph{Post-tonic syncope.}
Short unstressed \ird{e} in the final stem syllable was lost before eligible
vowel-initial endings where consonant preservation allowed a licit
syllabification: \ird{*Janek-u} $>$ \ird{Janku} and \ird{*perek-a} $>$
\ird{perka}. The corresponding unsuffixed forms retained the vowel in their
closed final syllable. Long unstressed vowels resisted this syncope; their
later shortening, together with later loans and morphological renewal, supplied
stable vowels in environments now shared with alternating stems. The modern unstressed long vowels have later lexical or morphological
sources, while the earlier quantity contrast survives indirectly in the
distinction between stable and alternating stems.

\paragraph{Metaphony.}
After syncope, a high vowel in the next vocalic position could raise a stressed
mid vowel; consonants were transparent, while an intervening full vowel blocked
the relation. Thus \ird{*lobek-u} $>$ \ird{*lobk-u} $>$ \ird{lubku}, and
\ird{*lobek-í} $>$ \ird{*lobk-í} $>$ \ird{*lubk-í}, followed by velar
mutation to \ird{lubcí}. Raising preserved quantity. High-suffix-triggered
raising of stressed vowels has a typological parallel in the Veneto pattern
analysed by Walker \cite{walker2001}.

\paragraph{Stem levelling and ending replacement.}
High-vowel obliques supplied the raised bound stem \ird{lubk-}, which was
extended to other bound forms, including those with non-high endings and direct
anchoring. Unraised reduced stems arose with older non-high endings:
\ird{*perek-a} $>$ \ird{perka}, \ird{*obel-a} $>$ \ird{obla}, and
\ird{*Merek-e} $>$ \ird{Merce}. Later extension of transitive \ird{-u} and,
in the relevant indirect subclass, \ird{-í}, gave \ird{perku}, \ird{oblu},
and \ird{Mercí} after metaphony had ceased. The modern suffix vowel therefore
does not determine membership in the raised class. The unraised vowel of \ird{Mercí} reflects the later date at which the
high-vowel ending entered this paradigm.

\paragraph{Verbal constructions.}
For the irrealis, a sequence of ordinary \ird{*-i} and a following formative
\ird{*=ew} supplies a possible source: raising preceded glide formation and
jotation, as in \ird{*zděk-i=ew} $>$ \ird{*zdik-i=ew} $>$
\ird{*zdik-jew} $>$ \ird{zdičev}. The glide is absorbed in \ird{k+j > č},
and the labial glide develops into modern \ird{v}. Later negative irrealis
\ird{-ir} belongs to a different construction and does not reactivate the
completed raising process.

The perfective--linker construction was integrated after nominal syncope had
ceased. Local suffixal assimilation gave \ird{*-ek-i > -ik-i}; the older
word-level linker was subsequently replaced by \ird{e}, reconstructed as a
phrase-level connective, while the bound perfective grade survived. This
suffixal history differs from stressed-root metaphony. The surviving suffixal correspondence is selected by the modern linker
construction. The participant nominaliser
\ird{-ou} selects the same bound perfective grade.

\paragraph{Quantity.}
A separate development phonologised greater duration before final sonorants and
voiced fricatives in a subset of stems: reconstructed
\ird{*straz} $>$ \ird{stráz}, beside short-root
\ird{*straz-y}, and \ird{*trav} $>$ \ird{tráv}, beside \ird{trava}.
A phonetic-duration source distinct from lost-vowel compensation is developed
for Western Slavic by Scheer \cite{scheer2017}, providing a comparative model
for the reconstructed Iridian development.
Modern quantity classes preserve the resulting stem relations, while independent
lexical long vowels can remain long before suffixes.
Modern final devoicing and coda vocalisation obscure the earlier consonantal
conditioning: \ird{stráz} ends in [s], while \ird{tráv} ends in [w].

\section{Operational summary}

A construction first selects the full or bound stem and any independently
specified height or quantity grade. Fleeting-vowel reduction preserves the
stressed vowel and enough vocalism to syllabify all liquids at syllable
margins. The consonantal grade is then interpreted once at its boundary:
plain morphology retains the input consonant; PAL supplies the coronal and
labial mappings; participating velars select /ts/ in the front grade or /tʃ/
in the yod grade. Restricted velar-front \ird{-ir} supplies only its velar
alternant. Antipassive \ird{-aš-} follows its separate replacement classes.

Syllabification and licensed repair establish the syllables on which initial
lexical stress is assigned (§\,\ref{sec:stress}); vowel quantity remains
independent. Pronounced /ɪ\,iː/ does not neutralise the two sibilant
series. Licensed silent \orth{i} after a labial supplies no vowel nucleus.
Orthographic normalisation represents the consonants with explicit
\ird{ť\,ď\,ň\,ľ} and \ird{š\,ž\,č}, distinguishes \ird{ce/če}, and preserves lexical
\ird{kě} for /kʲɛ/, distinct from /cɛ/ \ird{ťe}, and learned \ird{ř}.
Neither front-vowel spelling nor an
orthographic normalisation rule supplies an additional morphological trigger.
