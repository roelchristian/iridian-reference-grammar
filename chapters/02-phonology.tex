\chapter{Phonology, Prosody, and Orthography}\label{ch:phon}

\section{Introduction}

This chapter describes the phonology of standard Iridian as spoken in Roubže and the
surrounding region. The language shows a distinctly West Slavic profile: an
opposition between hard and soft consonant stems, productive coronal softening,
velar fronting, coda vocalisation of /l/ and /v/, prevocalic approximant realisation
of /v/, final devoicing, cluster voicing assimilation, and a system of
full and bound stems distinguished by vowel presence, height, or quantity.

The description distinguishes lexical contrasts, morphologically selected stem
grades, and contextual pronunciation. The alveolar /s\,z\,ts/ and
postalveolar /ʃ\,ʒ\,tʃ/ series contrast lexically, including before high
front vowels. Morphological PAL membership groups the correspondences
/s\,z\,ts/ $\rightarrow$ /ʃ\,ʒ\,tʃ/, /t\,d\,n\,l/ $\rightarrow$
[c\,ɟ\,ɲ\,ʎ], and secondary palatalisation of labials. These outputs
differ in articulation while sharing their morphological distribution.
Phonemic contrast and participation in an alternation are compatible
properties.

Constructions select their consonantal grades independently of the written vowel
of an ending. The velar alternants /ts/ and /tʃ/, conventionally written
\ird{c} and \ird{č}, belong to specified grades; neither should be confused
with either lexical /kʲ/, realised as a secondarily palatalised velar in
\ird{kěda}, or the palatal stop [c] in \ird{těda}. Antipassive
formation, conventionally labelled \ird{-aš-}, selects a separate system
of \term{sibilant replacement and augmentation}
(§\,\ref{sec:sibilant-replacement}). Orthography represents the resulting
consonants rather than causing their alternations.

The distinction between a change of primary articulation and secondary
palatalisation follows Bateman’s terminology \cite{bateman2007}: the standard
realisations [c\,ɟ\,ɲ\,ʎ] differ in primary constriction from their plain
coronal counterparts, whereas [pʲ\,bʲ\,mʲ\,ʋʲ] retain a labial constriction
and lexical [kʲ] retains a velar closure.
The synchronic description identifies the stem and suffix classes selecting
these alternants; possible historical sources of the consonantal and vocalic
grades are considered separately in §\,\ref{sec:historical-note}.

\section{Vowels}

\subsection{Vowel inventory}

Standard Iridian, the principal spoken variety described here, distinguishes
ten monophthongal vowel phonemes: six short
vowels /a\,ɛ\,ɪ\,ɨ\,ɔ\,ʊ/ and four long vowels /aː\,iː\,ɨː\,uː/.
Traditional \orth{é} and \orth{í} both represent /iː/, while \orth{ó}
and \orth{ú} both represent /uː/. These mergers preserve length and apply
throughout the native vocabulary, in stressed and unstressed syllables alike;
the high-vowel pronunciation persists in careful speech and reading aloud.
Conservative regional varieties retain /eː, oː/, whereas the standard
short mid vowels /ɛ, ɔ/ have no long mid counterparts.

Quantity is contrastive independently of lexical stress. The short high
vowels are generally more open or centralised than the corresponding long
vowels, whereas /a\,aː/ differ chiefly in quantity. Orthographic
\orth{i, í} represent /ɪ,\,iː/, while \orth{y, ý} represent the
non-palatalising central series /ɨ,\,ɨː/. Slashes identify phonemic
categories throughout this chapter; square brackets give their phonetic
realisations, with optional contextual detail omitted in broad pronunciations.

Orthographic \ird{ě} represents the same vowel /ɛ/ as plain \ird{e},
while also marking palatal quality on a preceding consonant
(§\,\ref{sec:orthographic-e-hook}).

Short \ird{y} /ɨ/ ordinarily has the lowered central realisation [ɨ̞],
with [ɨ] as a raised variant and further lowering towards [ɘ] in unstressed
and prosodically weak positions. The symbol [ɨ̞] specifies a target below
close [ɨ] rather than a fixed degree of lowering; it remains distinct from
front \ird{i} [ɪ] in careful standard speech. Long \ird{ý} /ɨː/ has the
higher target [ɨː], beside secondary [ɘː] in some dialects. Regional fronting
of the central series may produce short [ɨ̟] or [ɪ̠] and long [ɨ̟ː].
Such fronting need not eliminate the lexical opposition to \ird{i, í}.
The casual urban merger of \ird{ý} with \ird{í} instead neutralises the
long-vowel contrast at [iː], even where short \ird{y} remains distinct
from \ird{i}; it is an additional register-conditioned neutralisation,
separate from the standard mergers of \orth{é} with \orth{í} and
\orth{ó} with \orth{ú}. Only a variety in which the front--central
long-vowel merger is stable across registers has a nine-monophthong
inventory, provided that the short front--central contrast remains intact.

Adjacent palatal and palatalised consonants favour vowel fronting, most
readily represented for /a/ by [a̟]. The ordinary low-vowel target is [a],
including in stressed syllables, while central [ɐ] belongs to the reduction
patterns described below. These contextual modifications affect phonetic
quality without adding vowel phonemes or determining the consonantal grades
selected by morphology
(§§\,\ref{sec:soft-coronals} and~\ref{sec:velar-fronting}).

The conservative twelve-vowel arrangement has a close comparison in Northern
Welsh, whose stressed monophthongs comprise six pairs
/ɐ,\,ɐː/, /ɛ,\,eː/, /ɪ,\,iː/, /ɨ,\,ɨː/, /ɔ,\,oː/,
and /ʊ,\,uː/, together with /ə/. Its long vowels are restricted to
stressed final syllables, so the resemblance concerns inventory structure
rather than the distribution of quantity \cite{bell2023}.
Czech supplies a comparison for quantity independent of initial stress
and for phonemic /ou̯/, although its mid-vowel pairs show less qualitative
differentiation than the conservative Iridian pairs
\cite{simackova2012}.

\subsection{Long-vowel mergers and regional correspondences}\label{sec:long-vowel-mergers}

The regional systems differ in whether traditional \orth{é, ó} retain
mid-vowel contrasts (Table~\ref{tab:long-vowel-dialects}). Conservative
regional speech preserves both contrasts, while an intermediate regional
system merges only \orth{ó} with \orth{ú}. The back-vowel merger thus
has the wider distribution; neither regional system is defined by slow or
careful delivery. Standard speech has both mergers, without a residual
quality or quantity distinction between the historical classes in comparable
phonetic environments. The conservative inventory accordingly has twelve
monophthongs, and the intermediate regional inventory eleven, alongside
the ten of standard Iridian.

The historical analysis takes the conservative six short--long pairs as its
starting point and places the back-vowel merger before the front-vowel
merger. Long /oː/ and /eː/ rise into inherited /uː/ and /iː/, which
retain their high-vowel targets. Intermediate [o̝ː, e̝ː] schematise raising
while the contrasts remain; they are not additional phonemes of the modern
standard. Neither change involves shortening or a displacement of the
inherited high vowels. Traditional spelling preserves the historical vowel
classes and their morphological correspondences even where their
pronunciations have converged (§\,\ref{sec:vowel-raising}).

The merged vowels enter the ordinary contextual pronunciation rules.
Labials acquire secondary palatalisation before /iː/, including written
\orth{é}, and /x/ has [ç] in the same environment. Thus schematic
\orth{pé} and \orth{hé} have [pʲiː] and [çiː], whereas \orth{té}
has [tiː]: the high front vowel selects no morphological PAL grade.
In established forms, \ird{pérku} \trsl{body (transitive)} consequently has
standard [ˈpʲiːr.kʊ] beside conservative [ˈpeːr.kʊ], while
\ird{lenóčkime} \trsl{is running out} has standard
[ˈlɛ.nuːtʃ.kɪ.mɛ] beside conservative [ˈlɛ.noːtʃ.kɪ.mɛ].
These pronunciations retain initial stress and the same number of syllables.
The long monophthong /uː/ remains distinct from the diphthong /ou̯/.

\subsection{Vowel quantity}\label{sec:vowel-quantity}

Vowel quantity is independent of lexical stress, so /a : aː/, /ɪ : iː/, /ɨ : ɨː/, and /ʊ : uː/
remain phonologically contrastive in unstressed as well as stressed
syllables. Under comparable conditions, unstressed /aː/ is ordinarily longer
than unstressed /a/, although a stressed short vowel need not be shorter
than an unstressed long vowel. Speech rate and phonetic context also affect
duration: substantial phonetic shortening in rapid speech does not by
itself change a vowel's lexical quantity category. The contrast therefore
requires neither a fixed duration nor an invariant long-to-short ratio.

Stress can be expressed through pitch alignment and articulatory strength,
without assigning a fixed increase in vowel duration. Boundary-related
lengthening likewise leaves lexical quantity unchanged
(§\,\ref{sec:boundary-phenomena}). The acute accent in vowel spellings
marks length, not lexical stress; primary stress and subsidiary footing
are described in §§\,\ref{sec:stress} and~\ref{sec:secondary-stress}.

The pairs \ird{bil} [bʲɪw] `hook' and \ird{byl} [bɨ̞w] `child',

in consonantal palatalisation as well as vowel quality at the phonetic level.
Since labial palatalisation before pronounced high front vowels is predictable and
non-phonemic (§\,\ref{sec:orthographic-i}), the ten-vowel analysis
represents these pairs as /bɪl/--/bɨl/ and /biːl/--/bɨːl/:
they are phonemically minimal despite being phonetically near-minimal.
Their surface forms alone do not, however, determine whether the lexical
distinction belongs to the vowels or to consonantal softness.

Under the ten-vowel analysis, both series are also licensed after the
unchanged postalveolars /ʃ,\,ʒ,\,tʃ/ (§\,\ref{sec:postalveolars}).
Schematic /ʃɪr/--/ʃɨr/ and /ʃiːr/--/ʃɨːr/, pronounced
[ʃɪr]--[ʃɨ̞r] and [ʃiːr]--[ʃɨːr], isolate the vowel oppositions
without an accompanying consonantal difference; these strings have no
assigned lexical meanings. A lexical pair in this environment would provide
additional evidence for the vowel analysis, although no such pair is
specified here.

\subsection{Vowel reduction}

Ordinary unstressed lexical vowels in standard speech generally retain
their characteristic qualities. Short /ɛ,\,ɪ,\,ɔ,\,ʊ/ ordinarily remain
[ɛ,\,ɪ,\,ɔ,\,ʊ], while /a/ permits mild centralisation towards [ɐ]
and /ɨ/ ranges from [ɨ̞] towards [ɘ]. Neither lowering of central \ird{y}
nor the absence of stress imposes a general merger of /ɛ/, /ɪ/, and /ɨ/.
Broad careful-speech pronunciations use [a] in ordinary unstressed syllables
and [ɨ̞] for short \ird{y}; a narrower transcription can record the
contextual variants.

Grammatical clitics and other prosodically weak material admit greater
centralisation, especially in rapid connected speech. Short /a/ may then
surface as [ɐ], /ɨ/ as [ɘ], and /ɛ/ and /ɪ/ may move towards more central
targets. These are gradient possibilities whose extent depends on prosodic
weakness and register; an unstressed suffix does not automatically undergo
the stronger reduction. Final /ɛ/ retains [ɛ] before a pause. Qualitative
reduction is compatible with the lexical quantity distinctions described
in §\,\ref{sec:vowel-quantity}.

The fleeting-\ird{e} alternation in \ird{perek}/\ird{pérku} belongs to
lexically and morphologically restricted stem selection
(§\,\ref{sec:vowel-zero}). Although stable /ɛ/ survives in comparable
unstressed positions, fleeting \ird{e} deletes only in specified stem
classes; its loss is a morphophonological alternation rather than the
automatic endpoint of ordinary phonetic reduction.

\subsection{Diphthongs}

Standard Iridian has one phonemic diphthong, \ird{ou}\,/ou̯/, whose
careful-speech realisation [ou̯] begins with a closer vowel target [o]
than the [ɔ] of short /ɔ/. Coda /l/ and /v/ vocalisation also produces
surface vowel--glide sequences, as in \ird{sel} [sɛw] and \ird{tráv} [traːw]
(§§\,\ref{sec:l-vocalisation} and~\ref{sec:v-realisation}). These retain
the analysis of a vowel nucleus followed by a consonantal coda, even where
the vocalised consonant gives the sequence a diphthongal phonetic shape.

The following schematic strings illustrate the distinction without assigning
lexical meanings:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
{\sc underlying structure} & {\sc phonetic output} \\
\ipagloss{/tou̯/} & \ipagloss{[tou̯]} \\
\ipagloss{/tɔl/, /tɔv/} & \ipagloss{[tɔw]} \\
\ipagloss{/tuːl/} & \ipagloss{[tuːw]}
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Coda vocalisation neutralises /l/ and /v/ in the second row, whereas the
closer initial vowel of /ou̯/ and the long high vowel of /uːl/ preserve the other
careful-standard distinctions. The symbols [u̯] and [w] need not denote
different glide articulations for these contrasts to hold. Regional or casual
raising of [ɔ] could bring [ɔw] towards [ou̯], although phonetic proximity need not entail lexical merger or a change in the
syllabic analysis of the underlying vowel--consonant sequence.

Other falling vowel sequences, such as [eɪ̯] in interjections and affective
expressions, belong to formulaic exclamations rather than to the ordinary
inflectional system. Such expressions introduce no additional phonemic
diphthong contrast. Conditional \ird{-ej} /ɛj/ contains a vowel followed
by consonantal /j/, while sequences ending in [w] from /l/ or /v/ occur in
ordinary lexical and inflected forms wherever either segment occupies a coda.

\section{Consonants}

\subsection{Consonant inventory}\label{sec:consonant-inventory}

The core consonant inventory of standard Iridian is set out in
Table~\ref{tab:consonants}; restricted lexical palatality is specified
separately below. Surface [g] and [f] are not primary phonemes. The former occurs in careful
loan pronunciation and through voicing assimilation of /k/, while [f] arises
by voiceless assimilation of onset /v/
(§\,\ref{sec:lenition}). Native phonology accordingly lacks phonemic /g/ and
/f/. Predictable glottal [ʔ] likewise belongs to contextual pronunciation
rather than to the consonant inventory (§\,\ref{sec:glottal-onset}).

The inventory classifies /l/ as a liquid and places /v/ with the fricatives
on the basis of its onset behaviour in voicing assimilation. Both segments
have coda [w], while prevocalic /v/ has [ʋ]
(§§\,\ref{sec:l-vocalisation} and~\ref{sec:v-realisation}). Their outputs
coincide in codas, but onset [l] and [ʋ] preserve the distinction; neither
[w] nor [ʋ] adds a contrastive member to Table~\ref{tab:consonants}.

The letters \orth{č}, \orth{š}, and \orth{ž} represent /tʃ/, /ʃ/, and
/ʒ/. Bare \orth{c} denotes /ts/, including before pronounced /ɪ\,iː/;
\orth{č} denotes /tʃ/. Neither letter represents the palatal stop [c],
whose spelling is \orth{tě} with following /ɛ/ and \orth{ť} elsewhere.
The postalveolars occur lexically and as
outputs of productive PAL applied to /s, z, ts/.

The palatal nasal \ird{ň} /ɲ/ belongs to the core inventory, with lexical
occurrences before back vowels and word-finally as well as derived occurrences
under PAL. The other soft coronals cannot all be treated as
predictable variants of plain consonants either: \ird{těda} [ˈcɛ.da]
and \ird{htě} [çcɛ] contain lexical /c/, while \ird{zděc} [zɟɛts]
`room' contains /ɟ/ without a suffixal PAL boundary.
Lexical \ird{kěda} [ˈkʲɛ.da] instead contains a secondarily palatalised
velar, distinct from /c/ in \ird{těda}. The analysis therefore admits
restricted lexical /c, ɟ, kʲ/ alongside the core inventory; lexical
fricative softness /xʲ/ is specified below.
Lexically specified \ird{ľ} retains its soft quality,
although its productive occurrence in PAL paradigms alone does not establish
an independent lexical /ʎ/ contrast.

Labial softness also has a lexical source. The initial [pʲ] in
\ird{pjašt-} precedes /a/, with \orth{pj} spelling a single
palatalised labial rather than /pj/. This stem accordingly stores palatality on
its initial /p/, independently of the grade selected at its right edge.
Lexical palatality is represented here as a consonantal specification,
without extending a contrastive hard--soft pairing to every consonant.
The lexical spellings \ird{kaměn} `stone' and \ird{vravět} `letter'
likewise specify soft /m/ and /v/ before /ɛ/, realised [mʲ] and [ʋʲ].
These forms establish stored palatality without supplying a full set of
hard--soft minimal pairs; the independent lexical distribution of soft /b/
remains unspecified. Membership in the productive PAL mapping alone does
not establish such a lexical opposition.
Predictable secondary palatalisation before /ɪ, iː/ remains distinct from
this stored property.
Lithuanian provides a comparison for contrastive consonantal palatality
before back vowels \cite{baksiene2024}, without implying an equally
extensive set of hard--soft pairs in Iridian.

The letter \ird{h} represents the velar fricative /x/, not a glottal [h].
Its contextual realisation is [ç] before /ɪ, iː/ and by local assimilation
before a palatal consonant in the onset, as in \phon{htě}{çcɛ}{time}.
Before /ɛ/, lexical softness is represented independently: \orth{hě}
encodes /xʲɛ/, realised [çɛ], in \phon{hěma}{ˈçɛ.ma}{flag} and
\phon{hěř}{çɛʃ}{brow}, whereas plain \orth{he} encodes /xɛ/, with [xɛ],
as in relational \ird{ščehezna} (Table~\ref{tab:relstatives}). The
analysis treats this restricted softness as a lexical consonantal
specification, without asserting a lexical minimal pair or a full independent
/x/--/xʲ/ series. Contextual fronting before high front vowels and palatal
consonants does not require that specification; neither source of [ç]
licenses a productive /x/ to sibilant alternation.

\subsection{Coda lateral vocalisation}\label{sec:l-vocalisation}

Hard /l/ is realised as [l] in syllable onsets and as the labial--velar glide
[w] in codas, both word-medially and word-finally. The rule applies after
stem selection, consonant mutation, suffixation, and syllabification, within
the phonological word. A vowel-initial suffix can therefore place an underlying
/l/ in an onset, where it retains lateral articulation. Compare the existing
nominal forms \ird{byl} [bɨ̞w] and \ird{byla} [ˈbɨ̞.la]: the stem retains
/l/ throughout, although the bare form has a glide at the surface.

Soft \ird{ľ}, realised as [ʎ], retains lateral articulation in all
syllable positions. In the schematic softening pair \ird{ral}/\ird{ralě}
of Table~\ref{tab:soft-coronals}, the outputs are [raw] and [ˈra.ʎɛ].
Morphology selects the soft lateral before vocalisation can apply, so the
alternation continues to relate hard /l/ to its soft counterpart. Neither
liquid is syllabic, and vocalisation supplies no new nucleus
(§\,\ref{sec:vocalic-nuclei}).

The indirect evidential supplies a grammatical instance of this contrast
(§\,\ref{sec:vm-evid}). Its default \ird{-al} after consonants is
realised [aw] in an unlinked form; after vowels, it has the allomorph
\ird{-l}, whose final lateral undergoes the same positional realisation.
Continuous \ird{pjašta-l}, written \phon{pjaštal}{ˈpʲaʃ.taw}{indirect-evidential continuous},
illustrates this vowel-conditioned allomorph. The outer linker's front
grade selects [ʎ] in \phon{pjaštalě}{ˈpʲaʃ.ta.ʎɛ}{linked indirect-evidential continuous}, so that
local \ird{-al} [aw] corresponds to \ird{-alě} [aʎɛ]. Here
[aw] is a vowel followed by a vocalised coda, rather than a new phonemic
diphthong. Neither evidential allomorph changes vowel quantity or assigns
stress to the suffix.

The following schematic word-internal sequences illustrate the positional
rule; the dot marks a syllable boundary and initial glottal insertion is
outside the scope of these fragments:

\begin{center}
\ipagloss{/al.ta/} $\rightarrow$ \ipagloss{[aw.ta]}\qquad
\ipagloss{/a.la/} $\rightarrow$ \ipagloss{[a.la]}
\end{center}

Orthographic \ird{l} is retained for both [l] and [w], while the soft
lateral is written \ird{lě} before /ɛ/ and \ird{ľ} elsewhere. The synchronic rule maps /l/ directly
to [w], with no independent /w/ or /ɫ/ in the inventory. Coda-conditioned /l/ to [w]
vocalisation has a descriptive parallel in Brazilian Portuguese, where
Battisti and Moras document variation between lateral and vocalised outputs
\cite{battistimoras2016}; in standard Iridian the coda rule is
categorical.

\subsection{Positional realisation of /v/}\label{sec:v-realisation}

/v/ is realised as the labiodental approximant [ʋ] in prevocalic onsets and
as the labial--velar glide [w] in codas, including word-final position.
Thus the nominal forms \ird{trava} and \ird{tráv} have [ˈtra.ʋa] and
[traːw], respectively; their vowel quantities belong to the lexical
case-number grades, independently of the consonant's realisation. Both
outputs remain non-syllabic, and orthographic \ird{v} is retained.

Within the phonological word, postvocalic /v/ before a consonant is assigned
to the coda: a sequence /VvC/ is syllabified /Vv.C/, even where /vC/ is
possible as a word-initial onset. Vocalisation applies to this coda before
final devoicing and voicing assimilation, so /v/ yields [w] regardless of
the following consonant's voicing. A following vowel-initial suffix can
instead place /v/ in an onset. The schematic word-internal sequences /av.ta/ and /a.va/
therefore have [aw.ta] and [a.ʋa], abstracting away from word-edge
glottal insertion.

Preconsonantal onset /v/ retains fricative articulation: it surfaces as [v]
before a voiced consonant and as [f] when assimilated to a following
voiceless obstruent. Word-initial /vta/, for example, schematically yields
[fta], whereas the coda /v/ of /av.ta/ remains [w]. The lexical preverb
\ird{vra-} has the licensed obstruent--liquid onset /vr/, realised [vr]
before its vowel: \ird{vranočk-} \trsl{run around an obstacle} begins
[vra], with neither coda vocalisation nor an additional vowel
(§\,\ref{sec:lexical-preverbs}).

Where the front or mixed grade selects labial PAL, secondary articulation is retained
on the approximant, giving [ʋʲ] before the following vowel
(§\,\ref{sec:front-softening}). This is a conditioned realisation of /v/,
whose palatality is supplied by the construction. Any lexical palatality
belongs to the stem specification described in §\,\ref{sec:consonant-inventory};
the positional realisation of /v/ does not decide its lexical distribution.

The analysis retains onset /v/ as a voiced obstruent for cluster licensing
and voicing assimilation. It both undergoes assimilation and determines the
voicing of preceding obstruents when it is the rightmost obstruent in the
cluster, even if it subsequently surfaces as [ʋ]. The schematic sequence
/tva/ consequently yields [dʋa], with voicing assimilation preceding
prevocalic approximant realisation. Coda [w] from /v/ has sonorant behaviour:
it neither triggers voicing nor undergoes devoicing, although it is
transparent to assimilation between other obstruents. The onset and coda
distributions therefore differ in their participation in the voicing system.

The distribution has a positional parallel in Slovene, for which Unuk
describes prevocalic [ʋ] and non-syllabic [u̯] in codas
\cite{unuk2023}. Iridian's onset behaviour remains independently
specified: the voicing triggered by onset /v/ in the analysis here differs
from Unuk's account of Slovene /v/ as a non-trigger of obstruent voicing
\cite{unuk2023}.

\subsection{Lenition, final devoicing, and voicing assimilation}\label{sec:lenition}

\noindent\textit{Velar lenition}\quad

The velar stop /k/ is frequently realised as [k͡x] after a pause and lenites to
[x] or [ɣ] intervocalically and in certain clusters. The voiced fricative
[ɣ] is distinct from the stop [g] supplied by regressive voicing assimilation
of /k/ in an obstruent cluster. Loanwords may preserve [g] in
careful pronunciation, but in ordinary speech they are commonly adapted with
\orth{h} or with the native lenited series.

\noindent\textit{Final devoicing}\quad

Word-final obstruents are categorically devoiced in standard Iridian. Voiced
stops, fricatives, and affricates surface as their voiceless counterparts, for example:
/b\,d\,ɟ/ $\rightarrow$ [p\,t\,c], /z\,ʒ/ $\rightarrow$ [s\,ʃ].
Voiceless obstruents and sonorants are unaffected. Coda /v/ has already
vocalised to sonorant [w] when final devoicing applies, so \ird{tráv}
surfaces as [traːw]; the [f] allophone belongs to preconsonantal onsets
(§\,\ref{sec:v-realisation}). In the phasal proclitic
\ird{vy}, /v/ precedes the particle's own vowel and therefore has its
prevocalic realisation (§\,\ref{sec:class1-particles}).

Contemplative \ird{-áž} contains /aːʒ/: its final fricative
devoices word-finally, while the default exponent retains its long vowel. Thus
\phon{pjaštáž}{ˈpʲaʃ.taːʃ}{projected eating} has final [ʃ], whereas
linked \phon{pjaštáže}{ˈpʲaʃ.taː.ʒɛ}{projected eating, linked} retains
[ʒ]. The linker's front grade leaves the existing postalveolar unchanged;
it does not derive /ʒ/ from /x/. Evidential \ird{-al} likewise places
/ʒ/ before a vowel, giving \phon{pjaštážal}{ˈpʲaʃ.taː.ʒaw}{indirectly evidenced projected eating}
and linked \phon{pjaštážalě}{ˈpʲaʃ.taː.ʒa.ʎɛ}{indirectly evidenced projected eating, linked}.
The acute in \ird{-áž} marks quantity; primary stress remains on the
first syllable of the lexical prosodic word.
After a locally strong stem or a Class~C lexical long stem, the adjacent
aspect exponent has the short allomorph \ird{-až}, as in
\ird{pjáščaž}; the same /ʒ/ alternations apply. This quantity-conditioned
allomorph does not shorten the root or a following evidential.

\noindent\textit{Regressive voicing assimilation}\quad

Within an obstruent cluster---a sequence of adjacent obstruents uninterrupted by a
vowel---voicing is determined regressively: the voicing specification of the
rightmost obstruent spreads leftward to all preceding obstruents in the cluster.
Sonorants (liquids, nasals, and the glide \ird{j}) are transparent to the process:
they neither block the spread nor change their own voicing value.
Onset /v/ participates as an obstruent before its prevocalic output is
realised as [ʋ]. Coda /l/ and /v/ have already vocalised to [w], which
has the voicing behaviour of a sonorant (§§\,\ref{sec:v-realisation}
and~\ref{sec:l-vocalisation}).

underlyingly voiceless and surfaces unchanged in word-final position.

The same rules apply across morpheme boundaries and therefore form part of the
regular morphophonemic surface cleanup described in
Section~\ref{sec:cluster-repair}.

retains a posterior velar closure at the soft palate, accompanied by secondary

\subsection{Postalveolars as independent phonemes}\label{sec:postalveolars}

The postalveolar series \ird{č\,š\,ž} /tʃ\,ʃ\,ʒ/ occurs without front-vocalic
conditioning in \ird{šum} `noise', \ird{žen}
`wife (transitive)', and \ird{čer} `border', while \ird{šira} `silver
(direct)' begins with [ʃɪ] without further mutation. These consonants have
lexical distributions as well as occurrences derived by specified
constructions, although productive PAL preserves their quality. Thus
\ird{š} remains /ʃ/ where plain /t/ selects [c], written \ird{ť},
and plain /s/ selects /ʃ/.
The corresponding soft coronals \ird{ť\,ď\,ň\,ľ} also permit lexical
softness (§\,\ref{sec:consonant-inventory}), so the distinction between the
series concerns their articulation and mutation correspondences rather than
an opposition between lexical and derived sounds.

or a categorical neutralisation rule.

a postalveolar output from the same input. Antipassive \ird{-aš-} has
its own replacement classes, additive formations, and quantity alternations
(§\,\ref{sec:sibilant-replacement}). Likewise, the front and mixed grades select /ts/ and /tʃ/ respectively
from plain /k/, while an input /ts/ gives /tʃ/ in both grades through
their coronal PAL correspondence. These are alternative outputs from
different inputs, not successive steps at one boundary
(§\,\ref{sec:boundary-mapping}).


\section{Phonotactics}

\subsection{Syllable structure}

The basic syllable template is (C)V(C), with CV(C) as the preferred
surface shape. The maximum is CCCCVCCC, though most syllables fall into
one of CV, CVC, CCV, CCVC, or CVCC. Underlyingly vowel-initial lexical
words receive the predictable onset described below; this edge requirement
does not impose an onset on every word-internal syllable.

\subsection{Glottal onset at lexical word edges}\label{sec:glottal-onset}

Underlyingly vowel-initial lexical prosodic words receive a non-contrastive
laryngeal onset, ordinarily realised as [ʔ] in citation forms, careful
speech, and prosodically prominent positions. Thus \ird{obel} /ɔbɛl/
\trsl{window} has careful [ˈʔɔ.bɛw], with ordinary coda vocalisation of
/l/, while transitive \ird{oblu} has [ˈʔɔ.blʊ]. Less prominent positions
permit a creaky onset to the initial vowel, and weak rapid speech permits
loss of audible glottal marking. The schematic variants [ˈʔV], [ˈV̰],
and [ˈV] represent these realisations without introducing a contrast in
vowel quality or quantity. The stress mark precedes the syllable containing
the vowel; [ʔ] supplies neither a nucleus nor a mora. Glottal insertion
therefore changes neither initial stress nor the subsidiary parse
(§\,\ref{sec:stress}), and ordinary spelling leaves it unmarked.

The conditioning domain is the minimal lexical prosodic word, $\omega$,
which includes integrated prefixes and suffixes but excludes external
clitics (§\,\ref{sec:prosodic-domains}). In the analysis adopted here,
allomorph selection and lexical syllabification precede onset insertion;
independently licensed consonantal liaison can supply the initial onset
before insertion applies.

\begin{samepage}
With $V$ denoting the initial vowel and $X$ the remainder of the lexical word, the careful-speech mapping is schematically
\[
  {}_{\omega}(VX)\ \longrightarrow\ {}_{\omega}([\text{ʔ}]VX),
\]
provided that liaison has not already supplied an onset. Its variable
phonetic implementation does not change the domain of the rule.
\end{samepage}

The syllabic proclitic \ird{si} preserves its host's lexical edge:
\ird{si obel} has careful [sɪ ˈʔɔ.bɛw], although the two words can share
one accentual phrase. Negative \ird{z-}, by contrast, supplies the onset
of a vowel-initial verbal host through restricted consonantal liaison,
yielding schematic [ˈzV\ldots] rather than [zˈʔV\ldots]. Selection of
\ird{z-} from underlying /za/ precedes glottal insertion, so the
predictable [ʔ] of an independent pronunciation cannot cause retention of
\ird{za-} (§\,\ref{sec:neg-allomorphy}). Segmental integration of /z/
leaves its morphological externality and the host's lexical stress domain
intact. This liaison is specific to the reduced negative marker; it
establishes no general resyllabification across clitic boundaries.

Vowel-initial suffixes undergo the established lexical syllabification
without attracting [ʔ]: \ird{byla} retains [ˈbɨ̞.la], with the stem-final
/l/ in the suffixal syllable's onset. Internal hiatus and the left edges of
roots within an integrated word likewise attract no automatic insertion,
even when an onsetless syllable bears a subsidiary metrical head.
Combinations of lexical preverbs with vowel-initial roots retain their
requirement for individual lexical specification
(§\,\ref{sec:morphophon}); a lexically specified internal [ʔ] would
protect that boundary without assigning the root another primary stress.
No productive internal-hiatus insertion rule or lexical class requiring
such [ʔ] is specified here. Expressive final glottalisation is distinct
from onset insertion and establishes no word-final /ʔ/ contrast or
compensatory-lengthening rule.

The separation of word-edge onset marking from a general syllable-onset
requirement has a comparative precedent in Alber's treatment of
morphological edge effects in German, where root and prefix edges differ
from suffix edges \cite[p.~10]{alber2001}. German glottal marking also
varies with speech rate, accent, word type, and vowel quality
\cite[pp.~14--15]{pompinozygis2010}. These comparisons motivate distinguishing
structural licensing from phonetic realisation; the restriction to the
Iridian lexical word and the liaison of \ird{z-} are specifications of
the present analysis.

\subsection{Vocalic nuclei and liquid clusters}\label{sec:vocalic-nuclei}

Every syllable in Iridian has a vowel nucleus. The rhotic, the lateral, and
coda [w] from /l/ or /v/ remain non-syllabic in careful and rapid speech
alike, whether they occur next to a lexical vowel or in a cluster created by
weak-\ird{e} deletion (§§\,\ref{sec:l-vocalisation}
and~\ref{sec:v-realisation}). A liquid
therefore contributes no syllable of its own, and its presence cannot license
a consonant sequence that would otherwise violate the cluster restrictions
in Section~\ref{sec:sonority}.

In the fleeting-vowel class, short unstressed \ird{e} deletes where the
remaining consonants can be syllabified around the surviving vowels without
unlicensed consonant loss. Thus \ird{obel} yields \ird{oblu}, with the
liquid in the onset of the second syllable, while \ird{perek} yields
\ird{pérku}, with \ird{r} closing the first syllable and \ird{k} beginning
the second. The first \ird{e} of \ird{perek} is retained; deletion of both
vowels would leave an unlicensed initial cluster. Where deletion would remove
a necessary nucleus, the vowel is retained; an illicit cluster arising later
in the derivation is subject to the repair rules in
Section~\ref{sec:cluster-repair}.

\subsection{Sonority and cluster licensing}\label{sec:sonority}

Iridian cluster phonotactics are described by the following sonority hierarchy,
ranked from lowest to highest:

\begin{center}
voiceless obstruents $<$ voiced obstruents $<$ nasals $<$ liquids (\ird{r, l}) $<$
glides (\ird{j}) $<$ vowels
\end{center}

This hierarchy describes the segment classes at syllabification, before
coda vocalisation. Derived [w] has glide articulation but occupies the
coda already assigned to /l/ or /v/; it introduces no additional onset
type. Postvocalic /v/ before a consonant belongs to the coda, as in
schematic word-internal /av.ra/ [aw.ra]. Onset /v/ is licensed as an obstruent, with [ʋ]
arising before the nuclear vowel after voicing assimilation. A preconsonantal
onset retains fricative [v] or assimilated [f], preserving the existing
onset patterns (§§\,\ref{sec:l-vocalisation} and~\ref{sec:v-realisation}).

The general requirement for syllable-initial clusters is non-decreasing sonority
from the cluster edge to the nuclear vowel. This licences the most common Iridian
onset types: obstruent plus liquid (\ird{tr-, dr-, kl-}), obstruent plus nasal, and
obstruent plus glide. Sequences of two obstruents in onset position are also
permitted. For syllabification, adjacent onset obstruents count as a plateau
irrespective of their underlying voicing; subsequent assimilation makes their
voicing uniform (Section~\ref{sec:lenition}). Such plateaux are tolerated
at the left syllable edge, while falling sonority between other consonant
classes remains disfavoured.

Three- and four-consonant onsets arise primarily where a prefix adjoins a complex
stem onset. They conform to the same principle: the segment immediately before the
vowel must be at least as sonorous as the one preceding it.

Coda clusters show the complementary pattern. They fall in sonority away from the
syllable peak, so that the most peripheral coda segment is typically an obstruent.
Sequences of obstruent followed by sonorant are disfavoured in coda position and
are usually resolved either by resyllabification across a following vowel-initial
suffix or by vowel retention and epenthesis
(Sections~\ref{sec:vowel-zero} and~\ref{sec:cluster-repair}).

The productive stative \ird{mužn-} retains both /ʒ/ and /n/. In
\phon{mužna}{ˈmʊʒ.na}{is beautiful}, the sequence has a licensed
syllable boundary; an obstruent--nasal onset is also admitted by the general
onset condition. Neither an epenthetic vowel nor consonant deletion is
required. The root-strong \phon{múžna}{ˈmuːʒ.na}{beautiful, attributive}
selects /ʊ/ $\rightarrow$ /uː/ without changing the cluster.

Liquid-bearing clusters obey the same requirement for a vocalic nucleus as
other clusters. Weak-\ird{e} deletion is blocked where the remaining consonants
cannot be distributed among licensed onsets and codas around the surviving
vowels. Sonority ranks the consonants within these margins; it does not confer
syllabicity on \ird{r} or \ird{l}.


\section{Suprasegmentals}\label{sec:suprasegmentals}

\subsection{Stress and secondary prominence}\label{sec:stress}\label{sec:secondary-stress}

Primary lexical stress falls on the first syllable of the minimal lexical
prosodic word. This domain comprises the lexical stem, integrated derivational
prefixes, and inflectional suffixes. Stem-grade selection,
vowel deletion or retention, and cluster repair establish the surface syllables
before stress is assigned. Thus \ird{perek} and \ird{pérku} both have initial
stress and two vocalic nuclei; neither contains a syllabic liquid. The
\orth{j} of a labial digraph contributes no nucleus, while \ird{ou} forms one
diphthongal nucleus (§\,\ref{sec:orthographic-i}).

Integrated prefixes belong to this domain: the stressed syllable of
\ird{premužna} is \ird{pre-}. Suffixation, including the conjunctive linker,
leaves stress on the first syllable of the resulting word. These are statements
about the left edge of the derived domain, rather than an accent permanently
attached to the bare root.

Lexical preverbs follow the same rule: \ird{mněkářime} \trsl{is reading
through} bears primary stress on \ird{mně-}, as does its indicative negative
\ird{zamněkářime}. The preverb belongs to the lexical domain, whereas
\ird{za-} attaches outside it (§\,\ref{sec:lexical-preverbs}). The same
domain includes \ird{le-}, \ird{ra-}, \ird{vra-}, \ird{ša-},
\ird{na-}, and \ird{ru-}: progressive \ird{lenóčkime}
\trsl{is running out}, \ird{ranóčkime} \trsl{is running into an enclosure},
\ird{vranóčkime} \trsl{is running around an obstacle}, and
\ird{rustójime} \trsl{is retracing a route} bear stress on their respective
preverbs. Negative \ird{zalenóčkime} \trsl{is not running out} retains
stress on \ird{le-}; its initial \ird{za-} is unstressed. In \ird{ša-},
\orth{š} represents the independent postalveolar /ʃ/, so [ʃa] requires
neither \orth{i} nor \orth{ě} as a palatality marker
(§\,\ref{sec:postalveolars}). Ordinary orthography joins a lexical preverb
to its root; a hyphen identifies a bound formative or bare stem only.

pronunciations abstract away from optional reduction and connected-speech
assimilation.

Dependent clitics attach outside the minimal lexical prosodic word. Proclitics
lean on following material and enclitic-like postpositions on the preceding
complement, without assigning a new primary stress to the extended group.
Such a host and its dependants can form an accentual phrase
(§\,\ref{sec:prosodic-domains}); clitic attachment and syntactic constituency
need not coincide \cite{booij1996}. Orthographic fusion likewise does not
determine the stress domain. The negative proclitic \ird{za-} $\sim$ \ird{z-}
therefore leaves stress on its host: \ird{zapjaštník} is stressed on
\ird{pja-}, whereas \ird{zapremužna} is stressed on \ird{pre-}.
The marker's short /a/ may reduce to [ɐ]; its prevocalic allomorph
\ird{z-} reflects morphologically restricted deletion and supplies the
host's initial onset through liaison
(§§\,\ref{sec:glottal-onset} and~\ref{sec:neg-allomorphy}).

Vowel quantity remains contrastive independently of stress, and the acute accent
marks length throughout (§\,\ref{sec:vowel-quantity}). Edge-based stress of this
kind is typologically familiar \cite{goedemansvanderhulst2013}; Czech supplies
a comparison for the independence of initial stress and quantity
\cite{simackova2012}, though its treatment of prepositions does not
establish the Iridian clitic boundary. Lexical stress must also be distinguished
from phrasal prominence: focus, contrast, and intonational phrasing may strengthen
or weaken the audible prominence of a word without relocating its stressed
syllable. The distinction is particularly useful where fixed stress has weak
acoustic correlates, as in Czech \cite{skarnitzleriksson2017}; it does not
prescribe an identical phonetic realisation for Iridian.

\noindent\textit{Subsidiary footing.}\quad
Words of four or more syllables have subsidiary metrical heads on their
third and subsequent odd-numbered syllables, provided that the syllable is
nonfinal. The count begins at the left edge of the minimal lexical
prosodic word and excludes dependent clitics. Neither syllable weight nor
vowel quantity changes the placement of these heads: a short vowel can bear
secondary prominence while a neighbouring long vowel remains metrically
weak. The adopted rule is
\[
  \operatorname{Stress}_{2}(\sigma_i)
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  i\in\{3,5,7,\ldots\}\ \text{and}\ i<n,
\]
where $n$ is the number of syllables in the lexical domain and $i$ counts
from its first syllable. Primary stress remains initial in words of every
length.

in Table~\ref{tab:subsidiary-feet}. The first foot bears primary stress;
subsequent feet bear subsidiary heads. An odd final syllable remains
unfooted, while a monosyllable constitutes a degenerate primary foot. In
particular, a three-syllable word has no secondary stress. Morphological
construction, vowel deletion or retention, and cluster repair precede this
parse, so suffixation can introduce a subsidiary head or bring a formerly
final odd syllable into a foot without moving primary stress.

parse. Integrated compounds follow the same calculation across the whole
lexical word, whereas two independently prosodic components retain separate
parses (§\,\ref{sec:compound-prosody}). Root boundaries introduce no
additional accent within a single domain.

Subsidiary heads specify rhythmic organisation without requiring a separate
pitch or intensity peak at each position. Modest articulatory reinforcement
or clearer vowel targets are possible realisations in careful speech;
ordinary or rapid speech may leave the subsidiary rhythm scarcely audible.
Declamatory speech permits stronger reinforcement, while emphasis on a
constituent preserves its lexical parse. These are permitted phonetic
realisations rather than measured acoustic properties of Iridian. Neither
primary nor secondary stress assigns phonological length or triggers the
morphological quantity grades G1 or G3. Broad transcriptions elsewhere may
mark primary stress alone; transcriptions concerned with metrical rhythm
show subsidiary heads explicitly. Ordinary spelling continues to reserve
the acute for vowel length.

\subsection{Rhythm and temporal organisation}\label{sec:rhythm-timing}

Lexical vowels ordinarily retain their characteristic qualities throughout
an utterance, giving successive vowel nuclei a relatively even auditory
presence despite differences of prominence. Weak grammatical material
permits stronger reduction, whereas an unstressed suffix need not be
centralised merely because it follows the primary stress. This distribution
is independent of morphologically restricted fleeting-\ird{e} deletion
(§\,\ref{sec:vowel-zero}).

Consonant clusters, vowel deletion, lexical quantity, and phrase-edge
adjustments all affect the duration of successive syllables. The regular
alternation of metrical heads requires neither equal intervals between
stresses nor equal syllable durations, and no categorical stress-timed or
syllable-timed classification is assigned. Speech-rate variation can weaken
the phonetic expression of subsidiary heads while leaving the lexical
quantity contrasts and the domain of primary stress intact. A short vowel
can receive stress or boundary-related lengthening without becoming
phonologically long; conversely, compression of an unstressed long vowel
does not itself change its lexical quantity category.

\subsection{Prosodic hierarchy and cliticisation}\label{sec:prosodic-domains}

The minimal lexical prosodic word ($\omega$) determines primary stress,
subsidiary footing, and the domain of initial glottal insertion
(§\,\ref{sec:glottal-onset}), while larger constituents organise phrasal prominence
and melody. The descriptive hierarchy used here is
\begin{center}
intonational phrase ($\iota$) $>$ phonological phrase ($\varphi$)\\
$>$ accentual phrase ($\alpha$) $>$ prosodic word ($\omega$)\\
$>$ foot ($\mathrm{Ft}$) $>$ syllable ($\sigma$)
\end{center}
The accentual phrase groups a prominent lexical host with weak dependants
and may include further lexical words that receive no independent pitch
accent. Phonological phrases organise larger constituents and permit local
boundary effects, whereas an intonational phrase has one principal nuclear
prominence and a complete contour, potentially preceded by lesser accents.
The nuclear accent is the principal accent of that phrase, even when
unaccented words follow it. These levels constitute an analysis of Iridian
prosody; neither an independently audible boundary at every level nor a
universal inventory of constituents follows from the hierarchy. The
cross-linguistic status of the prosodic word is itself contested
\cite{schieringetal2010}.

Integrated derivational prefixes, lexical preverbs, roots, and suffixes
belong to $\omega$. Negative \ird{za-}, phasal \ird{vy} and \ird{so},
nominal anchoring \ird{si}, and weak dependent postpositions attach outside
that lexical domain, contributing no syllables to its footing
(§§\,\ref{sec:class1-particles}, \ref{sec:nm-spec}, and~\ref{sec:stress}).
A host and its clitics can consequently belong to one accentual phrase
without constituting a single minimal lexical word. Independent particles,
including emphatic \ird{by}, can retain their own lexical stress; phrasal
deaccentuation alone establishes no clitic boundary. The accentual phrase
is a domain of melodic grouping, rather than a category restricted to
clitic--host combinations.

A weak postposition such as \ird{na} in \ird{staže na}
\trsl{at night} ordinarily groups with its preceding complement
(\ref{ex:tc-generic-topic}). A phrase-edge tone or local lengthening may
therefore be realised on the postposition, although the principal lexical
stress remains on the complement. Syntactic constituency influences these
groupings without determining them uniquely: a long nominal expression
can contain several phonological phrases, while compact adjacent
constituents can share one. Orthographic punctuation likewise fixes no
prosodic boundary.

\subsection{Accentual melody and tonal alignment}\label{sec:accentual-melody}

An ordinary prenuclear accent places a low target on the prominent word's
primary stressed syllable and a modest high target after it. The resulting
delayed rise, represented as L*+H, allows initial stress to coexist with a
higher pitch on a following unstressed syllable. A stressed syllable need
therefore have neither the highest pitch nor the longest vowel in its
word. Contrastive prominence favours L+H*, whose rising movement reaches
its high target on the stressed syllable, with a larger local excursion.
Both patterns preserve the lexical stress domain and the independent
quantity contrasts. The delayed rise has a descriptive parallel in Czech,
whose ordinary stress-group melody can place a low target on the stressed
syllable and a higher target subsequently \cite{cuni-sound-patterns}; the
Iridian inventory and accentual domains are specified independently here.

The tonal description uses the autosegmental--metrical distinction between
pitch accents associated with prominence and tones associated with phrase
edges \cite{ladd2008}. H and L denote relative high and low targets;
an asterisk marks association with a prominent stressed syllable, a hyphen
marks a phonological-phrase edge, and a percent sign marks an
intonational-phrase edge. In L*+H and L+H*, the plus sign groups two
targets within a pitch accent, distinguishing the target associated with
stress from its leading or trailing partner. The inventory in
Table~\ref{tab:intonation-defaults} states the Iridian conventions rather
than a transcription of measured recordings.

High and low are relative to the current register and neighbouring targets,
with restrained excursions in neutral delivery and an expanded range under
emphasis. Successive prenuclear groups may show declining or compressed
peaks, while a new intonational phrase permits a register reset. Short
stretches of speech, including a monosyllabic nucleus at a phrase edge,
compress the approach to successive targets within the available voiced
material. These alignment conventions assign no fixed fundamental
frequency, peak latency, duration ratio, or numerical pitch range.

\subsection{Nuclear contours and sentence intonation}\label{sec:intonation}

Neutral declaratives ordinarily combine a high nuclear accent with a low
terminal boundary, H* L\%, whereas information-seeking polar questions
favour a low nuclear accent followed by a high boundary, L* H\%. The
boundary symbols specify endpoints; the complete fall or rise includes
the movement from the nuclear target through any following material.
Content questions permit a final fall, with nuclear prominence determined
by their focus domain rather than invariably by the interrogative word.
These contours are preferred realisations of utterance uses, with marked
falling polar questions also possible.

In \ird{Vlak staže na hlučna} \trsl{The wolf howls at night}
(\ref{ex:tc-generic-topic}), broad focus permits modest prenuclear
prominence on \ird{vlak} and a nuclear H* on the initial syllable of
\ird{hlučna}, whose remaining syllable carries the falling tail towards
L\%. The temporal expression may form a separate accentual phrase or
remain less prominent, according to its discourse contribution. In
\ird{By vy zdrávime?} \trsl{Is he already sleeping?}
(\ref{ex:tc-null-topic}), the nuclear L* similarly associates with initial
\ird{zdrá-}, while the rise extends through the following syllables towards
H\%. Neither a falling nor a rising tail relocates stress towards the
right edge of the predicate.

Interrogative \ird{by} independently marks clause type
(§\,\ref{sec:by-pol-head}); a null interrogative
Pol\textsuperscript{0} permits the same question rise without overt
\ird{by}. Table~\ref{tab:prosodic-readings} contrasts readings with an
unchanged lexical predicate and phasal particle. The null subject remains
recoverable from context, while \ird{vy} remains external to the stress
domain of \ird{zdrávime}. The two readings of \ird{Vy zdrávime} differ in
clause type and contour; the melody realises the question without replacing
its interrogative Pol\textsuperscript{0}.

A continuing phonological phrase may end in H-, realised as a modest rise
or elevated plateau within a larger contour. More marked reservation,
concession, or contrastive continuation permits a fall followed by a rise,
which can extend over several words. For a separately phrased contrastive
topic, H* followed by a low turning point and terminal H\% schematises
such a fall-rise. The low point belongs to the unfolding contour rather
than a newly introduced lexical tone; the precise distribution of the
movement depends on the available material. Distributed and split fall-rises
have a comparative precedent in Slovene, where the rising component can
signal syntactic or semantic nonfinality \cite{komar1999}. The Iridian
tonal sequence is specified independently; neither this contour nor
ordinary H- is obligatory on every incomplete discourse move.

\subsection{Information-structural prosody}\label{sec:phrasal-prominence}

A neutral broad-focus clause ordinarily places its principal phrasal
prominence on the final lexical predicate, provided that the predicate
belongs to the comment and no narrower focus overrides that distribution.
Narrow focus can instead select an earlier nominal, modifier, or other
constituent, with marked contrast commonly expressed by L+H*. Given
material following the nucleus may be deaccented or have a compressed
pitch range, while retaining its lexical stress, subsidiary footing, and
vowel contrasts. This relation between focus and prominence does not
itself diagnose movement to \synhead{FocP}
(§\,\ref{sec:tc-focus-contrast}).

In \ird{Deno že zastráda} \trsl{Nothing is lacking to me}
(\ref{ex:nm-negative-concord}), broad focus can place the nucleus on
\ird{strá-}, the first lexical syllable of the negative predicate;
proclitic \ird{za-} remains unstressed. A correction contrasting nothing
with something can instead place L+H* on initial \ird{de-}, leaving
\ird{že zastráda} as a compressed tail towards L\%. The negative
indefinite remains interpreted within the scope of negation in both
readings (§\,\ref{sec:tc-quantificational-pivots}); its prominence neither
makes it a referential topic nor changes negative-concord licensing.

The contrastive topic introduces a parallel subquestion, whereas the
principal nuclear accent of the comment supplies the local answer. A
separate intonational phrase permits the fuller fall-rise described above,
although neither a rise nor a pause defines contrastive topicality
(Chapter~\ref{ch:infstr}). A topic shift may instead be accompanied by a
modest register reset without invoking a contrasted set. A null continuing
topic supplies no phonetic material on which to realise a topic accent
or boundary.

\subsection{Prosodic phrasing and connected speech}\label{sec:boundary-phenomena}

A tightly integrated converb chain can occupy one intonational phrase
containing several phonological phrases. Its internal boundaries may mark
continuation with H- without requiring a pause or a full register reset,
while the principal nuclear contour belongs to the final assertion
(§\,\ref{sec:converb-phrasing}). Longer chains, changes of discourse
perspective, and deliberate narrative pacing permit separate intonational
phrases, each with its own nucleus. Embedded and relative clauses likewise
need not be followed by a pause merely because substantial dependent
material precedes the main predicate. The independent contour of an
insubordinate conditional belongs to its own intonational phrase, although
the suspended interpretation does not by itself select a falling boundary
(§\,\ref{sec:cond-insub}).

Weak clause-final discourse particles can remain within the accentual
phrase of a preceding lexical host and carry the final boundary target
without receiving an independent nuclear accent. In the neutral
predicate-accented pattern, the nucleus remains on the preceding lexical
host, while its falling or rising tail extends onto the particle; an
earlier narrow focus can instead supply the nucleus, with both host and
particle belonging to the following tail. Emphatic \ird{len}, \ird{prav}, \ird{však}, or \ird{blěc} may
instead form an accentual phrase with an independent pitch accent; a
separate intonational phrase also permits its own nuclear contour
(§\,\ref{sec:disc-final-particles}). The particle's presence changes
neither the host's lexical stress nor its footing. No obligatory individual
tune is assigned to each particle.

Lexical quantity remains contrastive under the durational adjustments of
connected speech (§\,\ref{sec:vowel-quantity}). A short vowel may undergo
phonetic lengthening near an intonational boundary without acquiring the
long-vowel category or a morphological quantity grade. The allowed
boundary cues include modest lengthening or local pitch movement at weaker
phrase edges and stronger lengthening, register reset, or a pause at
intonational boundaries. Silence is therefore optional at a phrase edge,
and its absence does not establish that two words form one stress domain.
Deliberate and poetic delivery permit clearer subsidiary prominence,
longer boundary transitions, and larger pitch excursions within the same
lexical stress system; neutral connected speech favours weaker internal
boundaries and smoother transitions.

An independent vowel-initial lexical word can retain its glottal onset
within a continuous intonational phrase, including a converb chain.
Prominence and stronger junctures favour fuller laryngeal marking, without
making [ʔ] a pitch accent or a boundary tone; H- continuation neither
requires nor suppresses insertion (§\,\ref{sec:glottal-onset}). Two
successive vowel-initial lexical words each license the onset at their own
left edge. Where full [ʔ] is realised, it prevents a preceding consonant
from serving as the following vowel's onset. This predicts preservation of
the preceding coda, while leaving the cross-word ordering of vocalisation
and voicing rules unresolved.

The lexical forms establish coda /l, v/ vocalisation, final devoicing, and
regressive voicing assimilation within inflected words
(§§\,\ref{sec:l-vocalisation}, \ref{sec:v-realisation}, and~\ref{sec:lenition}).
Suffixation can resyllabify a stem-final consonant before a vowel, as in
\ird{byl}/\ird{byla}. Their extension across an external clitic boundary
or between independent words, including whether such resyllabification
precedes vocalisation and final devoicing, is not specified. Apart from the
restricted liaison of negative \ird{z-}, prosodic attachment alone
licenses no additional cross-word segmental rule;
the metrical exclusion of a clitic is independent of those unresolved
segmental domains.


\section{Orthographic principles}

\subsection{Vowel letters, softness, and the glide}\label{sec:orthographic-i}\label{sec:orthographic-e-hook}

The letters \orth{i, í} always represent vowels, /ɪ, iː/ respectively,
and each supplies a syllabic nucleus. Consonantal softness belongs to the
lexical representation or to the output selected by morphology; its spelling
varies with the following vowel. Before /ɛ/, \orth{ě} marks softness in
\orth{tě, dě, ně, lě, pě, bě, mě, vě, kě, hě}, while the same consonants
with plain \orth{e} retain their plain specification. These correspondences
apply alike to lexical and morphologically selected outputs; the complete
normalisation table is given in §\,\ref{sec:e-hook}.

The citation form \ird{sviňek} \trsl{girl; daughter} retains the lexical
spelling \orth{ňe} for /ɲɛ/ before its fleeting vowel. Its reduced
\ird{svíňk-} and front-grade \ird{svíňc-} stems retain the palatal
nasal without extending this spelling exception to productive endings.
Regular Class~I plural \ird{véně}, from \ird{veň} \trsl{woman},
uses ordinary \orth{ně} for /ɲɛ/.

Before other vowels and word-finally, soft coronals use \orth{ť, ď, ň, ľ}.
A high front vowel does not itself select their soft grade: schematic
\ird{ti} [tɪ], \ird{ťi} [cɪ], \ird{ty} [tɨ̞], and \ird{ťy} [cɨ̞]
separate consonantal palatality from vowel quality. These syllables have no
assigned lexical meanings. Labials instead have predictable secondary
palatalisation before /ɪ, iː/, where their plain consonant letters suffice,
including before traditional \orth{é} in standard speech.
Any lexical palatal specification is retained in this environment even
though its spelling coincides with that of contextual palatalisation.

\subsection{Labial digraphs before back vowels}

In native words, \orth{pj, bj, mj, vj} before \orth{a, á, o, ó, u, ú}
represent single secondarily palatalised consonants /pʲ, bʲ, mʲ, vʲ/.
Thus \ird{pjaštou} [ˈpʲaʃ.tou̯] `food' contains two vocalic nuclei;
the \orth{j} of its initial digraph supplies neither a vowel nor an
independent glide. This convention is restricted to a labial immediately
before a back vowel, including the initial vowel of \orth{ou}. Native
entries specify no contrast between /CʲV/ and independent /CjV/ in this
environment. A foreign form retaining an independent postlabial /j/
requires an explicit pronunciation rather than an extension of the digraph
reading to its phonological representation.

The postalveolars \orth{š, ž, č} use their dedicated consonant letters
before every vowel, as in \ird{šarc} [ˈʃarts] `door' and
\ird{ža} [ˈʒa] `bell'. They take neither the labial digraph device nor
palatality-marking \orth{ě}; pronounced \orth{i, í} after them retain
their ordinary vowel values. Learned \ird{ř} is a lexical spelling of
/ʒ/ (§\,\ref{sec:r-hook}).

\section{Borrowing and assimilation patterns}\label{sec:loan-prosody}

A borrowing integrated into Iridian phonology follows the ordinary rules for
initial primary stress and subsidiary footing, independently of the stress and
vowel quantity of its source. Source-language stress may occur in deliberate foreign pronunciation of a name or quotation;
such a pronunciation is distinct from an integrated lexical exception. No general
class of exceptional native stress is posited here. Any loan that retains an
exceptional stress pattern in ordinary Iridian requires an explicit lexical
specification, together with its vowel quantities and syllabification.
