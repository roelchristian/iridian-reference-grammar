\chapter{Clause structure and word order}\label{ch:clause-structure}\label{ch:simple}

\section{Theoretical background and analytical conventions}
\label{sec:th-background}

The analysis of clause structure and word order developed in this chapter
adopts a derivational Minimalist framework, in which syntactic objects are
assembled hierarchically through Merge and the resulting structures supply
representations for interpretation and pronunciation at Logical Form (LF)
and Phonological Form (PF), respectively
\cites[ch.~3]{chomsky1995}{chomsky2000}. Although this architecture
specifies the general operations by which syntactic structure is
constructed, the organisation of the resulting clause is informed by the
articulated conception of functional syntax developed within the
cartographic tradition, particularly Rizzi's~\cite[§1]{rizzi1997} analysis
of the clausal periphery and its relation to the inflectional domain. The
distinction between the restricted inventory of operations responsible for
structure building and the more elaborate functional configurations thereby
generated follows Cinque and Rizzi~\cite[§5]{cinquerizzi2010}, whose account
permits a finely articulated hierarchy without requiring a corresponding
proliferation of primitive syntactic operations. The relationship between
this abstract structure and its phonological expression is treated within
Distributed Morphology, under which morphological exponents are inserted
into structures whose hierarchical organisation has already been established
by the syntax \cite{embicknoyer2007}. Taken together, these assumptions
provide the basis for analysing argument structure, case licensing, operator
scope and constituent placement within the Iridian clause, while permitting
the positions in which syntactic dependencies are established, the
configurations in which they receive interpretation and the forms through
which they are pronounced to be distinguished. The functional projections in
Table~\ref{tab:th-clause-spine} constitute the canonical Iridian hierarchy,
whose inventory and relative dominance relations are fixed by the grammatical
description. Their theoretical interpretation draws on the comparative
literature without importing every
functional category distinguished there into the language.

The need to distinguish hierarchical relations from surface precedence is
evident in ordinary active transitive clauses, where the actor precedes the
patient in the progressive but follows it in the perfective, although the
finite verb remains clause-final in both constructions
(§\,\ref{sec:vm-asp-case}). Since the patient retains its lower relation to
the verb as its complement (§\,\ref{sec:vm-root}), and since participant
selection and case licensing depend upon the completed verbal configuration
(§\,\ref{sec:vm-selection-domains}), the alternation cannot be understood as
a difference in thematic relations merely because the arguments occur in
different linear positions. Under the distinction formulated by
Chomsky~\cite[p.~140]{chomsky2008}, \term{External Merge} combines distinct
syntactic objects, whereas \term{Internal Merge} establishes a higher
occurrence of an object already present in the derivation, so that
displacement may alter the pronounced position of an argument while
preserving the structural relation in which its thematic interpretation is
established. Where feature-sensitive dependencies are implicated,
\term{Agree} provides an additional operation through which syntactic
features may be related without requiring displacement of either
participant, as in the probe--goal conception developed by
Chomsky~\cite[p.~101]{chomsky2000} and the broader treatment of agreement
dependencies in Deal~\cite{deal2025}. Although Iridian lacks verbal person
agreement, for which no dedicated $\varphi$-agreement projection is
consequently posited, the absence of such morphology does not determine the
computation of nominal case, whose possible mechanisms remain distinguished
in §\,\ref{sec:vm-selection-domains}. Configurational licensing may coexist
with licensing by functional heads, as Baker and
Vinokurova~\cite[§1]{bakervinokurova2010} argue for Sakha, while
dependent-case patterns may alternatively be derived through successive
applications of Agree, as proposed for Shawi by Clem and
Deal~\cite[§1]{clemdeal2026}. These comparative accounts establish distinct
computational possibilities against which the Iridian distribution may be
evaluated, although neither the occurrence of case morphology nor the
ordering of arguments is taken, independently of further evidence, to
determine which mechanism the grammar requires.

The articulation of the higher clause follows the cartographic method of
distinguishing functional positions through recurrent structural
dependencies \cites{rizzi1997}[§§2.1--2.2]{rizzibocci2017}, whereby
differences in interpretation, although potentially relevant, do not by
themselves establish the existence of separate syntactic heads. Where
constituent ordering, scope restrictions and connectivity converge, distinct
positions may be motivated even when the corresponding heads lack overt
exponence, while the comparison of adverbial ordering and functional-head
sequences undertaken by Cinque \cite{cinque1999} provides a methodological
precedent without requiring that his proposed universal hierarchy be imposed
upon Iridian. Within the present analysis, the upper clausal sequence is
TopP $>$ FocP $>$ OrdP $>$ SpeechActP $>$ outer EvidP $>$ PolP, beneath
which the lower Comm-field groups heterogeneous operators without assigning
them to a single functional head. The designation \term{Pol} is reserved for
the interrogative operator, realised by \ird{by} or zero, whose contribution
to whole-clause typing in polar and content questions is independent of
focus (§\,\ref{sec:by-pol-head}). It is accordingly distinguished from
indicative NegP, while neither its name nor its position entails the
existence of a general polarity projection or the adoption of the
cartographic ordering associated with Int and Force. The canonical hierarchy
thus fixes the functional distinctions and relative positions of the Iridian
clause, while the constructional descriptions specify when its projections
are licensed and how particular dependencies are computed within them.

Within this articulated periphery, the interpretation of higher constituents
depends upon discourse relations which, although capable of receiving
structural expression, must be distinguished from the grammatical relations
established within the lower clause. An \term{aboutness topic} supplies the
discourse subject matter with respect to which a comment is interpreted
\cite[chs.~4--5]{lambrecht1994}, its pragmatic function being distinct from
logical predication \cite[§4]{gecseg2006} and implicated in the organisation
and management of the common ground \cite[§2.2]{krifka2008}. \term{Focus},
by comparison, introduces alternatives relevant to the discourse context
\cite{rooth1992}, while the distinction between information focus and
identificational focus developed by É.~Kiss \cite{ekiss1998} motivates
separating ordinary focus interpretation from marked displacement without
extending the exhaustivity properties of Hungarian identificational focus to
Iridian. The grammatical selection of the direct-case pivot is independent
of these discourse functions: direct case serves nominative and absolutive
functions in the NOM- and ERG-aspect domains respectively, while the
designation \term{absolutive pivot}, where employed more broadly, remains a
descriptive convention. Although a referential pivot preferentially receives
an aboutness interpretation where the discourse supports one, this
preference does not entail occupation of Spec,\synhead{TopP}, since
grammatical pivot selection and the establishment of an aboutness relation
need not coincide in a single structural position. Linked topicalisation and
marked focus consequently involve independently licensed high dependencies,
whereas OrdP provides a position for constituent placement without itself
assigning either discourse function, and ordinary wh-placement does not
require movement to \synhead{FocP} (Chapter~\ref{ch:infstr};
§\,\ref{sec:th-wh-interrogation}).

Because arguments may be pronounced before the particle field while
retaining dependencies established within the lower predicate, including
those relevant to negative concord and actor-oriented reflexive binding,
their higher placement must be reconciled with the locality conditions
governing displacement and interpretation. The analysis accordingly assumes
that movement may proceed through accessible intermediate positions, subject
to the restrictions imposed by a phase-based derivational architecture. A
\term{phase}, in this framework, defines a locality domain whose completed
material is submitted to \term{Transfer}, through which syntactic structure
is delivered to the interfaces \cite[p.~142]{chomsky2008}. Under the
\term{Phase Impenetrability Condition} formulated by
Chomsky~\cite[pp.~12--14]{chomsky2001}, the complement of a phase head
becomes inaccessible to subsequent syntactic operations once transferred,
whereas the phase edge remains accessible, permitting successive-cyclic
displacement through intermediate landing sites. The availability of
functional positions through which movement may pass does not, however,
establish that every such projection constitutes a phase, whose
identification requires independent syntactic and interpretive evidence, of
the kind investigated by Legate~\cite{legate2003} through reconstruction and
interface diagnostics. Where displacement has occurred, the higher and lower
occurrences form a movement chain, whose syntactic representation is
distinguished from the determination of which occurrence receives
phonological realisation, following the treatment of chain pronunciation in
Nunes~\cite{nunes2011}. The availability of lower occurrences for
interpretation does not, in turn, require uniform reconstruction, since
different dependencies may permit or require interpretation at different
positions within the chain, as illustrated by the selective reconstruction
effects examined by Fox~\cite[pp.~157--158]{fox1999}. The particular
connectivity conditions governing Iridian are specified in
§\,\ref{sec:pivot-scope-connectivity}, while the derivation of ordinary
constituent placement additionally requires the multiple ordered edge
positions and restricted \term{tucking-in} operation defined in
§\,\ref{sec:pivot-placement}. This latter operation has a formal precedent
in the analysis of multiple movement developed by
Richards~\cite[§2]{richards2002}, and its departure from strict root
extension is compatible with the possibility recognised by
Chomsky~\cite[p.~141]{chomsky2008}; the selective searches and restricted
tucking-in defined in §\,\ref{sec:pivot-placement} are the adopted Iridian
placement rule. Its language-specific conditions preserve the ordering and
connectivity requirements of the grammar, without being attributed to either
comparative source.

Although hierarchical constituency and movement dependencies determine the
structural relations among constituents, their linear order requires further
assumptions concerning the mapping of syntactic structure to pronunciation.
The lower Iridian predicate follows a predominantly head-final convention,
under which a complement precedes its selecting head
(§\,\ref{sec:vm-obj-base}), while the cross-linguistic word-order
correlations documented by Dryer \cite{dryer1992} provide a typological
comparison without constituting a derivation of that convention. Kayne's
proposal to derive linear precedence from asymmetric c-command
\cite{kayne1994} represents a stronger alternative, whose assumptions are
not adopted here as a general account of Iridian head-finality. Within the
higher clause, where arguments and modifiers may precede the particle field,
their ordering requires the positions supplied by OrdP, while selected
finite complements additionally make use of its separately licensed external
edge (§\,\ref{sec:vm-po-comp}). The interrogative particle \ird{by}, which
is locally head-initial (§\,\ref{sec:th-head-finality}), presents an
exception to the predominant head-final pattern, and its relation to higher
silent final heads raises a further question concerning the
\term{Final-over-Final Condition}, which restricts configurations in which a
head-final head immediately dominates a head-initial phrase within a single
extended projection \cite{biberauerholmbergroberts2014}. Whether the
relevant domain restriction applies to the Iridian periphery remains
unresolved, since the surface order of the particle and neighbouring
constituents does not alone establish the extended-projection relations upon
which the condition depends.

The distinction between abstract syntactic structure and phonological
realisation extends to the internal organisation of morphologically complex
words, for which the grammar adopts the framework of \term{Distributed
Morphology} \cite{hallemarantz1993}. Its principal commitments include
\term{Late Insertion}, whereby phonological exponents are supplied only
after the relevant syntactic structure has been assembled;
\term{Underspecification}, whereby a Vocabulary Item need not specify every
feature of the terminal it realises; and the preservation of syntactically
hierarchical structure throughout word formation, subject to independently
specified morphological operations \cite[pp.~275--276]{hallemarantz1994}.
Under this architecture, \term{Vocabulary Insertion} selects the most
specific compatible phonological exponent through competition among
Vocabulary Items, whose applicability is determined by their feature
specifications and contextual conditions, while morphological operations
capable of altering the configuration available for insertion remain
distinct from insertion itself \cite{embicknoyer2007}. The resulting
correspondence between abstract structure and overt form consequently
permits zero exponence, whereby a syntactic position receives no pronounced
exponent; cumulative exponence, whereby several features are realised
together; and contextual allomorphy, whereby alternative exponents are
selected in restricted environments \cite{embicknoyer2007,embick2010}. The
relation between the order of morphological constituents and the hierarchy
of functional heads is further constrained by the qualified correspondence
associated with the \term{Mirror Principle}, whose application to Iridian
verbal structure is developed in §\,\ref{sec:vm-overview}
\cite{baker1985,harley2013b}. These principles permit morphological ordering
and exponence to inform the analysis of functional structure, without
requiring every syntactic head to possess a distinct overt exponent or every
morphological segment to correspond independently to a single syntactic
projection.

The relevance of this distinction is illustrated by several independently
established patterns of Iridian verbal morphology, whose exponents reflect
different relations between syntactic configuration and phonological
realisation. Asp.Align cumulatively realises aspect and alignment on a
single head, without requiring a separate AlignP (§\,\ref{sec:vm-asp}),
while indicative verbal \ird{za-} and equative \ird{staly} realise
Neg\textsuperscript{0} in different predicational environments. Negative
existential \ird{zad-}, by contrast, realises Exist\textsuperscript{0}
suppletively under negation, with Neg itself receiving zero exponence
(§\,\ref{sec:vm-neg}). The inner indirect evidential exhibits a further
pattern of contextually conditioned exponence, selecting \ird{-al} after a
consonant and \ird{-l} after a vowel in the completed finite host
(§\,\ref{sec:vm-evid}). Although these constructions all involve the
realisation of syntactically represented features, their particular mappings
differ, and the locality conditions governing each must therefore be
established with reference to the relevant morphological and syntactic
environment. The licensing of nominal case and the selection of participants
remain dependent upon Asp.Align, orientation, Mood and the constructional
configuration (Chapter~\ref{ch:argument-structure}), whereas case exponents
realise features licensed within that configuration. Neither a particular
verbal ending nor Vocabulary Insertion consequently determines,
independently of the syntax, which argument receives direct case or where
that argument is pronounced.

The canonical clause spine in Table~\ref{tab:th-clause-spine} brings the
lexical and argument-structural domain, the ordered operators and the distinct
peripheral dependencies into a common architecture. The established verbal
morphology and argument frames determine the lower constructional
configurations, while particle distributions and interpretations specify the
licensed operator relations (Chapter~\ref{ch:particles}), and discourse
conditions distinguish ordinary placement from topic and focus dependencies
(Chapter~\ref{ch:infstr}). These structural commitments remain binding
where a particular mechanism is not fully specified: the computation of case
or the morphological assembly of a higher affix may remain open without
making the projection that licenses the relevant relation provisional.
\synhead{OrdP} supplies the canonical ordinary placement edge, whose ordered
searches and locality and connectivity conditions are defined in
§\,\ref{sec:pivot-placement}.

Section~\ref{sec:grammatical-architecture} defines the canonical hierarchy,
whose implications for constituent order are developed in
§\,\ref{sec:th-head-finality} through the head-final convention and its local
exception, and in §\,\ref{sec:word-order} through the account of ordinary
placement, its connectivity conditions and the ordering of lexical adverbs.
Against this structural background,
§§\,\ref{sec:vm-comp-headfinal}--\ref{sec:vm-preverbal-zone} examine how the
base positions of complements relate to the preverbal fields and to
construction-specific placement, before §\,\ref{sec:vm-summary} brings the
resulting account of clause structure into correspondence with the
distribution of verbal exponents.

The structural commitments established here inform the analyses in
Chapters~\ref{ch:predicates}--\ref{ch:infstr}, which address predicate
constructions, negation and concord, particle scope, clause typing and
modality, and information structure, respectively, while preserving
throughout the analytical distinctions between syntactic licensing,
morphological realisation and discourse interpretation.
Chapters~\ref{ch:embedding}--\ref{ch:clause-linking} extend the account to
embedding, relativisation and clause linking, examining the conditions that
finite and reduced domains impose upon argument licensing, constituent
placement and operator interpretation. These dependencies provide the
background for Chapter~\ref{ch:discourse}, which considers how the
distinctions between pivothood, topicality and stance bear upon reference
management and spoken interaction.


\section{Grammatical architecture}\label{sec:grammatical-architecture}
\label{sec:th-clause-structure}% cross-reference compatibility

The Iridian finite clause is organised through a fixed hierarchy of lexical,
argument-structural, inflectional, operator and peripheral projections.
Table~\ref{tab:th-clause-spine} defines the canonical architecture used
throughout this grammar: its heads have invariant relative positions,
although their projection is conditioned by the construction, clause type and
selectional requirements. Structural dominance determines containment and the
domains available for grammatical dependencies, whereas pronunciation
additionally depends upon head direction, displacement and morphological
realisation. This functional articulation follows the cartographic conception
of clause structure, within the Minimalist framework introduced in
§\,\ref{sec:th-background}; the theoretical precedent concerns the
articulation of functional structure, while the inventory and ordering of
Iridian heads are specified by the language's grammar
\cite{rizzi1997,cinquerizzi2010}.

The lower verbal domain contains the categorised lexical predicate, its inner
valency structure and, where licensed, the actor-introducing layer, followed
by causation and outer orientation. Asp.Align dominates the completed derived
predicate and cumulatively expresses viewpoint aspect and alignment on one
head, projected as \synhead{AspP}. In negative indicatives, NegP is projected
above it, while phasal \ird{vy} and \ird{so} evaluate the
positive or negative predication. The higher Comm-field contains additive,
exclusive, affirmative, contrastive and interim-sequential operators. Mood
and the inner evidential complete the finite inflectional domain.
Interrogative \synhead{PolP}, outer
\synhead{EvidP} and \synhead{SpeechActP} stand above that domain, beneath
the distinct projections for ordinary placement, marked focus and linked
topicalisation.

\synhead{IP} abbreviates \synhead{MoodP} or, when the inner evidential is
present, the higher \synhead{EvidP}$_{\mathrm{inner}}$, together with the
selected lower structure. The abbreviation introduces no additional head and
implies neither an independent tense projection nor person agreement. In
verbal clauses, the \term{predicate domain} comprises \synhead{AspP} and
its lower lexical, valency, actor, causative and orientation structure, without an additional
\synhead{PredP}. In hierarchical sequences, $>$ means `structurally higher
than'; it need not indicate immediate dominance, linear precedence or a
succession of overt morphemes. Surface sequences are identified separately
below.

The sequence fixes the relative positions of instantiated layers without
requiring every clause to project them all. CausP belongs to causative
formation, inner antipassive Voice and Appl are alternatives within the
established co-occurrence restriction, and the actor head is present only
where the predicate independently licenses an actor. That head, designated
\synhead{VoiceP}$_{\mathrm{actor}}$ in the table, lies above inner valency
and below Caus; it remains distinct from both verbalising $v$ and outer
orientation (§§\,\ref{sec:vm-root}, \ref{sec:vm-selection-domains}).
Finite verbal predication requires Asp.Align and Mood, although either can
have a zero exponent in its licensed environment
(§§\,\ref{sec:vm-mood-asp}, \ref{sec:vm-moodp}). A projected but
phonologically silent head is thus distinct from an absent projection, just
as a cumulative exponent need not correspond to several independent heads.

Construction-specific predicate structure occupies the lower domain without
altering the hierarchy of the higher heads. StateP and its licensed BecomeP
or ManifestP extension belong below inner valency, causation, orientation
and Asp; a holder or experiencer licensed within StateP does not thereby
require an actor-introducing layer (§\,\ref{sec:vm-tension-stative}).
ExistP supplies the lexical predication in existential constructions,
within the aspect-bearing structure
(§\,\ref{sec:existential-constructions}). Zero-copula EquP instead lacks
verbal Asp.Align, while finite \ird{neh-} has the verbal spine
(§\,\ref{sec:copula}). Reduced constructions select their own constituents:
participant nominals and reduced \ird{-ovy} relatives select AspP, retaining
its lower structure while excluding indicative Neg, Phas, the Comm-field,
Mood and clausal evidentiality; purposive converbs select a smaller verbal
domain (§§\,\ref{sec:nominalised}, \ref{sec:th-rel-structure},
\ref{sec:vm-purp-comp}). Their selection and interpretation do not introduce
the finite periphery into a reduced predicate.

In finite copular predication, the verbal categoriser $v[\mathrm{Cop}]$
selects \synhead{StateP} with a nominal predicate in stative predication and
\synhead{BecomeP} containing it in transitional predication. The holder is
licensed within \synhead{StateP}, while the finite configuration values
its direct case and licenses indirect case on the nominal predicate
(§\,\ref{sec:neh}). This restricted verbal construction shares nominal
property application with \synhead{EquP}, without containing that
non-verbal projection or an actor layer solely by virtue of expressing a
transition.

The Comm-field is an articulated region of heterogeneous operators, whose
established ordering and combination restrictions are specified in
Chapter~\ref{ch:particles}. It has no single Comm\textsuperscript{0} and
assigns no uniform semantic type to all its members. Within the ordinary
particle field, the specified surface partial order is outer
speech-act/evidential material $>$ \ird{by} $>$ \ird{oh} $>$ \ird{jas}
$>$ \ird{vy}/\ird{so}. This order neither licenses every combination nor
settles each comment operator's position relative to every other member;
unspecified combinations remain distinct from prohibited ones
(§\,\ref{sec:particle-combinations}). Cinque's comparison of adverbial and
functional-head order supplies a methodological precedent for relating
operator distribution to functional articulation, without imposing his
universal hierarchy on the Iridian inventory \cite[chs.~3--4]{cinque1999}.
The restricted lower interpretation of \ird{kamo} with \ird{vy}/\ird{so}
follows the construction-specific composition rule in
§\,\ref{sec:th-exclusive-phasal}; its overt Comm-field position does not
license unrestricted lowering of focus-associated operators.

Interrogative Pol\textsuperscript{0}, realised by \ird{by} or zero,
occupies the fixed position between inner and outer EvidP and precedes its
complement locally. It types the clause as interrogative independently of
marked focus and remains distinct from indicative NegP
(§\,\ref{sec:by-pol-head}). Upper speech-act and evidential particles
occupy specifiers with silent final heads, whereas \ird{by} itself
realises a head. The inner evidential has the conditioned suffixes
\ird{-al} after a consonant and \ird{-l} after a vowel in the completed
finite host (§\,\ref{sec:vm-evid}); outer EvidP hosts the separately
pronounced \ird{oče}, \ird{hladě}, \ird{kady} and \ird{či}.
Their shared evidential designation does not identify one syntactic position
or settle a uniform semantic analysis of their contributions
(§\,\ref{sec:evidential-semantic-diagnostics}).

The verbal complex realises features from several syntactically distinct
layers. Its established morphological template places valency before Caus,
Caus before outer orientation, and Asp.Align before Mood and the inner
evidential (§\,\ref{sec:vm-overview}); Asp.Align remains a single
feature-bearing head rather than separate Asp and Align projections.
Distributed Morphology distinguishes the hierarchical representation of
such features from the insertion of their phonological exponents
\cite{hallemarantz1993,embicknoyer2007}, while the qualified Mirror Principle
relates morphological order to structural organisation without reducing all
affixation to syntactic head movement
\cite{baker1985,harley2013b}. The Iridian host template fixes suffix order,
but does not itself specify the operation assembling higher Mood and inner
Evid with the verbal host across intervening licensed particle structure;
Late Insertion alone supplies no such operation. This limit concerns the
assembly of the morphological word, leaving the syntactic heights and the
established suffixal forms intact.

Indicative verbal Neg has the explicit PF instruction stated in
§\,\ref{sec:vm-neg}: \ird{za-}/\ird{z-} is realised at the left edge
of the independently formed inflected verbal host, whose internal suffix
order is preserved. In zero-copula predication, head-final Neg directly
selects EquP and is realised by \ird{staly}; in existential negation,
\ird{zad-} instead realises Exist\textsuperscript{0} suppletively under
Neg, which has a zero exponent. Negative non-indicatives contain negative
Mood without a separate indicative Neg head, their silent negative operator
being interpreted at the AspP edge below phasal composition
(§\,\ref{sec:vm-moodp}). These constructions preserve distinct relations
between the interpreted negative operator, its syntactic licensing and the
pronounced marker.

The argument inventory is determined by lexical selection and the licensed
valency operations, while orientation, Asp.Align, Mood and the constructional
configuration determine the finite case frame and direct-case pivot
(§\,\ref{sec:vm-selection-domains}). Vocabulary Insertion realises that
configuration without assigning nominal case or selecting a constituent for
placement. OrdP supplies the ordinary ordered edge above the particle field,
with a silent head whose placement requirements distinguish the selected
participant from residual eligible constituents. It assigns neither thematic
roles nor case and carries no topic or focus feature; its occupants do not
uniformly receive an A-movement analysis. The selective first search,
subsequent residual searches and restricted tucking-in are defined in
§\,\ref{sec:pivot-placement}. A selected finite \ird{po}/\ird{poby} CP
occupies the separately licensed external OrdP edge, above the ordinary
constituents, while retaining its independent finite structure and lower
selection relation (§\,\ref{sec:vm-po-comp}).

FocP and TopP license higher dependencies independently of ordinary
placement. A linked aboutness topic retains its lower case, whereas a
constituent bearing marked focus or a wh-phrase singled out by the restricted
fronting dependency occupies Spec,\synhead{FocP}
(Chapter~\ref{ch:infstr}; §\,\ref{sec:th-wh-interrogation}). Ordinary
focus interpretation does not require FocP, and a direct-case pivot need not
occupy TopP. The cartographic distinction between peripheral functional
positions supplies the theoretical background to their separation
\cite{rizzibocci2017}; their Iridian licensing conditions remain distinct
from the functions assigned to comparable positions in other languages.

\section{Head-finality}
\label{sec:th-head-finality}

Iridian is predominantly head-final. Its lexical phrases and lower clauses are
internally verb-final or head-final: arguments precede their predicates,
modifiers precede their heads, adpositions follow their nominal complements (as
postpositions, cf.\ §\,\ref{sec:nm-postpositions}), and subordinate clauses precede
the matrix predicate. This property, which is statable as a general principle of
phrase structure, has several major
consequences that recur throughout the syntactic analysis.

In an ordinary finite clause, the inflected verbal host is clause-final,
while the heads whose features it realises retain their distinct syntactic
positions (§\,\ref{sec:grammatical-architecture}). Head-finality places a
lower complement before its selecting head; ordinary placement additionally
raises arguments and modifiers across the articulated particle projections
(§\,\ref{sec:pivot-placement}). A selected finite CP occupies the separately
licensed external OrdP edge, before the ordinary matrix constituents
(§\,\ref{sec:vm-po-comp}). Upper particles occupy specifiers before the
complements of their silent heads, without forming one phonological word
through adjacency.

Head-finality also has consequences for relativisation and complementation.
Relative clauses precede the head noun they modify; finite complement clauses
precede the matrix predicate that embeds them; and purpose-clause converbs
precede the verb they are oriented toward. Ordinary converb adjuncts precede
the matrix clause as a whole, although their lower attachment to IP or vP
does not itself derive this chain order, whose mapping remains unspecified
(§\,\ref{sec:vm-converb-adjunct}). The interrogative head
\ird{by} is a local exception: Pol\textsuperscript{0} precedes its
complement. Ordinary placement puts overt arguments and modifiers before
this head, yielding ordinary constituents $>$ \ird{by} $>$ lower
predication. The other particles retain their independently specified
specifier realisations and silent final heads.

\section{Word order}\label{sec:word-order}

The traditional descriptive shorthand for Iridian word order is \term{SOV}, but
that label should not be mistaken for the structural primitive of the clause. A
more faithful characterisation is \term{pivot-initial, verb-final}, or more
specifically \term{absolutive-first, verb-final}. The finite verb itself is
rigidly clause-final; the ordering of preverbal nominals is governed by
information structure and alignment rather than by a fixed subject-object
template.

In ordinary active nominative-domain clauses, the agent is the absolutive
pivot, so this pattern resembles SOV. In ordinary ergative transitives,
the same template yields a patient-initial order of the type
\term{absolutive $>$ ergative $>$ verb}. Without a distinct high topic, the neutral order is therefore
`pivot before the remaining arguments, with the finite verb last'.

\subsection{Participant selection and finite placement}
\label{sec:pivot-placement}

The direct-case pivot is identified by its case and the construction's
participant-selection rules, independently of its pronounced position.
Orientation, aspect and valency determine which participant is selected;
directive Mood can override ordinary indicative selection
(§§\,\ref{sec:vm-asp}, \ref{sec:aspect-caus}). Selection, case licensing and
placement are distinct dependencies. A relation established by Agree need not
involve displacement, and an exponent inserted at PF cannot retrospectively
choose a nominal for movement in the syntax.

Ordinary finite arguments and modifiers are pronounced at a clause-local
 placement edge, represented here as \synhead{OrdP}, above the particle
 projections. Its silent head bears an ordered set of placement requirements:
 first the participant selected by the completed Voice--Asp.Align--Mood
 configuration, then the remaining eligible constituents in their lower
 structural order. Each eligible chain bears a placement feature distinct
 from its Case and agreement features, so Case licensing does not discharge
 its placement requirement. The initial search restricts that feature to the
 chain identified by participant selection; the residual searches target
 the remaining chains. Ord discharges the feature at the final ordinary
 edge, while intermediate phase edges only register the chain's accessibility.
 A closer non-selected argument cannot intervene in the first search because
 it does not satisfy that search's selection description. Subsequent searches
 target the closest eligible argument or ordinary modifier that has not yet
 been placed. Operator particles, predicate heads and material internal to
 a nominal or selected clause are ineligible goals. Each later goal tucks
 in below the already established edge occupants. Thus the sequence of
 movements preserves the residual order rather than reversing it through
 repeated outermost adjunction. Ord is a placement head, without a topic,
 focus, case-valuing or independently scope-taking feature; its movement is
 not uniformly classified as A-movement.

The ordinary edge contains hierarchically ordered specifiers, rather than
 treating all its occupants as equidistant. While the projection of a placement
 head remains open, successive movements satisfying its placement requirement
 insert below earlier edge occupants; a higher head cannot subsequently reopen
 that completed projection. This restricted tucking-in has a formal precedent
 in Richards's treatment of multiple movement \cite[§2]{richards2002}.
 It relaxes root-only structure extension: Chomsky explicitly recognises
 tucking-in as a potential principled departure from the No-Tampering
 Condition \cite[p.~141]{chomsky2008}. The Iridian ordered-edge rule is
 adopted with that qualification, rather than derived from Merge alone.
 Case-independent placement activity is an assumption of the Iridian
 analysis: the stronger inactivity condition on completed Case/Agree chains
 discussed by Chomsky \cite[pp.~150--151]{chomsky2008} is not imposed on
 this placement dependency.

The lower thematic structure is preserved: the lexical patient merges with
 V, the actor in its Voice domain, and applied participants and causees in
 their independently licensed positions. The analysis assigns an ordinary
 placement edge requirement to each actor- or causer-introducing head treated
 as a phase within one finite clause. Before transfer, eligible ordinary
 constituents must be registered at its accessible edge: existing eligible
 specifiers remain accessible, and lower goals move there nearest first,
 tucking beneath the earlier edge occupants. Each search targets the highest
 accessible occurrence of a chain, and a chain already registered at that
 particular edge is not selected again through one of its lower copies.
 This non-pivot-selective preparation preserves the original residual order; it precedes the completed
 orientation--Asp.Align--Mood configuration on which Ord's first search
 depends. Only Ord gives the selected participant priority over that residual
 order. A non-phasal head adds no escape step, and the analysis does not infer
 the phasehood of every argument-building layer from surface order
 (§\,\ref{sec:vm-pivot-derivations}). Neither an intermediate edge occurrence
 nor final placement removes the lower actor occurrence required for binding. Finite CP and nominal boundaries delimit separate searches:
 placement of a selected clause as a whole does not extract its arguments.
 A selected finite \ird{po}/\ird{poby} CP moves as a whole to a
 construction-specific external edge of \synhead{OrdP}, preceding its
 pivot and residual occupants. A separate requirement for selected finite complements
 applies after ordinary placement while OrdP remains open;
 it licenses an outer edge rather than another tucked-in ordinary occupant.
 The complement completes its own local structure independently, and its
 internal arguments are not matrix placement goals. Reduced
 purposive complements retain their position relative to the pivot (§\,\ref{sec:vm-po-comp}). Ordinary
 placement orders their whole constituents within those licensed positions.

The following derivations make the ordering requirements explicit. Bracketed
 role letters designate whole constituents, and $\Pi$ designates the licensed
 particle sequence; they are structural schemata, not additional lexical
 examples. The arrows show successive pronounced edge occupants, with lower
 copies left in the thematic configuration throughout.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2em]
\item[\upshape Nominative-domain transitive.]
 From $[A\ [T\ V]]$, the selected actor moves first; the residual search
 then places $T$ beneath it: $A \longrightarrow A,T\longrightarrow
 A\ T\ \Pi\ V$. For \ird{Marėk travě pjaštíme}, this preserves actor-direct
 and patient-indirect case, with neither participant assigned a topic feature.
\item[\upshape Ergative-domain transitive.]
 From the same thematic configuration, the selected patient $T$ moves
 across the non-selected actor, followed by residual $A$:
 $T\longrightarrow T,A\longrightarrow T\ A\ \Pi\ V$.
 Thus \ird{Tak tóm Marku koupnik} retains patient-direct and
 actor-transitive case. The crossing belongs to the selective first search;
 the residual search cannot independently reverse $T$ and $A$.
\item[\upshape Linked actor topic.]
 After ordinary $T,A$ placement in an ergative transitive, the actor's
 independently licensed aboutness dependency moves it to Spec,\synhead{TopP}:
 $T,A\longrightarrow A_i\ [T\ t_i\ \Pi\ V]$.
 This derives \ird{Marku tak tóm koupnik} without changing the lower
 actor--patient relation or either case (§\,\ref{sec:tc-marked-topics}).
\item[\upshape Additional participant.]
 A simple applicative selects $B$ from the lower $A>B>T$ configuration:
 $B\longrightarrow B,A\longrightarrow B,A,T\longrightarrow
 B\ A\ T\ \Pi\ V$. The existing \ird{Byl Marcí travě pjaštébime}
 has beneficiary-direct, actor-indirect and theme-indirect case. An ergative
 transitive causative instead selects $T$ from $C>E>T$:
 $T\longrightarrow T,C\longrightarrow T,C,E\longrightarrow
 T\ C\ E\ \Pi\ V$, as in \ird{Trava Marku bylam pjášt-r-an-ik}
 (§\,\ref{sec:aspect-caus}). Causee $E$ remains indirect, and the movement
 must pass through the accessible edges of its non-finite Voice domain.
\item[\upshape Manner modification.]
 With lower $[A\ [M\ [T\ V]]]$ and actor selection, the sequence is
 $A\longrightarrow A,M\longrightarrow A,M,T\longrightarrow
 A\ M\ T\ \Pi\ V$. An omitted actor leaves $M\ T\ \Pi\ V$,
 preserving \ird{Skorně travě pjaštíme}. In an ergative clause, selected
 $T$ moves first, after which residual $A$ and $M$ retain their order;
 pivot priority is distinct from the manner--object relation in the
 nominative-domain example.
\end{description}

Only the highest ordinary occurrence is pronounced unless the constituent
 continues to a separately licensed topic or focus position, in which case
 that higher occurrence is pronounced. Lower occurrences remain available
 for the interpretation and licensing specified below. Null arguments supply
 no overt edge occupant. Neither topicless presentation nor quantificational
 reference suppresses ordinary placement. Participant nominals, reduced
 relatives and converbs retain their own selection and placement systems;
 an AspP-selecting construction does not acquire finite Ord, Mood, Pol or
 stance structure through sharing the same participant-selection rules.

\subsection{Scope connectivity}
\label{sec:pivot-scope-connectivity}

A high pronounced argument retains the case and interpretation licensed
 through its lower chain. In \ird{Deno že zastráda}, the lower occurrence of
 the negative indefinite remains in the domain of the single clausal negation
 (§\,\ref{sec:nm-negative-concord}); ordinary placement does not turn it into
 a referential topic. The actor's lower occurrence likewise remains available
 for the established reflexive dependency involving \ird{svem}
 (§\,\ref{sec:nm-pron-refl}). A chain counts as one argument for case:
 neither an additional silent occurrence nor a higher pronounced occurrence
 adds a case competitor, and case is not recalculated from surface order.
 The exclusion of same-chain occurrences from dependent-case competition has
 a precedent in the framework discussed by Baker and Vinokurova
 \cite[§1]{bakervinokurova2010}; it does not make the timing of Case assignment
 invariant across languages or constructions.
 Wh-variables are interpreted at their lower occurrences under Pol, while
 additive and exclusive association accesses the lower argument variable.
 Quantified interpretations retain their independently specified readings;
 ordinary placement alone grants no scope reversal or new binding relation.
 These are interpretation conditions on Iridian ordinary-placement chains,
 rather than consequences of displacement in general. Fox's distinction
 between chain pronunciation and selective interpretation supplies a
 reconstruction model, while his separate treatments of A- and A$'$-chains
 preclude inferring a uniform reconstruction mechanism from high pronunciation
 alone \cite[pp.~157--159, 192--193]{fox1999}. Lower interpretation is retained
 where the particular Iridian dependency requires it, without obligatory
 reconstruction in every respect.

The use of modifier distribution to constrain functional height has a
comparative model in Cinque \cite{cinque1999}, without requiring a dedicated
projection for every Iridian lexical adverb. The position of a pivot relative
to low manner modification, frequency
quantification, phasal particles and comment-domain particles supplies separate
constraints on its structural height. The two bounded exclusive/phasal readings
in §\,\ref{sec:th-exclusive-phasal} already show why overt precedence cannot
serve as a general rule for semantic scope. Those readings and negative-concord licensing constrain chain interpretation
independently of the finite placement edge.

\subsection{Lexical manner and frequency adverbs in the preverbal field}
\label{sec:lexical-adverb-position}\label{sec:lex-adv}

Lexical manner adverbs originate as low adjuncts to \synhead{vP} or the
 outer \synhead{VP}, while frequency modifiers may attach higher within the
 event description. Their ordinary pronounced occurrences occupy the finite
 placement edge with the arguments, preceding the entire particle field.
 Lower copies preserve their event-modifying interpretations; they acquire
 neither topic status nor stance-operator status through displacement.
 In an active nominative-domain transitive, the successive placement steps in
 §\,\ref{sec:pivot-placement} preserve pivot $>$ manner adverb $>$ object,
 with particles following all three.

\noindent With its pivot unexpressed and no overt particles, an intransitive
clause accordingly has the neutral surface order \term{Adverb $>$ Verb}:

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Skorně zemácime.//
\glb quickly run-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{I am running quickly.}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent In an active nominative-domain transitive with an unexpressed
actor and no overt particles, the non-pivot object follows the manner adverb:
\term{Adverb $>$ Object $>$ Verb}. The object retains its lower complement
occurrence for interpretation:

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Skorně travě pjaštíme.//
\glb quickly bread.\Ind{} eat-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{I am eating bread quickly.}//
\endgl
\xe

An overt actor makes the contribution of pivot selection clearer.
The schematic repair examples below keep the technician, pump and manner
constant while changing the aspectual domain:

\pex\label{ex:order-slot-manner-alignment}
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[technician]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}} skorně \textnormal{[[pump]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}} \textnormal{[[repair]]}\textsubscript{\Prog{}}.//
\glb technician.\Dir{} quickly pump.\Ind{} repair-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{A technician is repairing a pump quickly.}
\lingcontext{work is in progress during an interruption to the water supply}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[pump]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}} \textnormal{[[technician]]}\textsubscript{\Trs{}} skorně \textnormal{[[repair]]}\textsubscript{\Trs{}.\Pf{}}.//
\glb pump.\Dir{} technician.\Trs{} quickly repair-\Trs{}-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{A technician repaired a pump quickly.}
\lingcontext{the repair is presented as one completed operation}//
\endgl
\xe

The progressive places the direct actor before the manner expression and
indirect patient. In the perfective, direct patient selection gives the pump
priority, while the residual actor--manner order is preserved. Neither
order makes \ird{skorně} a stance particle, and the pump's initial position
in (b) does not independently establish an aboutness dependency.

\noindent Frequency adverbs such as \ird{ohly} \trsl{sometimes},
\ird{roca} \trsl{often}, \ird{člíně} \trsl{infrequently}, and \ird{sabo}
\trsl{never} belong to the broad preverbal lexical-adverb field. Their event-quantificational
interpretation differs from low manner modification; membership of that field
does not require attachment at the same structural height. Temporal frames
such as \ird{byh} \trsl{yesterday} locate the event in a time interval and
provide a third, distinct comparison. Even so, they are not members of the rigid particle series. More
leftward placement of lexical adverbs is possible under discourse pressure, but
such ordering belongs to marked information structure and should not be taken
as the neutral pattern. Lexical-adverb placement alone does not determine negative licensing
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-negative-concord}).

Korean case-marked adverbials make the difference between an argument's
case and an adjunct's case especially concrete. In Jou's example, both
the duration expression and the book bear accusative morphology, although
only the book is the selected internal argument:

\pex\label{ex:order-comparative-duration}
\a
\begingl
\glpreamble \textit{Korean} \cite[example~1a, p.~2]{jou2026}//
\gla Mina-ka han sikan-ul chayk-ul ilk-ess-ta.//
\glb Mina-\textsc{nom} one hour-\textsc{acc} book-\textsc{acc} read-\textsc{pst}-\textsc{decl}//
\glft \trsl{Mina read a book for one hour.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\glpreamble \textit{Iridian; schematic}//
\gla \textnormal{[[Mina]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}} tomem kářime.//
\glb Mina.\Dir{} book.\Ind{} read-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Mina was reading a book.}
\lingcontext{a point within the same one-hour reading session; duration is supplied by the context}//
\endgl
\xe

The Iridian schema represents the reading event with an ordinary direct
actor and indirect patient; its contextual hour supplies no pronounced
durative nominal and consequently establishes no case rule for one.
Jou's account of the Korean durative and multiplicative patterns correlates
adverbial case with subject position, using negative-concord intervention
and subject--event scope as independent diagnostics
\cite[§§4--6]{jou2026}. In Iridian, ordinary placement preserves lower
argument-case licensing, as the repair pair in
(\ref{ex:order-slot-manner-alignment}) illustrates; the shared Korean
accusative marking belongs to a durative construction, whereas
\ird{skorně} supplies a derived manner expression.

Manner adverbs in \ird{-ě} form an open derived class
(§\,\ref{sec:stative-adv}), whereas the frequency items constitute a lexical
subclass independent of that derivation. Neither belongs to the closed
particle series. Negative-concord temporal \ird{žemě} \trsl{never}
requires local negation (§\,\ref{sec:nm-negative-concord}); the distribution
of a lexical frequency modifier must be distinguished from that licensing
dependency.


\section{Verbal complements and head finality: preverbal position}
\label{sec:vm-comp-headfinal}

Internal nominal arguments and selected clauses originate in the complement domain
of the head-final predicate. Their lower positions determine selection and
interpretation, while the ordinary finite placement dependency determines
pronunciation before the articulated particle field
(§\,\ref{sec:pivot-placement}).

\subsection{The base position of the object}
\label{sec:vm-obj-base}

In a head-final language, the complement of a head $H$ is to the
left of $H$ in the linear string. For the lexical predicate V, its
internal argument (patient \synhead{DP}) is the complement of V and therefore
precedes V in the lower structure:

\begin{center}
\term{[\synhead{VP} [\synhead{DP} patient] V]}
\end{center}

\noindent This is the \emph{base-generated} complement position. Its
preverbal orientation follows from head-finality; its ordinary pronunciation
before particles additionally involves finite ordinary placement. In the Minimalist
framework used here, head-finality is the linearisation convention assigning
$<$Complement, Head$>$ order in the lower predicate. This directly represented
complement order is an architectural assumption; the further position before
particles follows from ordinary placement, rather than from head-finality
alone.

The patient \synhead{DP} is base-generated as the complement of V, before
 the verb. Its direct or indirect case is licensed within the aspectual and
 verbal domain; case assignment does not require movement to
 Spec,\synhead{TopP}. The patient's origin below the actor does not by itself
 derive its neutral position before that actor. Ordinary placement first
 attracts the direct-case pivot and then the eligible residual constituents
 to ordered positions below it in \synhead{OrdP}; its lower occurrence
 preserves the licensed case and thematic relation. This dependency is
 distinct from linked topicalisation, so a pre-particle pivot can lack an
 aboutness interpretation (§\,\ref{sec:tc-quantificational-pivots}).

When an argument is independently selected as a linked topic, an
 A$'$-dependency connects Spec,\synhead{TopP} with that lower position.
 Schematically, with intervening projections suppressed:
\[
 [_{\mathrm{TopP}}\ \mathrm{DP}_{i}\
   [\mathrm{lower\ clause}\ \ldots t_i\ldots V]\ \mathrm{Top}^{0}].
\]
The \synhead{DP} retains its case, so a perfective transitive can have either
 a direct-case patient topic or a transitive-case agent topic. Head-finality
 places complements before V; the topic dependency, when present, places its
 referent above the lower clause. These are distinct sources of preverbal
 order.

Selected finite complements and reduced purposive complements retain their
