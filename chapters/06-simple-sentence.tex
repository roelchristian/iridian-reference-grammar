\chapter{The Simple Sentence}\label{ch:simple}

\section{Theoretical background and analytical conventions}
\label{sec:th-background}

The analyses in Chapters~\ref{ch:simple} and~\ref{ch:complex} adopt a Minimalist
architecture in which clause structure is built by Merge, with the restricted
ordered-edge insertion defined in §\,\ref{sec:pivot-placement}, and interpreted through
hierarchies of lexical and functional projections, and linearized according to
the strongly head-final character of Iridian. Recurrent clause properties are
analysed through a shared structural spine comprising a predicate domain,
inflectional and operator projections, and an articulated left periphery.

Within that architecture, the clause edge comprises an ordered sequence of
projections for topic, focus, ordinary placement, clause typing, evidential
and epistemic operators, and speech-act marking. Topic placement, wh-licensing, polar questions,
complementation, and the order of high particles are analysed through these
positions, with information structure encoded especially in
\synhead{TopP} and \synhead{FocP}.

The morphology discussed in these chapters is interpreted in the same way as
elsewhere in the grammar: broadly within a Distributed Morphology model.
Syntactic structure is built first, and the exponence of heads and feature
bundles is determined at spellout. This matters directly for Iridian because
many contrasts that look at first glance like separate `constructions' are
better treated as alternative realisations of the same structural heads:
Neg\textsuperscript{0} surfaces as \ird{za-}, \ird{staly}, or zero-plus-root
suppletion depending on the host available at PF; converb suffixes realise
feature bundles on non-finite heads; and case morphology spells out configurations established in the syntax.

The central descriptive claim of the two syntax chapters is therefore more
specific than a general appeal to `topic-comment structure'. Iridian clause
structure is organised by a head-final functional sequence in which the
absolutive pivot is strongly privileged for topical interpretation, but topic,
focus, polarity, evidentiality, and clause combining are all represented by
independent structural positions. The split-ergative alignment, the ordering of
particles, the placement of subordinate clauses, the distribution of wh-phrases,
and the complement system are analysed as consequences of that shared
architecture. This section sets out the main information-structural
assumptions and terminological conventions used throughout both chapters; the
clause spine itself is given in Table~\ref{tab:th-clause-spine} of
Chapter~\ref{ch:overview}, and the particle framework is analysed in detail in
§\,\ref{sec:th-particle-framework} below.

\subsection{Topic-comment structure as the primary information-structural
dimension}
\label{sec:th-topic-comment-theory}

The direct-case \term{absolutive pivot} commonly serves as the aboutness topic
 of an Iridian clause, but the two relations are independently licensed.
 Asp.Align and Voice establish its case in the lower clause, while
 Spec,\synhead{TopP} hosts an independently topicalised referent. A
 transitive-case agent can occupy that high position with the patient remaining
 direct, and a quantificational or presentational pivot can remain below it
 without a referential topic interpretation
 (§§\,\ref{sec:tc-marked-topics}, \ref{sec:tc-quantificational-pivots}).

The \term{comment} is the domain interpreted as a predication about the high
 topic, including the focus and lower predicate fields. Logical predication,
 discourse aboutness, and grammatical pivot status may coincide, although none
 is defined by the others (§\,\ref{sec:tc-logical-subject}). The grammar uses
 pivot status for alignment and the relevant clause-linking relations, without
 taking a language-wide subject relation as its primitive. This analytical
 choice does not establish a typological claim of topic prominence by itself.

In perfective transitives with \ird{-n-}, the patient receives direct case and
 the agent transitive case; in imperfective active clauses, the agent receives
 direct case and the patient indirect case. The referential pivot is the
 default aboutness candidate, but the temporal interpretation of aspect is
 preserved when a different participant is topicalised. Anchoring identifies
 a nominal referent independently of both case and topichood.

The neutral order is pivot-initial and verb-final; a high topic precedes the
 lower argument domain when expressed (§\,\ref{sec:word-order}).

\subsection{Head-finality}
\label{sec:th-head-finality}

Iridian is predominantly head-final. Its lexical phrases and lower clauses are
internally verb-final or head-final: arguments precede their predicates,
modifiers precede their heads, adpositions follow their nominal complements (as
postpositions, cf.\ §\,\ref{sec:nm-postpositions}), and subordinate clauses precede
the matrix predicate. This property, which is statable as a general principle of
phrase structure, has several major
consequences that recur throughout the syntactic analysis.

For the clause in isolation, head-finality means that the finite verb is always
clause-final in standard Iridian: it is the head of the highest verbal
projection and accordingly surfaces after all its specifiers and complements.
For clause-combining, it means that subordinate clauses precede the matrix
clause whose head they are complements of (see §\,\ref{sec:th-subord-types}
of Chapter~\ref{ch:complex}). For the particle system, specifier realisation places each particle before
its lower complement, whereas ordinary placement raises arguments and
modifiers across the articulated particle projections
(§\,\ref{sec:pivot-placement}). Their resulting adjacency does not make
the particles one phonological word.

Head-finality also has consequences for relativisation and complementation.
Relative clauses precede the head noun they modify; finite complement clauses
precede the matrix predicate that embeds them; and purpose-clause converbs
precede the verb they are oriented toward. These properties are not stipulated
separately but follow from the single head-final phrase structure combined with
the clause-spine in Table~\ref{tab:th-clause-spine}. The interrogative head
\ird{by} is a local exception: Pol\textsuperscript{0} precedes its
complement. Ordinary placement puts overt arguments and modifiers before
this head, yielding ordinary constituents $>$ \ird{by} $>$ lower
predication. The other particles retain their independently specified
specifier realisations and silent final heads.

\subsection{The particle framework and its integration into the syntax
chapters}
\label{sec:th-particle-framework}

The adverbial particle system, analysed in detail in §\,\ref{sec:adv-particles}
of Chapter~\ref{ch:function-words}, establishes the main empirical evidence for
the left-peripheral structure summarised in Figures~\ref{fig:th-clause-fields}
and \ref{fig:th-left-periphery}. The particles serve here as syntactic
diagnostics: their ordering across structural zones, their scope properties,
and their ability or inability to license fragment answers jointly constrain
the structural analysis. Surface position alone does not
determine every scope relation, particularly the restricted lower exclusive
interpretation described in §\,\ref{sec:th-exclusive-phasal}.

Three adverbial-particle zones and a separate clause-typing position are
 distinguished within the finite clause. Ordinary arguments and modifiers
 precede all three zones and the clause-typing head through the dependency
 specified in §\,\ref{sec:pivot-placement}; independently linked topics and
 marked focus precede that ordinary placement region.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2em, labelwidth=!, labelindent=0em]
\item[\upshape Clause typing (\synhead{PolP}).] The interrogative particle
 \ird{by} realises locally head-initial Pol\textsuperscript{0}, below outer
 evidential and speech-act projections but above the comment operators.
 Its complement follows it, while arguments pronounced at the finite
 placement edge precede it (§\,\ref{sec:by-pol-head}). Pol names the
 interrogative operator of this analysis; its position below marked focus and
 ordinary placement is specific to the Iridian structure. Cartographic Force,
 Fin and Int distinguish other clause-edge functions and positions, including
 an Italian Int above Foc \cite[§§2.2--3.1]{rizzibocci2017}. Those labels do
 not independently establish the position or interpretation of Iridian \ird{by}.
\item[\upshape Zone~\textsc{i} (outer evidential and speech-act).]
 Speech-act particles such as \ird{nadě} and \ird{mlada}, and evidential
 and epistemic particles such as \ird{oče}, \ird{hladě}, \ird{kady} and
 \ird{či}, occupy articulated positions above \synhead{PolP}. Their
 semantic relations to propositions, sources and questions remain distinct.
 Their pronounced positions follow ordinary constituents, including those
 without a topic interpretation.
\item[\upshape Zone~\textsc{ii} (comment-domain).] The Comm-field contains
 additive \ird{och}, affirmative \ird{mó}, exclusive \ird{kamo},
 interim-sequential \ird{dlí}, and reorienting \ird{jas}. Within the
 specified partial order, \ird{och} precedes \ird{jas}, which precedes
 phasal particles; \ird{by} precedes both. Comm names an articulated region,
 rather than a single head or a uniform semantic type. No general ordering
 or co-occurrence rule follows for its other members.
\item[\upshape Zone~\textsc{iii} (phasal).] The phasal particles \ird{vy}
 (\trsl{already}) and \ird{so} (\trsl{still} / \trsl{not yet}) occupy
 \synhead{PhasP} above the polarity-bearing predication: indicative
 \synhead{NegP}, when present, otherwise the viewpoint/lexical predicate
 domain. Their input is a positive or negative proposition or a contextually
 ordered alternative licensed in §\,\ref{sec:class1-particles}; they do not
 replace Asp.Align.
\end{description}

\noindent The ordinary surface order is arguments and modifiers $>$ outer
 evidential/speech-act material $>$ \ird{by} $>$ \ird{och} $>$ \ird{jas}
 $>$ \ird{vy}/\ird{so} $>$ verbal predicate. This sequence states the
 relations among named positions, without licensing every combination or
 settling the order of every stance and comment operator. Arguments do not
 interrupt the ordinary particle field. Clause-final particles retain their
 utterance-edge positions, while nonverbal predication has the independently
 specified structures described below.

The ordering is therefore a partial ordering over independently licensed
positions, rather than a template into which every member of a semantic class
can be inserted. Within a shared field, complementary occupation of one
position, distinct ordered subpositions, and restricted adjunction would make
different predictions about co-occurrence and order; the unspecified Iridian
pairs do not yet distinguish these analyses. The exclusion of \ird{vy so}
and \ird{so vy} follows from their established competition for one phasal
position, whereas no corresponding single-occupant rule has been established
for the Comm-field or outer \synhead{EvidP}. Conversely, the compatibility of
two independently described meanings does not establish their joint syntactic
licensing. Pairwise licensing alone does not establish a sequence of three
particles.

Interpretation further depends on the object to which each contribution
applies. A phasal operator relates a polarised predication or another licensed
input to preceding and reference stages; a focus-associated operator compares
alternatives whose domain and associate must be identified; an evidential or
stance particle relates its content to a source, a perspective holder, or a
discourse relation. Identifying the source of a report separately from the
holder of an assessment prevents a single perspective from being assigned to
several operators merely because they share a field. Tagalog evidential
combinations illustrate the relevance of this distinction
\cite[§3.3.2.2]{tanalmazan2019}. Likewise, a proposition, a
question concerning that proposition, and the act of asking that question
are distinct possible objects of qualification. The upper-peripheral position
of an Iridian particle establishes its general relation to the lower clause
without, by itself, choosing among these more specific objects or supplying
an otherwise unspecified source or holder. The distinction between a local
syntactic domain and the attribution of its content is developed for embedded
clauses in §\,\ref{sec:particle-domains-attribution}.

Semantic scope must also be distinguished from the projection of background
content. When a phasal or additive background remains available under
interrogation, that persistence does not itself show that its introducing
particle takes scope over the question. The at-issue contribution and the
background conditions must be traced separately through the composition,
with the relevant stages and alternatives held constant. Hsieh and Erlewine's
Tagalog diagnostics illustrate both the value of this separation and its
limits: the projected presuppositions of \textit{na rin} can leave competing
scope analyses indistinguishable \cite[appendix~A]{hsieherlewine2024}.
Iridian's specified exclusive--phasal rules below consequently distinguish
interpretations by their complete profiles, rather than deriving scope from
linear precedence or from background persistence alone.

The renewed-occurrence construction with reorienting \ird{jas} and
phasal \ird{vy} likewise has a specified background: earlier validity of
the polarised predication precedes the contrary stage against which its
current attainment is asserted (§\,\ref{sec:class2-particles}). That
three-stage profile supplements ordinary \ird{vy}; neither the structural
height of \ird{jas} nor its discourse-reorienting contribution alone
introduces the earlier occurrence. The construction retains the
distinct positions of \ird{jas} above \ird{vy}, with ordinary constituents
preceding both. Its declarative profile does
not specify projection of the earlier-occurrence condition through Mood,
interrogation, or clausal embedding.


\subsection{Phasal scope and focus-associated exclusivity}
\label{sec:th-exclusive-phasal}

The lower interpretive order is comment-domain operators $>$ phasal operator
$>$ polarised predication $>$ viewpoint aspect and the inner lexical predicate. Thus the
ordinary negative combinations are $\mathrm{VY}(\neg P)$ and
$\mathrm{SO}(\neg P)$, rather than negation of an already/still proposition.
The distinction is independent of whether negation is pronounced as a prefix,
as existential suppletion, or as clause-final \ird{staly}. The relation between
phasal interpretation and the realisation of finite heads is compositional,
not a requirement to reorder the verbal suffixes.

In negative non-indicatives, a silent interpretable $\mathrm{Op}_{\neg}$
applies to the AspP proposition below Phas, while the higher Mood head bears
its concord feature and selects the negative suffix. The clause-local
licensing and composition are specified in §\,\ref{sec:vm-moodp}:
$M(C(\mathrm{PHAS}(\neg P)))$. Thus a prohibitive with \ird{so} calls for
continued restraint; it does not compose as $\neg\mathrm{SO}(P)$ and does
not introduce indicative NegP or \ird{za-}. The suffix's pronounced position
is not its semantic scope. The same lower polarity input is used under
hortative and irrealis Mood.

Focus-associated \ird{kamo} has two specifically licensed
compositions with \ird{vy}/\ird{so}. Its ordinary comment-domain interpretation
is $\mathrm{EXCL}_{A}(\mathrm{PHAS}(P))$, where $A$ is the contextually fixed
set of focus alternatives. A lower interpretation
$\mathrm{PHAS}(\mathrm{EXCL}_{A}(P))$ is available when that entire exclusive
proposition supplies the attained or continuing state. The focus associate
and alternative set must remain constant across the relevant stages.
Precisely, let $p_x(t)$ be the proposition with the focused associate replaced
by $x$, with actual associate $a\in A$ and all other material fixed.
For argument focus, $x$ ranges over individuals; for VP focus it ranges over
contextually comparable actions, with their participants held fixed. For
truth-valued alternatives $q_x$, write
$E_A(q)=q_a\wedge\bigwedge_{b\in A\setminus\{a\}}\neg q_b$.
The bounded lower rule constructs the stagewise proposition
$q(t)=E_A(x\mapsto p_x(t))$ and supplies that proposition to PHAS.
If the prejacent is negative, $p_x(t)$ already contains the one licensed
negation; the rule neither duplicates it nor moves it over PHAS.

In the ordinary high reading, exclusivity instead compares complete phasal
profiles. With the same reference stage $r$ and preceding stage $r^{-}$ for
all alternatives, define the truth-valued profile predicates
\[
\begin{aligned}
h_{\mathrm{VY}}(x)&=\neg p_x(r^{-})\wedge p_x(r),\\
h_{\mathrm{SO}}(x)&=p_x(r^{-})\wedge p_x(r).
\end{aligned}
\]
The high construction requires $h_{\mathrm{PHAS}}(a)$ and excludes
$h_{\mathrm{PHAS}}(b)$ for every distinct $b\in A$. The actual associate
retains its presupposed preceding-stage background, while each excluded
alternative is tested for satisfaction of the complete two-stage profile.
An alternative that lacks the relevant preceding state therefore fails that
profile without making the high exclusive construction undefined. Thus a
newly opened gate does not satisfy the profile of continued openness,
while a gate that was already open does not satisfy the attainment profile. The scope notation
$\mathrm{EXCL}_{A}(\mathrm{PHAS}(P))$ abbreviates this construction-specific
comparison; it does not negate a partial phasal denotation whose
presupposition has failed. These profile predicates formalise the ordinary
two-stage approximation, without replacing the phasal presupposition of the
actual associate or extending the rule to scalar constructions. For a negative
prejacent, $p_x$ remains the already polarised proposition in both profiles.

Only the \ird{kamo}--\ird{vy}/\ird{so} construction licenses the
lower exclusive composition, within one finite domain and without intervening Mood or
clausal evidentiality. It does not license lowering \ird{och}, \ird{mó},
\ird{dlí}, or an arbitrary sequence of comment operators.

The particle remains pronounced in the Comm-field above the phasal particle.
A topicalised focus associate binds its lower argument
variable $x$ (equivalently, its interpreted lower copy supplies that slot);
the pronounced topic itself is not moved into the phasal complement at LF.
Focus association requires a contrastive set containing that referent, even
when the same nominal is the aboutness topic. The lower rule reconstructs the
exclusive proposition, not all high material. These two rules are specified
Iridian conventions. Erlewine's lower-copy mechanism concerns English
\textit{even}; his discussion explicitly does not extend that mechanism to
English \textit{only} \cite[p.~863, fn.~9]{erlewine2016copies}. It therefore
neither derives Iridian exclusivity nor establishes backward association to a
topic. Here topic association is explicitly variable binding, and the lower
alternative-proposition rule is stipulated for the Iridian construction.

The following English schemata specify interpretations and discourse
conditions; they supply neither Iridian surface forms nor morphological
analyses.

\pex
\a \trsl{Only the western gate is still open.}
\lingcontext{$\mathrm{EXCL}_{A}(\mathrm{SO}(P))$. The western and eastern
 gates were open yesterday; the eastern gate has closed, while a third gate
 has just opened. Only the western gate satisfies the continuing profile.}
\a \trsl{It is still only the western gate that is open.}
\lingcontext{$\mathrm{SO}(\mathrm{EXCL}_{A}(P))$. The western gate was the
 sole open gate yesterday and remains the sole open gate today.}
\xe

The lower reading requires prior exclusivity, whereas the high reading
requires the western gate to be the sole continuing member of the earlier
set of open gates; a newly opened alternative need not have been open before.
With the same gate alternatives at both stages,
$\mathrm{SO}(\mathrm{EXCL}_{A}(P))$ entails
$\mathrm{EXCL}_{A}(\mathrm{SO}(P))$, although the converse fails. The
first context therefore excludes the lower reading, while the second supports
it without independently ruling out the high reading. The contrast concerns
the stronger requirement of continuing exclusivity, rather than two
mutually exclusive contextual distributions. The corresponding candle
schemata, \trsl{Only the oldest candle is still burning} and
\trsl{It is still only the oldest candle that is burning}, admit the same
comparison: a newly lit candle is compatible with exclusivity of continued
burning, whereas another current flame contradicts continuing exclusivity.

\paragraph{Scalar alternatives and low progress.}
\ird{Kamo} also bounds alternatives ordered by degree or contextual rank,
with the MIN/MAX interpretation defined in §\,\ref{sec:class2-particles}.
The Boolean $E_A$ above is a satisfaction diagnostic for the ordinary
individual- or action-exclusive cases, in which the prejacent is true and
all distinct relevant alternatives are false; it does not define every
scalar use or identify the asserted and presupposed contributions. In
\trsl{The child can read only the simplest passages}, the relevant alternatives
concern attainable difficulty levels under the ability modal. Several easy
passages may be readable, since the upper bound excludes ability beyond the
specified level rather than every other passage. This is a schematic
interpretation of scalar \ird{kamo}; the syntax of its association within
an overt modal construction requires the independently licensed modal
structure. Contextual ordering, rather than an unrestricted choice of
lexical senses, distinguishes this use from individual exclusivity
\cite[§2.3]{coppockbeaver2014}.

Low-progress \ird{kamo so} has a narrower interpretation than persistence
of an entire exclusive proposition. It describes an attained stage below a
salient expected goal while an upper bound continues from an earlier stage.
The lower bound is evaluated at the reference stage, so an earlier stage may
fall below it. This construction extends the ordinary lower interpretation
within the same finite phasal domain; it does not make every scalar use of
\ird{so} interchangeable with temporal persistence. The separation of
current attainment from a continuing upper bound follows the comparative
analysis of Tagalog \textit{pa lang}, while its licensing in Iridian is a
constructional convention \cite[§4]{hsieherlewine2023nthu}.

Let $d(t)$ measure cumulative attainment on a contextually ordered scale,
$k$ be the designated attained threshold, $k_{\mathrm{next}}$ the next
relevant higher threshold, and $g_C$ a salient expected goal. For
$r^{-}<r$, the specialised declarative profile is:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{current lower bound (MIN):}\quad & d(r)\geq k,\\
\text{asserted upper bound (MAX):}\quad & d(r)<k_{\mathrm{next}},\\
\text{preceding upper bound:}\quad & d(r^{-})<k_{\mathrm{next}},\\
\text{non-vacuous progression:}\quad & d(r^{-})<d(r).
\end{aligned}
\]
The current lower bound and preceding upper bound are background conditions;
the progression condition restricts this construction to an actual advance
between the chosen stages. The ordering must make $k<g_C$ contextually
salient, without entailing that the goal will be reached or that the speaker
regrets the limited progress. On a dense scale without a next threshold,
MAX instead requires that every true relevant attainment alternative be
ranked no higher than $k$. These conditions leave ordinary \ird{so} and
ordinary persistence of exclusivity unchanged.

\pex
\a \trsl{The scribe has copied only three folios so far.}
\lingcontext{One folio was complete at the earlier stage; three are complete
 now, against an expected twenty. The continuing upper bound is fewer than
 four completed folios; attainment of three is required only now.}
\a \trsl{The climbers have reached only the lower ridge so far.}
\lingcontext{The party has advanced from the trailhead to the lower ridge;
 the upper ridge and summit remain higher attainable milestones.}
\xe

The scribe's earlier stage satisfies MAX but not the current MIN of three;
requiring the entire MIN/MAX package to hold earlier would therefore reject
this progression. If three folios had already been complete and no further
progress had occurred, ordinary persistence of exclusivity could hold,
although the non-vacuous low-progress profile would fail. Cardinal quantities
and ordered geographical milestones consequently instantiate the same
construction without requiring the scale to count objects. Temporal focus
particles can impose distinct requirements on their alternative structures,
as the Dutch comparison shows \cite[§§2--3]{neelemanvandekoot2022}. In the additional
schema \trsl{The floodwater has risen only to the second step so far}, limited
progress may be reassuring; disappointment is no part of the upper bound.

Temporal \ird{so} in these profiles differs from scalar-additive \ird{so}
\trsl{even}, whose alternatives require addition and a contextual ordering
(§\,\ref{sec:class1-particles}). An exclusive upper bound and an additive
witness cannot be imposed indiscriminately on the same alternatives. In
\trsl{Even the master mason repaired merely the doorway}, the intended
scales concern the actor and the extent of the repairs respectively:
$C_{\mathrm{so}}$ and $C_{\mathrm{kamo}}$ must be specified separately.
This is a diagnostic schema, not an additional licensed Iridian particle
combination. Likewise, \trsl{Only the harbourmaster received another warning}
tests exclusivity over event addition, while \trsl{Only one bell has stopped
ringing already} tests a change-of-state prejacent under \ird{vy}; neither
English schema settles an unspecified Iridian co-occurrence or association
rule.

MIN, MAX and phasal backgrounds must be tracked separately under \ird{by},
negative Mood and epistemic qualification. The declarative low-progress
profile does not determine whether its progression condition projects under
those operators. Questioning MAX while retaining MIN and negating MAX while
retaining MIN are distinct formal diagnostics; their availability does not
establish those projection patterns as Iridian rules. The following bounded
paradigm retains the established polarity and phasal conventions, while the
extended semantic diagnostics distinguish lower-bound failure, upper-bound
failure and absence of progression.


\subsection{A bounded polarity--phase--focus diagnostic paradigm}
\label{sec:th-polarity-diagnostics}

The following constructed paradigms specify Iridian design judgements, not
attested speaker responses. They hold the ordinary two-stage interpretation
fixed. Write $V=\mathrm{VY}$, $S=\mathrm{SO}$,
$A=\mathrm{ADD}_A$ for additive \ird{och}, and $E=\mathrm{EXCL}_A$ for
\ird{kamo}; $A$ in the subscript denotes a fixed alternative set.
$A(q)$ asserts $q_a$ and presupposes some distinct $q_b$, whereas $E(q)$
checks that $q_a$ is true and every distinct $q_b$ in that set is false.
This satisfaction check abstracts away from the MIN/MAX division of content. In the tables,
$E(V(P))$ and $E(S(P))$, including negative prejacents and embedding under
Mood, abbreviate the high profile comparison defined in
§\,\ref{sec:th-exclusive-phasal}; $E(P)$ inside $V$ or $S$ denotes
stagewise truth-valued exclusivity. Additivity here associates with the whole phasal proposition, not an independently lowered
operator. Its additional alternative must satisfy both the preceding-stage
background and the reference-stage condition of that phasal profile; an
alternative whose background fails cannot supply the additive presupposition.
The associate is \ird{Marek} in Table~\ref{tab:th-indicative-diagnostics}:
$p_x(t)$ means that $x$ is sleeping at stage $t$. The topic binds its lower
argument variable as in §\,\ref{sec:th-exclusive-phasal}.

Here L means licensed with its stated background, not felicitous in every
context. For each asserted alternative, $V(P)$ requires non-sleeping before
and sleeping now; $S(P)$ requires sleeping at both stages; $V(\neg P)$
requires sleeping before and non-sleeping now; $S(\neg P)$ requires
non-sleeping at both stages. In an \ird{och} row at least one other person
must satisfy the same complete phasal description; in a high \ird{kamo} row
none may do so. A background mismatch for the actual associate, or the
absence of a suitable additive witness in an \ird{och} row, makes the
intended ordinary reading unavailable; an excluded exclusive alternative
need not satisfy that background. For the \ird{kamo} rows only, the alternative lower reading is
$V(E(P))$, $S(E(P))$, $V(E(\neg P))$, or $S(E(\neg P))$, respectively,
with the entire exclusive proposition evaluated at both stages. This is
licensed only by the bounded rule above. In particular, only Marek being
\emph{still not asleep} need not imply that everyone else is asleep now;
\emph{still only Marek not asleep} does imply that, and also prior exclusivity.

For the matched negative non-indicatives below, $P$ is eating with agent and
food fixed by context. Focus is the VP \trsl{eat}, with a fixed set of
alternative actions such as eating and drinking; it is not a free change of
subject or food between stages. These bare clauses permit contextually
understood participants. The morphology is \ird{piašt-é} (eat-\Proh{}),
\ird{piašt-ku} (eat-\Neg{}\Hort{}), and
\ird{piašt-ir} (eat-\Neg{}\Irr{}). Let $D$, $H$, and $R$ stand for
imperative, hortative, and irrealis force respectively. The evaluation stages
belong to the course of action under direction/proposal, or to the relevant
hypothetical situations under irrealis; these clauses do not assert an actual
prior episode of eating.

\ird{Och vy piaštir.} & $R(A(V(\neg P)))$ & L \\
\ird{Och so piaštir.} & $R(A(S(\neg P)))$ & L \\
\ird{Kamo vy piaštir.} & $R(E(V(\neg P)))$ & L \\
\ird{Kamo so piaštir.} & $R(E(S(\neg P)))$ & L \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The \ird{vy} rows urge, propose, or hypothetically describe cessation of
eating; the \ird{so} rows continued non-eating. M means licensed but
pragmatically marked: a cessation directive or proposal needs an established
course to stop, rather than a neutral preventative setting. It becomes
appropriate with an instruction such as \trsl{From now on, stop eating too}
for additive \ird{vy}, or \trsl{Only stop eating now; continue the other
activities} for high exclusive \ird{vy}. For \ird{so}, corresponding contexts
are \trsl{Continue refraining from eating too} and \trsl{Only continue
refraining from eating}; the additive or exclusive comparison is with the same
other actions. All twelve rows inherit the stage and alternative requirements
above, interpreted inside $D/H/R$. The lower exclusive option, when its
stagewise background is satisfied, is $M(V(E(\neg P)))$ or
$M(S(E(\neg P)))$ for $M\in\{D,H,R\}$.

Excluded analyses are $\neg M(C(\mathrm{PHAS}(P)))$ and
$M(C(\neg\mathrm{PHAS}(P)))$ as readings of these negative Mood patterns:
they place the single negation outside its licensed input position.
\ird{*Och so zapiašté}, \ird{*Och so zapiaštku}, and
\ird{*Och so zapiaštir} are excluded attempts to add indicative negation to
negative Mood. Lowering additive \ird{och} to derive
$M(\mathrm{PHAS}(A(\neg P)))$ is likewise excluded by the present rules.
No change to the established surface order, aspect quantity, or negative
suffix paradigms follows from these diagnostics.

\section{The left periphery: topics, operators, and clause typing}
\label{sec:th-left-periphery}

\subsection{Topic and topic drop}
\label{sec:th-topic}

The specifier of \synhead{TopP} hosts a clause-internal aboutness topic,
 whose case is retained from its lower argument position. The direct-case
 pivot supplies the neutral topic candidate; a non-direct topic is also
 possible, while initial quantificational pivots retain lower interpretation
 through their ordinary placement chains
 (§\,\ref{sec:tc-quantificational-pivots}). A continuing topic may be
 unexpressed when its referent is recoverable. Overt pronouns support
 disambiguation, reintroduction, or contrast according to context.

When the topic and other ordinary constituents are unexpressed,
Zone~\textsc{i} particles can begin the overt string. Any remaining overt
arguments or modifiers precede them through ordinary placement; omission
does not relocate the particles
(§\,\ref{sec:adv-particles}).

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Janek zdrávime.//
\glb Janek.\Dir{} sleep-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek is sleeping.} \lingcontext{canonical topic-initial}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Zdrávime.//
\glb sleep-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{[He] is sleeping.} \lingcontext{topic drop; referent established}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Oče zdrávime.//
\glb \textsc{mir} sleep-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Oh, [he] is sleeping!} \lingcontext{topic drop with upper-peripheral mirative; no overt topic}//
\endgl
\xe

\subsection{Focus and focus movement}
\label{sec:th-focus}

Information focus may receive ordinary placement within the comment. The question under
 discussion determines whether a nominal, the verb, or a larger predicate
 supplies the relevant alternatives; the rightmost ordinary nominal is
 therefore not invariably the sole focus. Marked displacement is analysed as
 movement to Spec,\synhead{FocP}, above \synhead{OrdP} and the particle field, and is
 optional in wh-questions (§\,\ref{sec:th-wh-interrogation}).

A prominent nominal need not have undergone marked focus movement.
 Identically ordered strings distinguished only by anchoring do not diagnose
 focus movement (§\,\ref{sec:tc-focus-contrast}). Neither ordinary focus nor
 its association with \synhead{FocP} establishes an exhaustive reading without
 an independent semantic or contextual diagnostic. Clause typing comes from
 Pol\textsuperscript{0}, overt \ird{by} or its null counterpart, rather than
 from movement to the focus position.

\subsection{The clause-typing operator \ird{by} and matrix interrogatives}
\label{sec:th-polar-questions}

Matrix interrogatives project \synhead{PolP}. Pol\textsuperscript{0} may be
spelled out overtly as \ird{by} or left phonologically null. In polar questions,
overt \ird{by} is the ordinary and unmarked explicit strategy. In content
questions, both overt \ird{by} and null Pol\textsuperscript{0} are standardly
available: a wh-question may appear with or without \ird{by}, and the choice
reflects the speaker's discourse position rather than a grammatical asymmetry
between question types. The full discussion of the overt/null alternation in
content questions is in §\,\ref{sec:th-wh-interrogation}; the present subsection
focuses on the clause-typing function of \ird{by} itself and on the properties
shared across all matrix interrogatives (§\,\ref{sec:by-pol-head}).

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek by zdrávime?//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} \textsc{q} sleep-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Is Marek sleeping?}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Janek by vy ščének?//
\glb Janek.\Dir{} \textsc{q} already arrive-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Has Janek already arrived?}//
\endgl
\xe

Zone~\textsc{i} particles follow ordinary constituents in questions,
with \ird{by} typing the lower clause as interrogative. Mirative \ird{oče}
supplies the surprise or counterexpectational framing illustrated below,
whereas reportative \ird{či} supplies attributed or relayed framing. Their
positions above \synhead{PolP} represent that general relation, but do not
independently determine whether surprise concerns a possible answer, an
earlier expectation, or the occasion for asking, nor whether a reportative
question relays someone else's question or seeks an answer supported by a
report. Their placement establishes the high framing without
specifying the distribution of all these more particular readings.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Tak tóm Janku oče by vy koupnik?//
\glb this book.\Dir{} Janek.\Trs{} \textsc{mir} \textsc{q} already buy-\Trs{}-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Has Janek, surprisingly, already bought this book?}//
\endgl
\xe

The translation illustrates surprise associated with Janek's possible
purchase, while leaving the purchase itself at issue; it does not assert that
he bought the book. No further reading follows simply from the relative
pronunciation of \ird{oče} and \ird{by}. This distinction matters in the
Tagalog comparison: AnderBois analyses interrogative \textit{pala} as a
revelation about performing the question update, including remembering an
intention to ask or recognising the question's relevance
\cite[§5.2]{anderbois2023pala}, while Tan Almazán distinguishes reported
questions from questions anticipating reportative evidence for an answer
with \textit{daw} \cite[§4.3.4]{tanalmazan2019}. These findings identify
different interpretive dependencies to be distinguished; they do not assign
the Tagalog readings to Iridian \ird{oče} or \ird{či}.

A constituent may be fronted to Spec,\synhead{FocP} or set as a topic in
Spec,\synhead{TopP} inside a polar question, while \ird{by} in
Pol\textsuperscript{0} continues to type the clause as interrogative. The
fronting of a non-wh constituent inside a polar question is not a special
interrogative mechanism but ordinary information structure operating inside an
interrogative clause. A direct-case topic may remain in Spec,\synhead{TopP}; a
narrow-focus non-wh constituent may move to Spec,\synhead{FocP}; either
operation is independent of the interrogative clause typing supplied by
\ird{by}, whose operator supplies interrogative force independently of the
position chosen for the argument or adjunct.


\section{Content questions and \textit{wh}-interrogation}
\label{sec:th-wh-interrogation}

\subsection{Licensing of content questions: \synhead{PolP} and wh-operators}
\label{sec:th-single-wh}

Content questions combine interrogative clause typing with a wh-expression
whose lower occurrence supplies a variable in the same finite domain. In this
analysis, interrogative Pol binds that variable; neither ordinary placement
nor dedicated wh-fronting supplies interrogative force by itself.
\textsc{Pol}\textsuperscript{0} may be phonologically null in neutral content
questions, while remaining the clause-typing head.

In neutral content questions, wh-expressions undergo ordinary placement
 with the other arguments and modifiers, without dedicated wh-fronting.
 Their lower chain occurrences supply interrogative variables within the
 scope of Pol. A single wh-expression may additionally move to
 Spec,\synhead{FocP}, at the left edge of the ordinary placement field,
 when singled out against a salient frame or assigned contrast, insistence
 or other discourse prominence. At most one marked wh-fronting dependency
 is licensed per clause; ordinary placement of several wh-expressions does
 not constitute multiple marked frontings.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek jena mulša?//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} where live-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Where does Marek live?} \lingcontext{neutral; without dedicated wh-fronting}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Jena Marek mulša?//
\glb where Marek.\Dir{} live-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Where does Marek live?} \lingcontext{marked; wh to \synhead{SpecFocP} for discourse prominence}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Tak tóm jeda koupnik?//
\glb this book.\Dir{} who buy-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Who bought this book?} \lingcontext{neutral; without dedicated wh-fronting}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Jeda tak tóm koupnik?//
\glb who this book.\Dir{} buy-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Who bought this book?} \lingcontext{marked; wh to SpecFocP}//
\endgl
\xe

A fronted wh-phrase does not itself license interrogative force; its movement
signals discourse prominence, contrast, or singled-out status of the
wh-variable. The interrogative clause type is established by the \synhead{PolP}
layer independently of whether any fronting has occurred. A content question is
therefore structurally defined by the conjunction of an interrogative operator
in \synhead{PolP} and a wh-expression interpreted within its scope, not by the position
of the wh-phrase at the surface.

Matrix content questions permit both overt and null Pol\textsuperscript{0}, and
these are \emph{both} standard options, not a default with an emphatic variant.
The two differ in discourse profile. A content question with null
Pol\textsuperscript{0} is the structurally lighter form, appropriately neutral
in ordinary information-seeking contexts. A content question with overt \ird{by}
is more explicitly typed as a direct question: it is more interactionally
foregrounded, conversationally engaged, or oriented toward a specific addressee
in the current exchange. The difference is a pragmatic tendency, not a semantic
or grammatical opposition. Both forms are equally grammatical in standard
Iridian.

Both relative orders of Marek and the wh-phrase occur with null and overt
Pol\textsuperscript{0}. The resulting four combinations distinguish the
explicitness of clause typing from the discourse prominence of the wh-phrase.
The paradigm distinguishes the two choices in the surface patterns.

The following paradigm distinguishes ordinary placement from marked
wh-fronting, with both overt and null Pol:

\pex\label{ex:wh-four-combinations}
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek jena mulša?//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} where live-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Where does Marek live?} \lingcontext{null Pol; without dedicated wh-fronting: neutral}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek jena by mulša?//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} where \textsc{q} live-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Where does Marek live?} \lingcontext{overt \ird{by}; without dedicated wh-fronting: explicit, interactionally foregrounded}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Jena Marek mulša?//
\glb where Marek.\Dir{} live-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Where does Marek live?} \lingcontext{null Pol; wh fronted: wh-phrase discourse-prominent}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Jena Marek by mulša?//
\glb where Marek.\Dir{} \textsc{q} live-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Where does Marek live?} \lingcontext{overt \ird{by}; wh fronted: both clause and wh-phrase foregrounded}//
\endgl
\xe

The same four-way system extends to transitive and oblique content
questions. The wh-word type (agent, patient, location, instrument, and so on)
does not restrict the availability of any combination; all four remain possible
across the full inventory of wh-operators in Table~\ref{tab:nm-interrogatives}.

Zone~\textsc{i} particles follow ordinary and marked wh-expressions,
 although their operator positions remain above Pol:

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek jena hladě mulša?//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} where \textsc{infr} live-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Where would Marek presumably be living?}\\
      \lingcontext{\ird{hladě} in \synhead{EvidP} above \synhead{PolP}; without dedicated wh-fronting}//
\endgl
\xe

The inferential framing leaves Marek's location unresolved. Neither the
position of \ird{hladě} nor its English paraphrase independently
specifies whose evidence would support an answer; that dependency remains
distinct from the particle's established upper-peripheral placement, as in
the polar-question discussion in §\,\ref{sec:th-polar-questions}.

\subsection{Multiple \textit{wh} and ordinary placement}
\label{sec:th-multiple-wh}

When the clause contains more than one wh-phrase, the ordinary pattern is for
all wh-phrases to undergo ordinary placement without dedicated wh-fronting.
At most one wh-phrase may undergo marked fronting,
and only when that phrase is assigned specially marked focus
within the pair-list interpretation. The remaining wh-phrases are interpreted as
interrogative variables through their lower chain occurrences, licensed by the clause-level
interrogative operator (overt \ird{by} or a null \synhead{PolP} head).

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Jede ježe koupnik?//
\glb who what buy-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Who bought what?}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Jede jena mulša?//
\glb who where live-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Who lives where?}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent In these multiple-wh clauses neither phrase undergoes marked
wh-fronting; their order does not by itself establish their exact positions
within the lower clause. Their lower occurrences are bound by the null interrogative
operator in \synhead{PolP}. The licensing principle relates a wh-expression's eligible lower occurrence
within its own finite domain to interrogative Pol; ordinary or marked
pronunciation above Pol does not sever that relation. It supplies no
independent licence for a dependency across a finite or nominal boundary.
Overt fronting marks the phrase as the salient or
foregrounded variable in the pair-list; its landing site is Spec,\synhead{FocP}, above \synhead{OrdP}, as
illustrated in \ref{ex:wh-four-combinations}. Fronting does not itself establish the
interrogative type of the clause, which is always a function of \synhead{PolP}.

\subsection{Tag, echo, and indirect questions}
\label{sec:th-tag-echo}

For indirect questions embedded under \ird{poby}, see §\,\ref{sec:poby}. For
embedded content questions in \ird{poby}-complements, the same distributional
principle applies as in matrix clauses: the embedded wh-phrase may undergo ordinary placement
(the neutral option) or additional marked movement to the embedded focus edge
for marked focus. Wh-fronting within the embedded clause's own periphery is
therefore not obligatory; it signals the same kind of discourse prominence as
fronting in matrix questions. The \ird{poby} complementizer marks the embedded
clause as a non-assertive complement regardless of whether the internal
wh-phrase has fronted. The full analysis of \ird{poby} and its complement types
is in §\,\ref{sec:poby}.

\section{Word order}\label{sec:word-order}

The traditional descriptive shorthand for Iridian word order is \term{SOV}, but
that label should not be mistaken for the structural primitive of the clause. A
more faithful characterisation is \term{pivot-initial, verb-final}, or more
specifically \term{absolutive-first, verb-final}. The finite verb itself is
rigidly clause-final; the ordering of preverbal nominals is governed by
information structure and alignment rather than by a fixed subject-object
template.

In ordinary active nominative-domain clauses, the agent is the absolutive
pivot, so this pattern resembles SOV. In ordinary ergative transitives,
the same template yields a patient-initial order of the type
\term{absolutive $>$ ergative $>$ verb}. Without a distinct high topic, the neutral order is therefore
`pivot before the remaining arguments, with the finite verb last'.

\subsection{Participant selection and finite placement}
\label{sec:pivot-placement}

The direct-case pivot is identified by its case and the construction's
participant-selection rules, independently of its pronounced position.
Orientation, aspect and valency determine which participant is selected;
directive Mood can override ordinary indicative selection
(§§\,\ref{sec:vm-asp}, \ref{sec:aspect-caus}). Selection, case licensing and
placement are distinct dependencies. A relation established by Agree need not
involve displacement, and an exponent inserted at PF cannot retrospectively
choose a nominal for movement in the syntax.

Ordinary finite arguments and modifiers are pronounced at a clause-local
 placement edge, represented here as \synhead{OrdP}, above the particle
 projections. Its silent head bears an ordered set of placement requirements:
 first the participant selected by the completed Voice--Asp.Align--Mood
 configuration, then the remaining eligible constituents in their lower
 structural order. Each eligible chain bears a placement feature distinct
 from its Case and agreement features, so Case licensing does not discharge
 its placement requirement. The initial search restricts that feature to the
 chain identified by participant selection; the residual searches target
 the remaining chains. Ord discharges the feature at the final ordinary
 edge, while intermediate phase edges only register the chain's accessibility.
 A closer non-selected argument cannot intervene in the first search because
 it does not satisfy that search's selection description. Subsequent searches
 target the closest eligible argument or ordinary modifier that has not yet
 been placed. Operator particles, predicate heads and material internal to
 a nominal or selected clause are ineligible goals. Each later goal tucks
 in below the already established edge occupants. Thus the sequence of
 movements preserves the residual order rather than reversing it through
 repeated outermost adjunction. Ord is a placement head, without a topic,
 focus, case-valuing or independently scope-taking feature; its movement is
 not uniformly classified as A-movement.

The ordinary edge contains hierarchically ordered specifiers, rather than
 treating all its occupants as equidistant. While the projection of a placement
 head remains open, successive movements satisfying its placement requirement
 insert below earlier edge occupants; a higher head cannot subsequently reopen
 that completed projection. This restricted tucking-in has a formal precedent
 in Richards's treatment of multiple movement \cite[§2]{richards2002}.
 It relaxes root-only structure extension: Chomsky explicitly recognises
 tucking-in as a potential principled departure from the No-Tampering
 Condition \cite[p.~141]{chomsky2008}. The Iridian ordered-edge rule is
 adopted with that qualification, rather than derived from Merge alone.
 Case-independent placement activity is an assumption of the Iridian
 analysis: the stronger inactivity condition on completed Case/Agree chains
 discussed by Chomsky \cite[pp.~150--151]{chomsky2008} is not imposed on
 this placement dependency.

The lower thematic structure is preserved: the lexical patient merges with
 V, the actor in its Voice domain, and applied participants and causees in
 their independently licensed positions. The analysis assigns an ordinary
 placement edge requirement to each actor- or causer-introducing head treated
 as a phase within one finite clause. Before transfer, eligible ordinary
 constituents must be registered at its accessible edge: existing eligible
 specifiers remain accessible, and lower goals move there nearest first,
 tucking beneath the earlier edge occupants. Each search targets the highest
 accessible occurrence of a chain, and a chain already registered at that
 particular edge is not selected again through one of its lower copies.
 This non-pivot-selective preparation preserves the original residual order; it precedes the completed
 orientation--Asp.Align--Mood configuration on which Ord's first search
 depends. Only Ord gives the selected participant priority over that residual
 order. A non-phasal head adds no escape step, and the analysis does not infer
 the phasehood of every argument-building layer from surface order
 (§\,\ref{sec:vm-pivot-derivations}). Neither an intermediate edge occurrence
 nor final placement removes the lower actor occurrence required for binding. Finite CP and nominal boundaries delimit separate searches:
 placement of a selected clause as a whole does not extract its arguments.
 A selected finite \ird{po}/\ird{poby} CP moves as a whole to a
 construction-specific external edge of \synhead{OrdP}, preceding its
 pivot and residual occupants. A separate requirement for selected finite complements
 applies after ordinary placement while OrdP remains open;
 it licenses an outer edge rather than another tucked-in ordinary occupant.
 The complement completes its own local structure independently, and its
 internal arguments are not matrix placement goals. Reduced
 purposive complements retain their position relative to the pivot (§\,\ref{sec:vm-po-comp}). Ordinary
 placement orders their whole constituents within those licensed positions.

The following derivations make the ordering requirements explicit. Bracketed
 role letters designate whole constituents, and $\Pi$ designates the licensed
 particle sequence; they are structural schemata, not additional lexical
 examples. The arrows show successive pronounced edge occupants, with lower
 copies left in the thematic configuration throughout.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2em]
\item[\upshape Nominative-domain transitive.]
 From $[A\ [T\ V]]$, the selected actor moves first; the residual search
 then places $T$ beneath it: $A \longrightarrow A,T\longrightarrow
 A\ T\ \Pi\ V$. For \ird{Marek travě piaštíme}, this preserves actor-direct
 and patient-indirect case, with neither participant assigned a topic feature.
\item[\upshape Ergative-domain transitive.]
 From the same thematic configuration, the selected patient $T$ moves
 across the non-selected actor, followed by residual $A$:
 $T\longrightarrow T,A\longrightarrow T\ A\ \Pi\ V$.
 Thus \ird{Tak tóm Markuto koupnik} retains patient-direct and
 actor-transitive case. The crossing belongs to the selective first search;
 the residual search cannot independently reverse $T$ and $A$.
\item[\upshape Linked actor topic.]
 After ordinary $T,A$ placement in an ergative transitive, the actor's
 independently licensed aboutness dependency moves it to Spec,\synhead{TopP}:
 $T,A\longrightarrow A_i\ [T\ t_i\ \Pi\ V]$.
 This derives \ird{Markuto tak tóm koupnik} without changing the lower
 actor--patient relation or either case (§\,\ref{sec:tc-marked-topics}).
\item[\upshape Additional participant.]
 A simple applicative selects $B$ from the lower $A>B>T$ configuration:
 $B\longrightarrow B,A\longrightarrow B,A,T\longrightarrow
 B\ A\ T\ \Pi\ V$. The existing \ird{Byl Marcí travě piaštébime}
 has beneficiary-direct, actor-indirect and theme-indirect case. An ergative
 transitive causative instead selects $T$ from $C>E>T$:
 $T\longrightarrow T,C\longrightarrow T,C,E\longrightarrow
 T\ C\ E\ \Pi\ V$, as in \ird{Travó Marku bylam piášt-r-an-ik}
 (§\,\ref{sec:aspect-caus}). Causee $E$ remains indirect, and the movement
 must pass through the accessible edges of its non-finite Voice domain.
\item[\upshape Manner modification.]
 With lower $[A\ [M\ [T\ V]]]$ and actor selection, the sequence is
 $A\longrightarrow A,M\longrightarrow A,M,T\longrightarrow
 A\ M\ T\ \Pi\ V$. An omitted actor leaves $M\ T\ \Pi\ V$,
 preserving \ird{Skorňe travě piaštíme}. In an ergative clause, selected
 $T$ moves first, after which residual $A$ and $M$ retain their order;
 pivot priority is distinct from the manner--object relation in the
 nominative-domain example.
\end{description}

Only the highest ordinary occurrence is pronounced unless the constituent
 continues to a separately licensed topic or focus position, in which case
 that higher occurrence is pronounced. Lower occurrences remain available
 for the interpretation and licensing specified below. Null arguments supply
 no overt edge occupant. Neither topicless presentation nor quantificational
 reference suppresses ordinary placement. Participant nominals, reduced
 relatives and converbs retain their own selection and placement systems;
 an AspP-selecting construction does not acquire finite Ord, Mood, Pol or
 stance structure through sharing the same participant-selection rules.

\subsection{Scope connectivity}
\label{sec:pivot-scope-connectivity}

A high pronounced argument retains the case and interpretation licensed
 through its lower chain. In \ird{Deno že zastráda}, the lower occurrence of
 the negative indefinite remains in the domain of the single clausal negation
 (§\,\ref{sec:nm-negative-concord}); ordinary placement does not turn it into
 a referential topic. The actor's lower occurrence likewise remains available
 for the established reflexive dependency involving \ird{svem}
 (§\,\ref{sec:nm-pron-refl}). A chain counts as one argument for case:
 neither an additional silent occurrence nor a higher pronounced occurrence
 adds a case competitor, and case is not recalculated from surface order.
 The exclusion of same-chain occurrences from dependent-case competition has
 a precedent in the framework discussed by Baker and Vinokurova
 \cite[§1]{bakervinokurova2010}; it does not make the timing of Case assignment
 invariant across languages or constructions.
 Wh-variables are interpreted at their lower occurrences under Pol, while
 additive and exclusive association accesses the lower argument variable.
 Quantified interpretations retain their independently specified readings;
 ordinary placement alone grants no scope reversal or new binding relation.
 These are interpretation conditions on Iridian ordinary-placement chains,
 rather than consequences of displacement in general. Fox's distinction
 between chain pronunciation and selective interpretation supplies a
 reconstruction model, while his separate treatments of A- and A$'$-chains
 preclude inferring a uniform reconstruction mechanism from high pronunciation
 alone \cite[pp.~157--159, 192--193]{fox1999}. Lower interpretation is retained
 where the particular Iridian dependency requires it, without obligatory
 reconstruction in every respect.

The use of modifier distribution to constrain functional height has a
comparative model in Cinque \cite{cinque1999}, without requiring a dedicated
projection for every Iridian lexical adverb. The position of a pivot relative
to low manner modification, frequency
quantification, phasal particles and comment-domain particles supplies separate
constraints on its structural height. The two bounded exclusive/phasal readings
in §\,\ref{sec:th-exclusive-phasal} already show why overt precedence cannot
serve as a general rule for semantic scope. Those readings and negative-concord licensing constrain chain interpretation
independently of the finite placement edge.

\subsection{Lexical manner and frequency adverbs in the preverbal field}
\label{sec:lexical-adverb-position}

Lexical manner adverbs originate as low adjuncts to \synhead{vP} or the
 outer \synhead{VP}, while frequency modifiers may attach higher within the
 event description. Their ordinary pronounced occurrences occupy the finite
 placement edge with the arguments, preceding the entire particle field.
 Lower copies preserve their event-modifying interpretations; they acquire
 neither topic status nor stance-operator status through displacement.
 In an active nominative-domain transitive, the successive placement steps in
 §\,\ref{sec:pivot-placement} preserve pivot $>$ manner adverb $>$ object,
 with particles following all three.

\noindent With its pivot unexpressed and no overt particles, an intransitive
clause accordingly has the neutral surface order \term{Adverb $>$ Verb}:

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Skorňe zemácime.//
\glb quickly run-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{I am running quickly.}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent In an active nominative-domain transitive with an unexpressed
actor and no overt particles, the non-pivot object follows the manner adverb:
\term{Adverb $>$ Object $>$ Verb}. The object retains its lower complement
occurrence for interpretation:

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Skorňe travě piaštíme.//
\glb quickly bread.\Ind{} eat-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{I am eating bread quickly.}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent Frequency adverbs such as \ird{ohly} \trsl{sometimes},
\ird{roca} \trsl{often}, \ird{člíně} \trsl{infrequently}, and \ird{sabo}
\trsl{never} belong to the broad preverbal lexical-adverb field. Their event-quantificational
interpretation differs from low manner modification; membership of that field
does not require attachment at the same structural height. Temporal frames
such as \ird{bych} \trsl{yesterday} locate the event in a time interval and
provide a third, distinct comparison. Even so, they are not members of the rigid particle series. More
leftward placement of lexical adverbs is possible under discourse pressure, but
such ordering belongs to marked information structure and should not be taken
as the neutral pattern. Lexical-adverb placement alone does not determine negative licensing
(§\,\ref{sec:lex-adv}).

\section{The copula}\label{sec:copula}

Iridian distributes equative and classificatory predication across two
constructions that differ both structurally and semantically. The default
strategy is the \term{zero copula}: a null functional head that licenses a bare
equative predication without event structure. The second strategy is the overt
marked copular verb \ird{neh-}, which introduces an event of transition and
projects a full verbal spine. Whereas the zero copula expresses present or
atemporal classification, \ird{neh-} presents entry into that classification
as an event with aspectual structure.

Property predication --- the expression of adjectival meanings such as \trsl{be
beautiful} or \trsl{be good} --- belongs to the stative verb system and is not
treated here; see Chapters~\ref{ch:overview} and \ref{ch:verbs}. Existential
predication through the \ird{jec-}/\ird{zad-} pair is structurally distinct and
treated separately in §\,\ref{sec:existential-constructions}. The equative
strategies, their structural type, negation, and temporal range are summarised
in Table~\ref{tab:copula-overview}.

\subsection{Equative and class-membership clauses: the zero copula}%
\label{sec:zero-copula}

\subsubsection{The construction and its distribution}

The default pattern for equative clauses --- those asserting identity, role, or
class membership --- is zero copula. The topic \synhead{DP} occupies the initial
position and the predicative complement appears in the direct case in
clause-final position; no overt verb is present. This is the stylistically
neutral, unmarked construction.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek doktor.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} doctor.\Dir{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek is a doctor.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Bách němscvení kompozitor.//
\glb Bach.\Dir{} German.\textsc{rel} composer.\Dir{}//
\glft \trsl{Bach is a German composer.}//
\endgl
\xe

Inclusive and class-membership predications follow the same zero-copula pattern
and carry no inference beyond simple predication. The predicative complement
always appears in the direct case regardless of which case would otherwise be
assigned in the same structural position.

\subsubsection{Structural analysis: \synhead{EquP} and the absence of AspP}

The zero copula is analysed as the phonologically null exponent of
Cop\textsuperscript{0}, which projects an \term{equative phrase}
(\synhead{EquP}) licensing the relation between the topic \synhead{DP} and
the predicative \synhead{DP}. In the unmarked present or atemporal
construction, \synhead{EquP} contains no aspectual projection and is selected
by \synhead{NegP}, when negation is present, and then by any selected
phasal or comment-domain layers before the higher \synhead{IP} layer. With
no such operators, \synhead{IP} selects the equative predication directly. Transitional interpretations instead employ \ird{neh-},
whose event structure supports the verbal aspect paradigm
(§\,\ref{sec:neh}); property predication likewise belongs to the stative
verbal system. The affirmative zero-copula structure is represented below:

\begin{center}
\begin{forest}
  syntax tree
  [\synhead{EquP}
    [{\synhead{DP}\\\textit{Marek}\\{\footnotesize\textsc{[dir]}}\\{\scriptsize topic}}]
    [{Eq$'$}
      [{\synhead{DP}\\\textit{doktor}\\{\footnotesize\textsc{[dir]}}\\{\scriptsize pred.}}]
      [{Eq$^{0}$\\{$\varnothing$}}]
    ]
  ]
\end{forest}
\end{center}

The two DPs bear the direct case as the default case assigned under
\synhead{EquP}: both the topic and the predicative complement are direct-case
nominals within a caseless, event-free predication domain.

\subsubsection{Temporal restriction}

The temporal corollary of the absence of \synhead{AspP} is that the zero-copula
construction is restricted to \term{present-time} and \term{atemporal} or
\term{generic} predications. A sentence like \ird{Marek doktor} is interpreted
as holding at the moment of utterance or as a classification that transcends any
particular time. It cannot, without additional context, be interpreted as a past
or future statement. This restriction follows directly from the lack of any
tense or aspect projection: there is no functional head in the clause that could
anchor the predication to a non-present time.

For past equative predications, \ird{neh-} in the retrospective (\ird{nav}) is
used; see §\,\ref{sec:neh-past}. For future-oriented predictions, the
contemplative of \ird{neh-} (\ird{náž}) is available. The zero copula's
restriction to present and generic contexts thus parallels the zero-copula
pattern found in Russian, Arabic, and Hebrew, where the copula is similarly
absent in the present indicative but required in other tenses --- though in
Iridian the structural basis is the absence of \synhead{EquP}'s eventuality
rather than the suppression of a tensed copula.

\subsubsection{Negation with \ird{staly}}

In a zero-copula equative, negation is expressed by clause-final \ird{staly},
whereas the verbal proclitic \ird{za-} requires a verbal host and does not occur
in this construction:

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek doktor staly.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} doctor.\Dir{} \Neg{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek is not a doctor.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Doktor Marek staly.//
\glb doctor.\Dir{} Marek.\Dir{} \Neg{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek is not the doctor.}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent In both orders, \ird{staly} remains clause-final, while the
initial \synhead{DP} supplies the topic. With \ird{Marek} as topic, the
negative denies a property ascription; with \ird{doktor} as topic, it denies
Marek's identification as the doctor.

Invariant \ird{staly} realises Neg\textsuperscript{0}$[u\textsc{neg}]$, which selects the
equative predication directly, without an intervening aspectual layer. The
silent operator $\mathrm{Op}_{\neg}$$[i\textsc{neg}]$ in Spec,\synhead{NegP} contributes semantic
negation, while the topic occupies the higher Spec,\synhead{TopP}:

\begin{center}
\begin{forest}
  syntax tree
  [\synhead{TopP}
    [{\synhead{DP}$_i$\\\textit{Marek}\\{\footnotesize\textsc{[dir]}}\\{\scriptsize topic}}]
    [{Top$'$}
      [\synhead{NegP}
        [{$\mathrm{Op}_{\neg}$\\{\footnotesize$[i\textsc{neg}]$}}]
        [{Neg$'$}
          [\synhead{EquP}
            [{\synhead{DP}\\$t_i$}]
            [{Eq$'$}
              [{\synhead{DP}\\\textit{doktor}\\{\footnotesize\textsc{[dir]}}\\{\scriptsize pred.}}]
              [{Eq$^{0}$\\{$\varnothing$}}]
            ]
          ]
          [{Neg$^{0}$\\\ird{staly}\\{\footnotesize$[u\textsc{neg}]$}}]
        ]
      ]
      [{Top$^{0}$\\$\varnothing$}]
    ]
  ]
\end{forest}
\end{center}

\noindent The tree omits the intervening phonologically null inflectional and
left-peripheral projections. The topic's lower copy occupies the equative
specifier, while the predicative \synhead{DP} remains the complement of
Eq\textsuperscript{0}; the inner Spec,\synhead{NegP} hosts the operator.
The participant's intermediate outer negative edge is omitted.

The distribution of \ird{staly} and \ird{za-} is conditioned by predicate
type (§\,\ref{sec:vm-neg-exponents} of Chapter~\ref{ch:verbs}). Zero-copula
clauses take \ird{staly}, while the overt transitional copular verb
\ird{neh-}, being a finite indicative verb, takes \ird{za-}, giving the
negative stem \ird{za-neh-} (§\,\ref{sec:clausal-negation}).

Affirmative \ird{Marek vy doktor} retains \ird{doktor} as the nominal
 predicate within EquP: only its referential participant undergoes ordinary
 placement. Negative \ird{Marek doktor vy staly} has a different pronounced
 predicate position. The negative equative analysis assigns a participant
 escape requirement to Neg\textsuperscript{0}, realised as \ird{staly},
 and, when Phas is present, an EquP-remnant requirement to the outer edge of
 PhasP. First the equative participant moves to an outer specifier of NegP,
 above the silent negative operator; the EquP remnant then raises to the outer
 Spec,\synhead{PhasP}, preceding the phasal particle in its inner specifier.
 Finally Ord targets the participant's accessible occurrence at the negative
 edge, from which it continues to OrdP and, if independently licensed, TopP.
 The participant-escape, remnant and ordinary-placement requirements are
 distinct: the remnant occupies an outer phasal specifier, whereas tucking-in
 governs repeated satisfaction of the ordinary-placement requirement.
 Ord does not extract the participant's lower copy from the displaced EquP.
 This sequence evacuates the participant before remnant movement and
 completes each lower step before building the higher placement edge:
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 [\mathrm{EquP}\ D\ N_{\mathrm{pred}}\ \mathrm{Eq}^{0}]
 &\ \longrightarrow\ [D_i\ [\mathrm{Op}_{\neg}\ [\mathrm{EquP}\ t_i\ N_{\mathrm{pred}}\ \mathrm{Eq}^{0}]\ \mathrm{staly}]]\\
 &\ \longrightarrow\ [\mathrm{EquP}]_j\ [\mathrm{vy}\ [D_i\ [\mathrm{Op}_{\neg}\ t_j\ \mathrm{staly}]]]\\
 &\ \longrightarrow\ D_i\ [\mathrm{EquP}]_j\ [\mathrm{vy}\ [t_i\ [\mathrm{Op}_{\neg}\ t_j\ \mathrm{staly}]]].
 \end{aligned}
\]
The outer phasal edge requirement is restricted to this negative equative
 construction; it does not turn a nominal predicate into an ordinary argument
 goal of Ord. Interpretation uses the lower EquP occurrence under negation
 and Phas, retaining $\mathrm{PHAS}(\neg\mathrm{Equ})$, while the participant
 is pronounced only at its highest licensed occurrence. Without Phas,
 participant escape and ordinary placement suffice for \ird{Marek doktor staly};
 no remnant displacement above Phas is required. The remnant step temporarily
 places a participant copy above its antecedent; the later Ord step restores
 the participant's higher pronounced position. Locality is checked at each
 movement step, without imposing proper binding on every intermediate
 representation. The distinction between derivational locality and such a
 representational requirement is discussed by Müller \cite[§1]{mueller2000};
 it supplies no general permission to extract from a moved phrase.
 Thus nominal predicates
 neither move indiscriminately with arguments nor invariably follow every
 particle. Existential \ird{Pivo vy zada}, by contrast, places the nominal
 pivot before Phas while retaining the existential predicate and its negative
 suppletion in the lower predication (§\,\ref{sec:existential-constructions}).

\subsection{Identificational equatives and participant nominals}%
\label{sec:ident-equative}

When the predicative complement of a zero-copula equative clause is a
\term{participant nominal} (a nominalised verb form,
§\,\ref{sec:nmlz-predicative} of Chapter~\ref{ch:verbs}), the construction is
\term{identificational}: the topic \synhead{DP} is identified with the referent
of the nominal description, which is interpreted as definite in the following example.
The English definite descriptions here and in the related existential examples
presume contextually recoverable referents; neither unanchored indirect
\ird{lúbcí} nor participant \ird{-ou} itself encodes definiteness:

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Lobek Janku piaštník.//
\glb apple.\Dir{} Janek.\Trs{} eat-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek ate the apple.} \lingcontext{verbal clause: propositional assertion}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Lobek Jancí piaštníkou.//
\glb apple.\Dir{} Janek.\Ind{} eat-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{}//
\glft \trsl{The apple is what Janek ate.} \lingcontext{identificational equative: patient nominal}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent In both clauses, bare \ird{lobek} permits the contextually
recoverable reading expressed by English \trsl{the apple}; shared-anchored
\ird{lúbko} would explicitly mark retrieval through the common discourse
model (§\,\ref{sec:nm-def}). That anchoring contrast leaves the predication
type unchanged: transitive \ird{Janku} with finite \ird{piaštník} forms the
verbal clause, whereas indirect \ird{Jancí} with participant nominal
\ird{piaštníkou} forms the equative. In (b), the apple is identified with the
referent of the nominalised description. An
agent-nominal equative identifies the topic as the agent of the nominalised
event:

\ex
\begingl
\gla Janek lúbcí piáščikou.//
\glb Janek.\Dir{} apple.\Ind{} eat-\Antip{}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek is the one who ate the apple.}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent The indirect dependents belong to the nominal case domain:
\ird{Jancí} specifies the agent of the patient description, while
\ird{lúbcí} specifies the theme associated with the antipassive-based agent
description. The latter is licensed by the nominal relational head described
in §\,\ref{sec:nominalised}; it is not an overt patient argument inside
antipassive \synhead{VP}. The same nominal derivation appears in existential
pivots, independently of matrix case or predication type.

\paragraph{Negation of identificational equatives.}
When an identificational equative is negated with \ird{staly}, the identification
of the topic with the nominal description is denied. Where the discourse treats
the event and a uniquely identifiable participant as given, this
\term{identificational negation} leaves that background intact and commonly
suggests a different participant, as the following contrast illustrates:

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Lobek Janku zapiaštník.//
\glb apple.\Dir{} Janek.\Trs{} \Neg{}-eat-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek did not eat the apple.} \lingcontext{propositional negation: denies that the event occurred}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Lobek Jancí piaštníkou staly.//
\glb apple.\Dir{} Janek.\Ind{} eat-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \Neg{}//
\glft \trsl{The apple is not what Janek ate.} \lingcontext{identificational negation: event presupposed; identification denied}//
\endgl
\xe

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Janek lúbcí piáščikou staly.//
\glb Janek.\Dir{} apple.\Ind{} eat-\Antip{}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \Neg{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek is not the one who ate the apple.} \lingcontext{agent-identification denied: it was someone else}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent The finite negative \ird{Lobek Janku zapiaštník} leaves open
whether Janek ate anything, whereas the identificational negatives reject a
participant identification against an established event. In the patient-nominal
construction, the apple is rejected as what Janek ate; in the agent-nominal
construction, Janek is rejected as the eater. In both, the suggestion of an
alternative participant depends on retaining the existential background of the
nominal description.

\ird{Staly} has the same negative function in property-ascribing and
identificational equatives, with the interpretation determined by the nominal
predicate and its discourse context (§\,\ref{sec:vm-ident-neg} of
Chapter~\ref{ch:verbs}).

\subsection{The overt copular verb \ird{neh-}: transition and inchoative
predication}\label{sec:neh}

\subsubsection{Status and structural contrast with the zero copula}

The root \ird{neh-} is the overt copular verb of Iridian. Its most basic
function is to encode \term{transition}: the topic \synhead{DP} is asserted to
be moving from one classification or condition into another, or to have crossed
a threshold between them. This transitional meaning is not a pragmatic
implication but a semantic contribution of the root itself.

Zero-copula \synhead{EquP} lacks \synhead{AspP}, whereas the transition
introduced by \ird{neh-} can be viewed with respect to its onset, duration,
or completion. Accordingly, Asp\textsuperscript{0} selects the verbal
\synhead{VP}, and a negative clause projects \synhead{NegP} above that
aspectual predicate. The two structures are represented below:

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.42\textwidth}
\centering
\textit{Zero copula:}\\[6pt]
\begin{forest}
  syntax tree
  [\synhead{EquP}
    [{\synhead{DP}\\{\footnotesize topic}}]
    [{Eq$'$}
      [{\synhead{DP}\\{\footnotesize pred.}}]
      [{Eq$^{0}$\\{$\varnothing$}}]
    ]
  ]
\end{forest}
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.52\textwidth}
\centering
\textit{Overt copula:}\\[6pt]
\begin{forest}
  syntax tree
  [\synhead{AspP}
    [{\synhead{DP}\\{\footnotesize topic}}]
    [{Asp$'$}
      [\synhead{VP}
        [{\synhead{DP}\\{\footnotesize pred.}}]
        [{\ird{neh-}}]
      ]
      [{Asp$^{0}$}]
    ]
  ]
\end{forest}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

\noindent The consequence for negation is immediate: since \ird{neh-} is a
verbal root embedded under \synhead{AspP}, the standard verbal negation strategy
applies. Neg\textsuperscript{0} is spelled out as the prefix \ird{za-}, which
procliticizes to the left edge of the inflected form. \ird{Staly} does not arise
because the condition for its appearance --- the absence of a verbal host --- is
not satisfied.

\subsubsection{Paradigm}

The paradigm of \ird{neh-} is strongly suppletive and morphologically irregular;
principal forms are given in Table~\ref{tab:neh-paradigm}. Contemplative
\ird{náž} retains the copula's suppletive stem selection while carrying
the long contemplative exponent; its written final \ird{ž} has the regular
word-final devoiced realisation [ʃ].

\begin{table}[ht]
  \footnotesize
  \caption{Principal forms of \ird{neh-} (overt copula).}\label{tab:neh-paradigm}
  \begin{tabularx}{0.84\textwidth}{L l l}
    \toprule
    {\sc form} & {\sc affirmative} & {\sc negative} \\
    \midrule
    Continuous          & \ird{neha}   & \ird{zaneha} \\
    Progressive         & \ird{nejme}  & \ird{zanejme} \\
    Contemplative       & \ird{náž}   & \ird{zanáž} \\

  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\noindent The negative forms are entirely regular: unstressed \ird{za-}
procliticizes onto each consonant-initial affirmative form without displacing
its primary stress. Before a vowel-initial host the allomorph is \ird{z-}
(§\,\ref{sec:neg-allomorphy}). No suppletive negative root exists for \ird{neh-}; the
formation parallels that of all other finite verbs.

\subsubsection{Aspectual semantics}

\ird{Neh-} introduces a transition event, and its aspectual forms characterise
different perspectives on that event in the usual way.

The \textsc{continuous} (\ird{neha}) presents the transition as ongoing and
unbounded: the subject is in the process of moving into the new classification.
The inchoative flavour is inherent to \ird{neh-} itself, not to the continuous
aspect.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek doktor neha.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} doctor.\Dir{} \Cop{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek is becoming a doctor.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek doktor zaneha.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} doctor.\Dir{} \Neg{}-\Cop{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek is not becoming a doctor.}//
\endgl
\xe

The \textsc{progressive} (\ird{nejme}) adds an agentive or effortful dimension:
the subject is actively engaged in the process of transition. Where the
continuous reports a change in progress, the progressive foregrounds the subject's
directed activity toward the new state.

\ex
\begingl
\gla Marek doktor nejme.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} doctor.\Dir{} \Cop{}-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek is working toward becoming a doctor.}//
\endgl
\xe

The \Ctpl{} (\ird{náž}) locates the transition in prospective
time: the subject will enter the new classification. It is neutral between
imminent and more remote futures.

\ex
\begingl
\gla Marek doktor náž.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} doctor.\Dir{} \Cop{}-\Ctpl{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek will become a doctor.}//
\endgl
\xe

The \textsc{perfective} (\ird{nek}) presents the transition as a completed
event: the subject has crossed the threshold and entered the new state. The
resulting state is typically understood as still holding unless contextually
cancelled.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Tak byl kapitan nek.//
\glb \Dem{}.\Prox{} child.\Dir{} captain.\Dir{} \Cop{}-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{This child became captain.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Tak byl kapitan zanek.//
\glb \Dem{}.\Prox{} child.\Dir{} captain.\Dir{} \Neg{}-\Cop{}-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{This child did not become captain.}//
\endgl
\xe

\subsubsection{The retrospective and past equative predication}%
\label{sec:neh-past}

The \textsc{retrospective} (\ird{nav}) presents the transition as situated in a
past frame and viewed from the current moment. Its semantics are aspectually
neutral between the transitional reading (the subject entered the state at some
point in the past) and the stative reading (the subject was in the state during
the relevant past period).

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek doktor nav.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} doctor.\Dir{} \Cop{}-\Ret{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek was a doctor. / Marek had been a doctor.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek doktor zanav.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} doctor.\Dir{} \Neg{}-\Cop{}-\Ret{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek was not a doctor.}//
\endgl
\xe

This dual function of the retrospective is a direct consequence of the temporal
restriction on the zero copula. Since the zero copula is confined to
present-time and atemporal predications, past equative contexts require an overt
verb, and \ird{nav} is the form that fills this role. The transitional
implication that \ird{neh-} normally carries is therefore weakened or absent in
retrospective equatives: \ird{Marek doktor nav} does not necessarily imply that
Marek underwent a process of becoming a doctor, only that the doctor
classification held in the past. The implication is a pragmatic default, not a
semantic entailment of the retrospective form, and is readily cancelled by
context.

Whether the past classification still holds is not encoded. \ird{Marek doktor
nav} is compatible both with Marek's remaining a doctor and with his having
ceased to be one; the current-state inference, if present, is a conversational
implicature from the choice of a past-framed form over the zero-copula present.

The sequential converb \ird{nesu} --- colloquially \ird{nu} --- is a suppletive
form established independently of the regular aspect paradigm. Its
grammaticalisation as a discourse particle is described in
Section~\ref{sec:seq-copula} of Chapter~\ref{ch:complex}.

\section{Existential constructions}
\label{sec:existential-constructions}

\subsection{Overview}
\label{sec:existential-overview}%
\label{sec:jec}% retained for cross-reference compatibility
\label{sec:zad}% retained for cross-reference compatibility
\label{sec:jec-zad}% retained for cross-reference compatibility

Iridian expresses existential predication through a pair of suppletive verbal
roots that together constitute the affirmative/negative alternation of a single
functional head, Exist\textsuperscript{0}. The affirmative root \ird{jec-}
asserts the existence or presence of a referent; the negative root \ird{zad-}
denies it. Both inflect for the full aspect system; both are morphologically
irregular and suppletive, with the perfective and retrospective built on
distinct suppletive stems. The two paradigms are given together in
Table~\ref{tab:jec-zad-paradigm}. The contemplatives select
\ird{jec-} and \ird{zad-} with long \ird{-áž}; their irregularity in
other aspectual cells does not require a separate contemplative ending.

\begin{table}[ht]
  \footnotesize
  \caption{Principal forms of \ird{jec-} (\textsc{exst}) and \ird{zad-} (\textsc{neg.exst}).}\label{tab:jec-zad-paradigm}
  \begin{tabularx}{0.88\textwidth}{L l l}
    \toprule
    {\sc form} & \ird{jec-} (\textsc{exst}) & \ird{zad-} (\textsc{neg.exst}) \\
    \midrule
    Continuous          & \ird{jeca}   & \ird{zada} \\
    Progressive         & \ird{jecime} & \ird{zdíme} \\
    Contemplative       & \ird{jecáž} & \ird{zadáž} \\
    Perfective          & \ird{jak}    & \ird{zděk} \\
    Retrospective       & \ird{ján}   & \ird{znení} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The affirmative and negative roots are analysed as suppletive allomorphs of
Exist\textsuperscript{0}, with \ird{jec-} in affirmative clauses and
\ird{zad-} in the environment of Neg. Neg\textsuperscript{0} receives a zero
exponent while remaining present as Neg\textsuperscript{0}$[u\textsc{neg}]$ in the syntax;
the silent operator in Spec,\synhead{NegP} contributes semantic negation
(§\,\ref{sec:neg-exst-alternation}; §\,\ref{sec:vm-jec-zad}).

Iridian has three main productive uses of the existential construction: to
assert existence or presence, including location in a particular place
(\S\,\ref{sec:existence-and-presence}), to express possession
(\S\,\ref{sec:existence-possession}), and certain idiomatic presentational
constructions (\S\,\ref{sec:existential-presentatives}).


\subsection{Existence and presence}\label{sec:existence-and-presence}

The primary use of \ird{jec-} is to assert that a referent exists or is present
at the moment of utterance or within a contextually given frame. The entity in
question stands in the direct case. \ird{Zad-} forms the direct negative
counterpart, asserting non-existence or absence under the same structural
conditions.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Byl jeca.//
\glb child.\Dir{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is a child. / A child is present.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl

\endgl
\xe

\endgl

dedicated functional head, \term{Exist}\textsuperscript{0}, which projects

feeds the higher functional sequence including \synhead{NegP}. When

\paragraph{Location.} \ird{Jec-} is also used to locate an entity: the entity
appears in the direct case and the location is expressed by an indirect noun
phrase followed by a postposed clitic adposition. \ird{Zad-} negates location,
asserting that the referent is not present at the named location.

\pex
\a
\begingl

\endgl

\noindent The locative phrase is merged as a PP adjunct to ExistP, introducing
the spatial domain within which the existential relation holds, without altering
the valency of Exist\textsuperscript{0}. The pivot remains the sole direct
argument; the structures parallel those for bare existentials above, with the PP
adjoined at the ExistP periphery in both affirmative and negative derivations.


\subsection{Possession}\label{sec:existence-possession}

Iridian has no separate verb meaning \trsl{have}. What English presents as
\trsl{I have X} is in Iridian construed as \trsl{there is X at me}: the
possessor stands in the indirect case, the possessed thing in the direct, and
the construction is a special case of existential predication. \ird{Zad-}
produces the negative possessive, asserting that the possessed item does not
exist within the possessor's sphere.

The ordinary arrangement of the clause places the possessor phrase first, the
possessed noun next, and the existential verb last:

\begin{center}
[\emph{possessor}.\Ind{} \ \ird{na}] \ \ [\emph{possessee}.\Dir{}] \ \ \ird{jec-} / \ird{zad-}
\end{center}

\noindent The possessed noun is the thing whose existence is asserted or denied
relative to the possessor as reference point. In grammatical terms it is the
existential pivot --- the direct argument of Exist\textsuperscript{0} --- while
the possessor is an oblique locative domain, a PP adjunct to ExistP, within
which the existential relation holds.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Bylam na tóm jeca.//
\glb child.\Ind{} in/on book.\Dir{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{The child has a book.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Bylam na tóm zada.//
\glb child.\Ind{} in/on book.\Dir{} \NegExst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{The child does not have a book.}//
\endgl
\xe

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Tóm bylam na jeca.//
\glb book.\Dir{} child.\Ind{} in/on \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{As for the book, it is with the child.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Tóm bylam na zada.//
\glb book.\Dir{} child.\Ind{} in/on \NegExst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{As for the book, the child does not have it.}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent The second pair illustrates that topic-initial order is fully
available with \ird{zad-} on the same terms as with \ird{jec-}: the alternation
belongs to the preverbal field and is independent of the affirmative/negative
opposition.

Following Freeze (1992) and the localist tradition, possession is treated here
as a structural subtype of location: \trsl{the book exists at the child}. The
possessor phrase [\textit{possessor}.\Ind{} \ird{na}] and a plain locative
phrase [\textit{location}.\Ind{} \ird{na}] are structurally parallel: both are
oblique PPs that modify ExistP without altering the valency of
Exist\textsuperscript{0}. The affirmative and negative possessive structures
are:

\begin{center}
\begin{forest}
  syntax tree

      [{\synhead{DP}\\\textit{tóm}\\{\footnotesize book.\Dir{}}\\{\scriptsize pivot}}]
      [{Exist$'$}
        [{Exist\\\ird{jec-}}]
      ]
    ]
  ]
\end{forest}
\hspace{2em}
\begin{forest}
  syntax tree
  [\synhead{NegP}
    [{$\mathrm{Op}_{\neg}$\\{\footnotesize$[i\textsc{neg}]$}}]
    [{Neg$'$}
      [\synhead{AspP}
        [{Asp$'$}
          [ExistP
            [{PP\\\ird{bylam na}\\{\footnotesize possessor}}]
            [ExistP
              [{\synhead{DP}\\\textit{tóm}\\{\footnotesize book.\Dir{}}\\{\scriptsize pivot}}]
              [{Exist$'$}
                [{Exist\\\ird{zad-}\\{\scriptsize suppletive}}]
              ]
            ]
          ]
          [{Asp\\\ird{-a}\\{\footnotesize\textsc{[cont]}}}]
        ]
      ]
      [{Neg\\$\varnothing$\\{\footnotesize$[u\textsc{neg}]$}}]
    ]
  ]
\end{forest}
\end{center}

\noindent The structural parallelism between locative and possessive
existentials is a direct consequence of this unified analysis: both
constructions merge a PP at the ExistP periphery, and the only grammatical
difference is the semantic content of the oblique phrase itself. The suppletive
readjustment applies identically in both frames; the PP adjunct is unaffected by
the presence or absence of \synhead{NegP}.

Since possession is expressed through the existential verb, possessive clauses
naturally inflect across the full aspectual system, and \ird{jec-} and
\ird{zad-} participate symmetrically in these aspectual contrasts. The
continuous is the common unmarked form: \ird{Bylam na tóm jeca} \trsl{the child
has a book}; \ird{Bylam na tóm zada} \trsl{the child does not have a book}. The
progressive conveys a relation in the course of arising: \ird{Bylam na tóm
jecime} \trsl{the child is getting a book}. The contemplative projects into
prospect: \ird{Bylam na tóm jecáž} \trsl{the child will have a book}. The
perfective of this lexically alternating existential permits an acquisition
reading:
\ird{Bylam na tóm jak} \trsl{the child got a book}. The retrospective places the
relation in a prior frame: \ird{Bylam na tóm žav} \trsl{the child had a book}.
In all these forms the construction remains fundamentally existential: the
possessed noun remains the direct pivot, the possessor remains oblique, and the
aspect construes the lexically available relation or acquisition event.
This existential alternation does not make perfective aspect a general
inchoative operator on productive property statives
(§\,\ref{sec:stative-inchoative}).

Since the possessed noun is the existential pivot, indefinite possessees are
especially natural. Definite possessees are not excluded, but with them the
clause more readily shades toward a meaning of location, availability, or
custody rather than settled ownership --- a consequence of the existential
source of the construction.


\subsection{Event-anchored existential pivots}\label{sec:existential-presentatives}

The existential predicate in Iridian consistently selects a nominal pivot: the
entity asserted to exist stands as the direct-case nominal argument of
Exist\textsuperscript{0}. The nominal pivots admitted by this selectional requirement fall into
three subtypes according to the internal structure of the pivot \synhead{DP}:

\begin{enumerate}[wide]
\item a bare \synhead{NP} (plain existential; §\,\ref{sec:existence-and-presence}),
\item a complex \synhead{DP} with an overt lexical head modified by a
  participant-oriented attributive (overt-head presentational;
  §\,\ref{sec:exst-overt-head}),
\item a headless \synhead{DP} whose sole content is a participant nominal
  derived from verbal structure by Iridian's participant-nominal morphology
  (headless participant-nominal; §\,\ref{sec:exst-headless-nmlz}).
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The three subtypes share a single structural identity as the nominal
argument of Exist\textsuperscript{0}, originating in Spec,\synhead{ExistP}
and appearing in the low Spec,\synhead{AspP} in the bare and presentational
aspectual structures illustrated here; the PP-initial possessive retains the
pivot in the lower existential domain. They differ in the internal architecture of the pivot
\synhead{DP}; neither low position confers aboutness topichood, and both fall
within the scope of $\mathrm{Op}_{\neg}$ in a negative existential.
Ordinary finite pronunciation precedes the particle field through the
placement dependency; PP-initial possessives retain their independently
specified PP--pivot order rather than acquiring a new aboutness topic.

\subsubsection{The participant-nominal pivot}
\label{sec:exst-pivot-arch}

An unidentified event participant may be introduced by a participant nominal,
which combines nominal external distribution with a verbal event description
(§\,\ref{sec:nmlz-predicative} of Chapter~\ref{ch:verbs}). In the analysis
adopted here, $n_{\mathrm{part}}$ supplies nominal category and participant
abstraction directly, without an intervening null lexical noun. Negative
indefinites such as \ird{zide} \trsl{no one} and
\ird{deno} \trsl{nothing} provide a distinct lexical strategy under strict
negative concord (§\,\ref{sec:nm-negative-concord}); their availability does not
require a silent noun within participant-nominal pivots.

The pivot's orientation follows from the same embedded aspect and Voice
configuration as in other participant nominals
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-derived-participant}). With transitive bases, perfective
and retrospective agents require antipassive Voice, whereas active continuous,
progressive, and contemplative forms already privilege the agent. An
intransitive nominal selects its sole participant without antipassive
morphology. Patient-oriented Voice, realised as \ird{-n-} or as
\ird{-an-} after a rhotic causative, and applicative promotion retain their
ordinary selectional roles, subject to the higher causative configuration where present. Existential
predication introduces no additional operation selecting an event participant.

\subsubsection{Overt-head presentational pivots}
\label{sec:exst-overt-head}

The overt-head presentational pivot consists of a lexical head noun in the direct
case modified by a participant-oriented attributive. Its canonical shape is:

\begin{center}
[\textit{(nominal dependent.\Ind{})} \ \textit{participant-oriented-relative} \ \textit{head-NP}.\Dir{}] \ \ \ird{jeca}
\end{center}

The modifier selects the participant privileged by embedded Voice and aspect.
Non-agentive heads are preferred in this presentational use, so patient-oriented
and applicative relatives are especially natural, as in \trsl{a letter that
the woman wrote} or \trsl{a child for whose benefit someone ate}. This preference
concerns the choice of pivot description; it does not change the relative's participant
selection.

\ird{Jeca} is fixed in this use. Temporal and aspectual interpretation come
entirely from the embedded attributive, which may be perfective, retrospective,
continuous, progressive, or contemplative. The matrix \ird{jeca} signals only
that the referent is being introduced into the current discourse.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Kářnikový senica jeca.//
\glb read-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}-\textsc{rel} news.\Dir{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is some news that (I) read.} \lingcontext{patient/theme introduced}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Že dbórnikový kíl jeca.//
\glb \textsc{1sg}.\Ind{} hear-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}-\textsc{rel} song.\Dir{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is a song that I heard.} \lingcontext{stimulus introduced via perception event}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Védnikový ryc jeca.//
\glb see-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}-\textsc{rel} cat.\Dir{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is a cat that (I) saw.} \lingcontext{perceived referent introduced via completed event}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Piaštébikový byl jeca.//
\glb eat-\Appl{}-\Pf{}-\textsc{rel} child.\Dir{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is a child for whose benefit someone ate.} \lingcontext{promoted participant introduced}//
\endgl
\xe

The attributive is an adjunct within the pivot \synhead{DP}; it is not a
predicate of the matrix clause and contributes no independent propositional
content at the matrix level. Exist\textsuperscript{0} asserts the existence
of an individual satisfying the complex pivot description. The construction
fixes the matrix form as \ird{jeca}, while the embedded relative supplies
its own event viewpoint; this restriction does not extend to the locative and
possessive existentials, which admit aspectual variation.

Patients, themes, recipients, and other non-agentive heads are strongly
favoured in the overt-head presentational, whereas an agent-oriented relative
is marked in this frame:

\ex

  marked: agentive head disfavoured in overt-head presentational}//
\endgl
\xe

This discourse preference is, however, a property of the overt-head subtype
specifically. The headless participant-nominal construction
(§\,\ref{sec:exst-headless-nmlz}) is the default indefinite strategy across all
participant roles and does not inherit any preference for non-agentive pivots.

The negative counterpart of the overt-head presentational is formed with
\ird{zada}. \synhead{NegP} scopes over ExistP, and the construction asserts that
no referent satisfying the event-anchored description exists. As with
\ird{jeca}, the continuous form \ird{zada} is fixed; the aspectual
interpretation of the embedded event comes entirely from the attributive.

\pex
\a

\endgl

\endgl
\xe

  [\synhead{NegP}

\noindent The negative existential clause denies that any referent satisfies the
event-anchored description expressed by the complex pivot. The
attributive remains an adjunct within the pivot \synhead{DP} with no
matrix-level propositional force. The disfavour of agentive pivot heads carries
over to \ird{zada} for the same reasons as in the affirmative.

The \ird{zad-} existential takes a singular pivot; plural participant nominals
are not licensed in the negative existential frame.

\subsubsection{Headless participant-nominal pivots}
\label{sec:exst-headless-nmlz}

The headless participant-nominal construction employs a pivot \synhead{DP} that
contains no lexical head noun. The pivot's entire content is a participant
nominal derived from verbal structure, whose voice--aspect configuration determines
participant orientation. This construction is the default means of expressing
indefinite participant reference in Iridian.

The participant nominal has the same $n_{\mathrm{part}}$--Asp--Voice
structure as the identificational predicates of §\,\ref{sec:nmlz-predicative}
of Chapter~\ref{ch:verbs}, with construction-conditioned exponence and an
independent nominal case domain. The participant is selected by the complete
voice--aspect configuration (Table~\ref{tab:participant-nominals}); the
nominaliser neither assumes finite absolutive assignment nor promotes an
otherwise unprivileged argument.

\noindent When such a nominal occupies the existential pivot position,
Exist\textsuperscript{0} asserts the existence of an individual satisfying the
participant description. The event description is internal to the nominalised
pivot. With contemplative aspect, the existence of a participant does not
assert realisation of the projected event. The matrix \ird{jec-}
contributes only $\textsc{exist}(x)$; the verbal material, together with its
aspect and voice, supplies the property that identifies the individual
introduced.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Ščénikou jeca.//
\glb arrive-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is someone who arrived.} \lingcontext{sole-participant indefinite: intransitive nominal}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Lúbcí piáščikou jeca.//
\glb apple.\Ind{} eat-\Antip{}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is someone who ate the apple.} \lingcontext{agentive indefinite: antipassive agent-nominal}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Védnikou jeca.//
\glb see-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is something (I) saw.} \lingcontext{patient indefinite: transitive patient-nominal}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Piaštébikou jeca.//
\glb eat-\Appl{}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is someone for whose benefit someone ate.} \lingcontext{promoted participant: applicative nominal}//
\endgl
\xe

The formal structure of the headless participant-nominal existential differs
from the overt-head construction only in the internal architecture of the pivot.
The pivot contains \synhead{AspP} above \synhead{VoiceP}, with the root
and its verbal categoriser in the lowest constituent.
The head $n_{\mathrm{part}}$ introduces nominal category and participant
abstraction; no lexical noun is present. Optional causative structure lies
below the outer patient-selecting Voice head and above the lower predicate,
which may itself contain applicative or antipassive morphology
(§\,\ref{sec:morph-template}). A simple applicative privileges its applied
participant; a higher causative instead determines the selected participant
according to its derived frame. Existential predication contributes no
additional event-participant selection.

\begin{center}
\begin{forest}
  syntax tree
  [\synhead{AspP}
    [{\synhead{DP}\\{\footnotesize pivot}}
      [{\synhead{nP}}
        [\synhead{AspP}
          [\synhead{VoiceP}
            [{\synhead{vP}\\{Root + categoriser}}]
            [{Voice}]
          ]
          [{Asp}]
        ]
        [{n\textsuperscript{0}\\\ird{-ou}\\{\scriptsize\Nmlz{}}}]
      ]
    ]
    [{Asp$'$}
      [ExistP
        [{Exist$'$}
          [{Exist\\\ird{jec-}}]
        ]
      ]
      [{Asp\\\ird{jec-a}\\{\scriptsize Exist+Cont}}]
    ]
  ]
\end{forest}
\end{center}

\noindent The semantics is parallel to the plain and overt-head cases. The nP
denotes a property of individuals, event-anchored by the verbal material it
encodes; Exist\textsuperscript{0} introduces a witness individual satisfying
that property. The eventive content is entirely internal to the pivot; no
clausal embedding of the verbal structure into the matrix occurs.

The contrast between the two constructional subtypes is already visible in the
grammar's existential question--answer paradigm:

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Védnikou by jeca?//
\glb see-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \textsc{q} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Did you see anything?} \lingcontext{headless NMLZ pivot; referent as yet unidentified}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Védnikový ryc jeca.//
\glb see-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}-\textsc{rel} cat.\Dir{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is a cat that I saw.} \lingcontext{overt-head pivot; specific referent introduced}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent In (a), headless \ird{védnikou} supplies the participant property
whose satisfaction is questioned. In (b), \ird{ryc} additionally classifies
the participant as a cat, while \ird{védnikový} supplies the event-related
restriction. The question leaves the participant unidentified; the answer
introduces a referent under a lexical description. The presence of the lexical
noun does not itself encode specificity.

\subsubsection{Indefinite participant introduction}
\label{sec:exst-indef-pos}

The headless participant-nominal construction is the primary means by which
Iridian expresses what English achieves with the free indefinite pronouns
\trsl{somebody}, \trsl{someone}, \trsl{something}, and \trsl{anything}. The
existential pivot is a participant nominal whose voice--aspect configuration selects the
relevant participant role.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Ščénikou jeca.//
\glb arrive-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Someone arrived.} \lingcontext{lit.\ there is one-who-arrived; sole-participant indefinite}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Lúbcí piáščikou jeca.//
\glb apple.\Ind{} eat-\Antip{}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Someone ate the apple.} \lingcontext{lit.\ there is one-who-ate-it; agentive transitive indefinite}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Védnikou jeca.//
\glb see-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{(I) saw something.} \lingcontext{lit.\ there is a seen-thing; patient indefinite}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Piaštébikou jeca.//
\glb eat-\Appl{}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Someone benefited from an eating event.} \lingcontext{lit.\ there is one-for-whose-benefit-someone-ate; applied participant}//
\endgl
\xe

The headless nominals in (a) and (b) freely introduce the sole participant
of arriving and the actor of eating, respectively. The preference against
agent-oriented heads belongs to the overt-head presentational; it does not
restrict participant reference in the headless construction.

In these existential clauses, the participant nominal constitutes the pivot
without an unexpressed lexical head noun, while embedded Voice and aspect
specify its participant role.

\subsubsection{Negative existential indefinites}
\label{sec:exst-indef-neg}

Negative existential clauses with participant-nominal pivots express the
meanings rendered in English by \trsl{nobody}, \trsl{no one}, and
\trsl{nothing}. The derivation is structurally parallel to the affirmative,
with \synhead{NegP} added above the aspectual predicate. Its silent operator
$\mathrm{Op}_{\neg}$$[i\textsc{neg}]$ takes scope over the existential predication, while
Neg\textsuperscript{0}$[u\textsc{neg}]$ conditions suppletive Vocabulary Insertion at
Exist\textsuperscript{0}. The resulting clause denies that any individual
satisfies the nominal description. A participant nominal formed from
affirmative verbal material does not acquire a negative-concord feature merely
by serving as the pivot: its negative indefinite interpretation follows from
existential predication within the scope of negation.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Ščénikou zada.//
\glb arrive-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \NegExst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Nobody arrived.} \lingcontext{lit.\ there is no one-who-arrived}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Lúbcí piáščikou zada.//
\glb apple.\Ind{} eat-\Antip{}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \NegExst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Nobody ate the apple.} \lingcontext{lit.\ there is no one-who-ate-it}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Védnikou zada.//
\glb see-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \NegExst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Nothing was seen. / (I) saw nothing.} \lingcontext{lit.\ there is no seen-thing}//
\endgl
\xe

The negative existential denies that any individual satisfies the participant
description, without presupposing an established event. Identificational
negation, by comparison, rejects a participant identification against a
contextually given event (§\,\ref{sec:exst-vs-ident}).

Neither negative existential \ird{zada} nor identificational \ird{staly}
negates the embedded verbal description. Participant nominalisation selects
actual \synhead{AspP}, including inner valency, causative and orientation
structure, and hence excludes internal verbal \ird{za-} as well as higher
Phas, Comm, Mood and clausal Evid structure (§\,\ref{sec:nominalised}).
The matrix structure $[\mathrm{NegP}\ [\mathrm{AspP}_{\mathrm{matrix}}\ [\mathrm{ExistP}\
[n_{\mathrm{part}}\ [\mathrm{AspP}\ \ldots]]]]]$ thus differs from excluded
$*[n_{\mathrm{part}}\ [\mathrm{NegP}\ \ldots]]$. The same distinction
separates a matrix phasal/comment operator over an existential or equative
predication from an operator inside its participant description. A negative
existential with
\ird{piáščikou} denies the existence of an eater satisfying the description;
it does not introduce someone characterised as not having eaten.

\subsubsection{Relation to identificational equatives}
\label{sec:exst-vs-ident}

The same participant nominals that serve as existential pivots with
\ird{jec-}/\ird{zad-} also serve as predicates in identificational equatives
(§\,\ref{sec:ident-equative}). As an existential pivot, the nominal supplies the description whose
satisfiers are asserted to exist; as an equative predicate, it supplies the
description with which the topic is identified.

In an identificational equative, the participant nominal is the predicative
complement of a zero-copula clause, and the topic is identified with its referent.
In an identificational reading with a contextually established event,
negation with \ird{staly} rejects the topic as the relevant participant
while retaining the event as background:

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Janek lúbcí piáščikou.//
\glb Janek.\Dir{} apple.\Ind{} eat-\Antip{}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek is the one who ate the apple.} \lingcontext{identificational equative: event presupposed; Janek identified as agent}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Janek lúbcí piáščikou staly.//
\glb Janek.\Dir{} apple.\Ind{} eat-\Antip{}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \Neg{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek is not the one who ate the apple.} \lingcontext{identificational negation: event still presupposed; Janek misidentified as agent}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent The suggestion that someone else ate the apple depends on the
continued assumption that there was an eater, together with the rejection of
Janek as that person (§\,\ref{sec:ident-equative}).

With the same nominal as an existential pivot, the affirmative asserts the
existence of an eater, while the negative denies it:

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Lúbcí piáščikou jeca.//
\glb apple.\Ind{} eat-\Antip{}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Someone ate the apple.} \lingcontext{existential: asserts existence of an agent; no event presupposition}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Lúbcí piáščikou zada.//
\glb apple.\Ind{} eat-\Antip{}-\Pf{}-\Nmlz{} \NegExst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Nobody ate the apple.} \lingcontext{negative existential: no agent exists; event not presupposed}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent In the existential construction, the participant nominal supplies the
pivot, and the negative clause denies that any individual satisfies its
description, without requiring the event to be established in advance.
The existential assertion thus differs from an identification whose event and
participant description are already given in the discourse.

To assert that no participant satisfies a description, the existential
construction with \ird{zad-} supplies the relevant reading; the identificational
construction with \ird{staly} instead disputes the proposed identity of a
participant. In contexts where the event is already established, the latter
naturally preserves that background and suggests a different participant,
whereas the negative existential makes no such commitment.

Lexical negative indefinites such as \ird{deno} \trsl{nothing} and
\ird{zide} \trsl{no one} participate in strict negative concord with both
existential and equative negation (§\,\ref{sec:nm-negative-concord} of
Chapter~\ref{ch:nouns}). Their $[u\textsc{neg}]$ feature requires the local negative
operator, whose presence is expressed by \ird{zad-} or \ird{staly} according
to predicate type; neither the indefinite nor the overt negative exponent
introduces another semantic negation. This lexical strategy coexists with the
participant-nominal existential, whose pivot need not contain a concord item.

\subsubsection{Discourse profile of the two constructional subtypes}
\label{sec:exst-discourse}

The overt-head presentational introduces a referent under both a lexical
category and an event-related description. The headless construction supplies
the participant description alone, making it suitable when the participant is
otherwise unidentified. Overt-head pivots favour non-agentive referents,
whereas headless pivots show no corresponding restriction by participant role.

The two constructions may therefore form a question--answer sequence:
\ird{Védnikou by jeca?} \trsl{Did you see anything?} asks whether an
individual satisfies the participant description, and \ird{Védnikový ryc
jeca} \trsl{There is a cat that I saw} introduces such an individual as a
cat. The same answer is poorly suited to \trsl{Who saw the cat?}, which asks
for the agent of an already specified event rather than for the patient
introduced by this pivot. The contrast follows from the participant selected
by the relative and the information requested in the question; it does not
make agents generally incompatible with existential predication.

\subsection{Suppletive exclusivity: \ird{jec-}, \ird{zad-}, and the \synhead{NegP} alternation}\label{sec:neg-exst-alternation}%
\label{sec:vm-jec-zad}%
\label{sec:vm-jec}

The alternation \ird{jec-}/\ird{zad-} is analysed as polarity-conditioned
suppletion at Exist\textsuperscript{0}, with a higher Neg head conditioning
\ird{zad-} and receiving a zero exponent
(§\,\ref{sec:vm-neg-exponents} of Chapter~\ref{ch:verbs}). The negative
contribution is supplied by $\mathrm{Op}_{\neg}$$[i\textsc{neg}]$ in Spec,\synhead{NegP}, while
Neg\textsuperscript{0}$[u\textsc{neg}]$ conditions its overt morphological expression at
the existential root. Semantically, an affirmative existential asserts that an individual satisfies
the pivot description, while the negative existential denies that any such
individual exists. In the syntactic analysis, the existential contribution belongs
to Exist and the negative contribution to the operator in Spec,\synhead{NegP},
while Neg\textsuperscript{0}$[u\textsc{neg}]$ conditions the overt root form \ird{zad-}.
The same opposition is preserved across all
construction types --- bare existential, locative, possessive, and
presentational --- and there is no grammatical asymmetry between the two roots
in terms of which construction types each permits.

In presentational constructions, both \ird{jeca} and \ird{zada} have fixed
continuous forms. The affirmative introduces a referent satisfying an
event-anchored description, whereas the negative denies that any such referent
exists, with the description itself supplied by the discourse.

\paragraph{The exclusion of \ird{*zajec-}.} The existential root \ird{jec-}
does not take the indicative negative marker \ird{za-}, so that forms such as
\ird{*zajeca} and \ird{*zajecime} are excluded across the constructions
described here, which instead use \ird{zad-}:

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Bylam na tóm zada.//
\glb child.\Ind{} in/on book.\Dir{} \NegExst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{The child does not have a book.} \lingcontext{\ird{zad-} obligatory in the negative possessive}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla {*}Bylam na tóm zajeca.//
\glb child.\Ind{} in/on book.\Dir{} \Neg{}-\Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{(intended: the child does not have a book)} \lingcontext{\ird{za-} cannot prefix \ird{jec-}}//
\endgl
\xe

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Kářnikový senica zada.//
\glb read-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}-\textsc{rel} news.\Dir{} \NegExst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is no news that (I) read.} \lingcontext{\ird{zad-} obligatory in the negative presentational}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla {*}Kářnikový senica zajeca.//
\glb read-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}-\textsc{rel} news.\Dir{} \Neg{}-\Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{(intended: there is no news that (I) read)} \lingcontext{\ird{za-} cannot prefix \ird{jec-}}//
\endgl
\xe

Negative existential clauses combine \ird{zad-} at Exist\textsuperscript{0}
with zero at Neg\textsuperscript{0}, excluding the affirmative root in
\ird{*zajeca} and the additional prefix in \ird{*zazada}. These restrictions
form part of the contextual spellout of existential polarity, under which the
negative head remains syntactically present but has no overt exponent of its
own.

\section{Negation in clausal context}\label{sec:clausal-negation}

Iridian distinguishes ordinary indicative verbal negation with \ird{za-},
zero-copula negation with \ird{staly}, and negative existential predication with
\ird{zad-}, while the non-indicative moods have dedicated negative suffixes.
In the analysis of §\,\ref{sec:vm-neg} of Chapter~\ref{ch:verbs},
Neg selects \synhead{AspP} in indicative verbal clauses and \synhead{EquP}
in zero-copula clauses, with direct realisation of Neg in \ird{za-} and
\ird{staly}, and root suppletion in existential \ird{zad-}
(Figure~\ref{fig:neg0-comparative}). In each indicative construction,
Spec,\synhead{NegP} hosts the silent $\mathrm{Op}_{\neg}$$[i\textsc{neg}]$, while the head bears
$[u\textsc{neg}]$. This division between semantic negation and its morphological reflexes
also governs strict negative concord: negative indefinites require the local
operator and the appropriate overt negative realisation, with any number of
concord associates interpreted under one negation
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-negative-concord}).

\subsection{Verbal clauses: \ird{za-}}

In ordinary finite indicative verbal clauses, unstressed \ird{za-} attaches to
the left edge of the verbal complex, with \ird{z-} before vowel-initial hosts
and primary stress remaining on the host (§\,\ref{sec:neg-allomorphy}). The
negative head is analysed as selecting the aspectual predicate, with
\ird{za-} attaching to the host's left edge at PF
(§\,\ref{sec:vm-neg} of Chapter~\ref{ch:verbs}).

\subsection{Equative clauses: \ird{staly}}

In zero-copula equative and class-membership clauses, invariant \ird{staly}
follows the topic and nominal predicate, as in \ird{Marek doktor staly}
\trsl{Marek is not a doctor}. In these clauses, Neg is analysed as a
head-final projection selecting the nominal predication directly. The same
marker appears with participant nominal
predicates, as in \ird{Lobek Jancí piaštníkou staly} \trsl{The apple is not what
Janek ate} (§\,\ref{sec:ident-equative}).

\subsection{Identificational negation and finite verbal negation}

Finite verbal and identificational negatives differ in the predication
within the scope of negation (§\,\ref{sec:ident-equative}):

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2em, labelwidth=!, labelindent=0em]
\item[\upshape Finite verbal negation (\ird{za-}).] The prefix \ird{za-} negates
  the event or state description expressed by the finite verb, denying that
  it holds without itself presupposing an alternative event, state, or
  participant.
\item[\upshape Identificational negation (\ird{staly} + participant nominal).]
  The combination of a participant nominal predicate and \ird{staly} negates the
  identification of the topic with the referent of the nominalised description.
  In uses with a contextually established event, the negative disputes the
  identity of its participant.
\end{description}

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Lobek Janku zapiaštník.//
\glb apple.\Dir{} Janek.\Trs{} \Neg{}-eat-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek did not eat the apple.}
      \lingcontext{propositional: the eating may not have occurred at all}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Lobek Jancí piaštníkou staly.//
\glb apple.\Dir{} Janek.\Ind{} eat-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}-\textsc{nmlz} \Neg{}//
\glft \trsl{The apple is not what Janek ate.}
      \lingcontext{identificational: eating presupposed; the apple is the wrong identification}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent In (b), the suggestion that Janek ate something else depends on
the continued assumption that his eating had an identifiable patient; the
negative clause rejects the apple as that patient
(§\,\ref{sec:vm-ident-neg} of Chapter~\ref{ch:verbs}).

\subsection{Existential clauses: \ird{zad-}}

Existential clauses use \ird{zad-} in place of affirmative \ird{jec-}, retaining
the aspectual contrasts described in §\,\ref{sec:zad}. Existential suppletion
is analysed as conditioned by Neg\textsuperscript{0}, whose zero exponent
excludes the prefix \ird{za-} (§\,\ref{sec:neg-exst-alternation}).

\subsection{Negative mood forms}

The prohibitive \ird{-é}, negative hortative \ird{-ku}, and negative irrealis
\ird{-ir} replace their affirmative mood suffixes and exclude \ird{za-}.
They are analysed as exponents of negative Mood selecting the Asp-bearing
predicate domain through any licensed phasal and comment layers, without a
separate Neg projection (§\,\ref{sec:vm-moodp} of Chapter~\ref{ch:verbs}).
The prohibitive and negative hortative direct or propose restraint, while the
negative irrealis expresses non-occurrence in a hypothetical situation.
Negative Mood bears $[u\textsc{neg}]$ and participates in the same concord dependency as
indicative Neg: a silent negative operator at the AspP edge, below Phas when present, licenses both
Mood and any negative indefinites in the clause. Concord therefore requires
the appropriate negative mood suffix, without adding \ird{za-} or a separate
Neg head. The local interpretation is $M(C(\mathrm{PHAS}(\neg P)))$, including
$\mathrm{IRR}(\neg P)$ without higher operators
(§\,\ref{sec:vm-moodp}).

\section{Comparative and superlative constructions}\label{sec:comparison}

Comparison in Iridian is expressed analytically using particles that combine
with stative verbs. The subject of comparison stands in the direct case; the
standard of comparison takes the indirect case. Two particles are
distinguished: \ird{stá} for direct comparison (\textit{more\ldots than},
\textit{-er than}) and \ird{zrc} for equative comparison (\textit{as\ldots
as}).

\subsection{Direct comparison}

Direct comparison is formed with the particle \ird{stá} placed between the
standard of comparison and the stative verb. The stative verb inflects for the
ordinary aspectual system within the comparative frame. A simple state stays
a state; an overt \ird{-él-}/\ird{-el-} transition can be viewed as
unfolding or bounded. Comparison specifies the scalar standard separately
from this state/change contrast:

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Janek Marcí stá četna.//
\glb Janek.\Dir{} Marek.\Ind{} \textsc{comp} be.tall-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek is taller than Marek.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Janek Marcí stá četnélime.//
\glb Janek.\Dir{} Marek.\Ind{} \textsc{comp} be.tall-\textsc{inch}-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek is becoming taller than Marek.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Janek Marcí stá četnélek.//
\glb Janek.\Dir{} Marek.\Ind{} \textsc{comp} be.tall-\textsc{inch}-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek became taller than Marek.}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent where \ird{četn-} is \trsl{be tall}. The particle \ird{stá} may also
combine with the \ird{-á} attributive form (§\ref{sec:stative-attr}) to form
an attributive comparative. The particle precedes the \ird{-á} form
prenominally:

\pex
\a \ird{stá četná pres} \trsl{the taller man}
\a \ird{Marcí stá četná pres} \trsl{the man who is taller than Marek}
\xe

\subsection{Equative comparison}

Equative comparison is formed with the particle \ird{zrc}. The structure is
otherwise identical to the direct comparative, with the subject in the direct
case and the standard in the indirect. Negation of the equative falls on the
stative verb itself, which takes the regular negative prefix \ird{za-}
(§\ref{sec:negation}):

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Janek Marcí zrc četna.//
\glb Janek.\Dir{} Marek.\Ind{} \textsc{eq} be.tall-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek is as tall as Marek.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Janek Marcí zrc začetna.//
\glb Janek.\Dir{} Marek.\Ind{} \textsc{eq} \Neg{}-be.tall-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek is not as tall as Marek.}//
\endgl
\xe
