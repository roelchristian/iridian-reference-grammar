\section{Overview}
\label{sec:nm-overview}

The nominal morphological template of Iridian, reading from the lexical root
outward to the rightmost morpheme, is:

\medskip
\begin{center}
\textsc{root} + (\textsc{case}) + (\textsc{anch})
\end{center}
\medskip

\noindent Prenominal material precedes the root in the surface
string:

\medskip
\begin{center}
(\ird{si}) + (\textsc{dem}) +
(\textsc{quant}/\textsc{num}) + (\textsc{poss}.\Ind{}) +
(\textsc{prop.root} / \textsc{stative} / \textsc{\ird{-ový}}) +
\textsc{root} + (\textsc{case.num}) + (\textsc{anch})
\end{center}
\medskip

This template is interpreted under the \term{\synhead{DP} Hypothesis} (Abney
1987): the nominal expression is not simply an \synhead{NP} but a full
functional projection with an articulated D-domain. In this chapter I use
\term{anchoring} as the theoretical label for the category expressed by
\ird{-to}; \emph{definiteness} is retained only as the traditional descriptive
name. The suffix \ird{-to} is therefore treated as the overt realisation of an
anchoring head, labelled \textsc{Anch\textsuperscript{0}}, with the features
[\textsc{+anch,+shr}].

Case-number and shared anchoring constitute the suffixal spine; the speaker-
anchoring exponent has the separate proclitic placement described below.
Case and number are fused into a single morphological slot: each declension class
provides an exponent for each of six case-number cells, although some cells
are syncretic, and no separate number morpheme exists. Among the prenominal items,
the demonstrative system is the overt realisation of a distinct higher nominal
projection whose exponents surface at the left edge of their domain. The particle
\ird{si} is treated differently: it is the proclitic Vocabulary Item for
Anch\textsuperscript{0}[\textsc{+anch,\,-shr}], sharing its head with the
suffixal \ird{-to}/\ird{-te} but given left-edge precedence by nominal-domain linearisation
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-si-linearisation}). The hierarchical
organisation of functional projections, reading from innermost to outermost, is given in
Table~\ref{tab:nm-projections}.

The Mirror Principle (Baker 1985) predicts that the morphological order reflects
the syntactic order. Reading the template from root outward, \textsc{case.num}
appears closer to the root than \textsc{anch}; this means \synhead{KP} is
dominated by \synhead{AnchP}. The same suffixal order follows directly from
head-finality. The fused \textsc{case.num} exponent reflects the PF Merger of
Num\textsuperscript{0} into K\textsuperscript{0}: number semantics is projected
in the syntax but its spellout is entirely subsumed by the case-number VI,
leaving no independent morpheme in the surface string. By contrast, the higher
projections realised prenominally do not need to be treated as ordinary suffixal
heads. The simpler analysis is that Iridian has an articulated nominal left
periphery whose overt exponents realise \synhead{DemP} at the left edge of their
domain, while the suffixal spine remains strictly head-final. The proclitic
\ird{si} receives left-edge precedence within its nominal domain, although
its source remains Anch\textsuperscript{0}; the linearisation rule in
§\,\ref{sec:nm-si-linearisation} supplements the head-final default.

The suffixal nominal spine parallels the verbal complex: case-number precedes
anchoring within the noun phrase, just as the lower verbal morphology precedes
the outer finite suffixes. The following tree represents this nominal hierarchy before PF linearisation.

\begin{center}
\begin{forest}
  syntax tree
  [\synhead{DemP}
    [{\textit{demonstrative series}}]
    [\synhead{QP}
      [{\textit{numeral / quantifier}}]
      [\synhead{AnchP}
        [\synhead{KP}
          [\synhead{PossP}
            [{\textit{oblique possessor}}]
            [{\synhead{NP}\\\textit{root}}]
          ]
          [{K\textsuperscript{0}\\{\footnotesize case.num}}]
        ]
        [{Anch\textsuperscript{0}\\\ird{-to}/\ird{-te} / \ird{si} / $\varnothing$}]
      ]
    ]
  ]
\end{forest}
\end{center}

\noindent The tree keeps the nominal spine uniform across the subsections below.
Overt exponents of higher nominal projections are shown directly at the edge of
the projection they realise, and silent heads are not inserted merely to force a
specifier analysis. Only the case-number and anchoring heads are represented as
overt heads to display the suffixal spine; the proclitic exponent of Anch
receives its precedence at linearisation rather than at the head-final
position shown here (§\,\ref{sec:nm-si-linearisation}).
The \synhead{QP} layer is drawn only as a conservative cover term for prenominal
quantificational material; the sections below do not assume a finer
numeral-vs-quantifier split unless the data require it.


\subsection{The root and NP}
\label{sec:nm-root}

The lexical noun occupies the head position of \synhead{NP}. Unlike the verbal
root, which is licensed by a selecting functional head (the Asp.Align morpheme
is obligatory on every finite verb; cf.\ §\,\ref{sec:vm-root}), the nominal root
may surface without an overt case-number ending in consonant-stem and bare-root
classes, whereas thematic classes retain an overt direct ending. A nominal
without either \ird{si} or a suffixal anchor retains the extended structure
although \textsc{Anch\textsuperscript{0}} receives no overt spellout; its
referent may nevertheless be identifiable in context
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-bare-noun}).

Iridian nominals project beyond \synhead{NP}; within this extended projection,
\synhead{AnchP} names the layer whose overt exponents express anchoring.
\emph{Definiteness} is the traditional descriptive label for one common use of
that layer.

\section{The case system}
\label{sec:nm-case}

\subsection{Case, \synhead{KP}, and the alignment-sensitive argument system}
\label{sec:nm-case-system}

The lowest overt functional layer of the noun phrase is \synhead{KP}, the case
projection. Modern Iridian has three productive structural cases: \term{direct},
\term{transitive}, and \term{indirect}; a fourth case, the \term{vocative},
survives as a nonproductive form limited to direct address in lexically
specified items. The direct case supplies the citation form, with zero or a thematic ending
according to class. Case-number endings realise \textsc{K\textsuperscript{0}},
while the vocative is a specialised
speech-act-adjacent realisation of the same low nominal domain.

\begin{table}
  \footnotesize
  \caption{Case values in the Iridian nominal domain.}
  \label{tab:nm-cases}
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{l L L}
    \toprule
    {\sc case} & {\sc primary function} & {\sc structural interpretation} \\
    \midrule
    Direct & citation form; absolutive argument; predicate nominal &
    unmarked case value; zero or thematic exponent by class \\
    Transitive & agent of perfective/retrospective transitives &
    ergative realisation of K \\
    Indirect & patient in nominative-alignment aspects; possessors;
    obliques; governed complements of postpositions &
    general non-core K realisation \\
    Vocative & direct address in lexically specified names, kin
    terms, and a small residue of nouns &
    speech-act linked case in the low nominal domain \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The split alignment facts belong to the clause, but the nominal contribution is
straightforward: \synhead{KP} makes available the case values that the clausal
architecture selects. In the perfective and retrospective aspects the patient
remains direct while the agent is transitive; in the nominative-alignment
aspects the agent remains direct while the patient appears in the indirect.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek zmacime.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} run-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek is running.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Travato Marku piaštnik.//
\glb bread.\Dir{}.\Def{} Marek.\Trs{} eat-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek ate the bread.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek travě piaštime.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} bread.\Ind{} eat-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek is eating bread.}//
\endgl
\xe

The direct supplies the unmarked absolutive case, while the transitive marks the
agent in perfective and retrospective clauses and the indirect marks patients
in nominative-alignment clauses as well as possessors, obliques, and
postpositional complements. The vocative is confined to lexically specified
forms of direct address.

Declension combines case-number exponence with stem selection: the same ending
may follow different stem grades, since each noun carries both a declension
class and the lexical information that licenses reduction, raising, or quantity
alternation (§§\,\ref{sec:nm-stemform}--\ref{sec:nm-declension}). Anchoring is
assembled outside \synhead{KP} and can therefore follow any case form, although
its non-direct uses favour contrastive or resumptive reference
(§\,\ref{sec:disc-def}). The morphological order is:

\medskip
\begin{center}
\textit{root} $\rightarrow$
\textit{class-selected stem grade} $\rightarrow$
[K\textsuperscript{0}$+$Num\textsuperscript{0}] exponent $\rightarrow$
Anch\textsuperscript{0} exponent
\end{center}
\medskip

\noindent Case and number are fused at [K\textsuperscript{0}$+$Num\textsuperscript{0}];
the exponent selected for each of the six case-number cells belongs to the
noun's declension class, with no separate number morpheme in the surface string.

\subsection{Stem formation and morphophonemic processes}
\label{sec:nm-stemform}

Nominal paradigms distinguish full and bound stems, consonant-mutation classes,
and quantity grades. A noun's declension class specifies its case-number
exponents, while its stem class determines which vocalic relation those
exponents select. The two specifications need not coincide: \ird{perek} and
\ird{lobek} share transitive \ird{-u}, but select \ird{perk-} and \ird{lubk-},
respectively.

In fleeting-vowel stems, one short unstressed \ird{e} in the final stem
syllable alternates with zero. Thus \ird{Janek}/\ird{Jank-},
\ird{perek}/\ird{perk-}, and \ird{obel}/\ird{obl-} preserve the stressed
vowel and every consonant. The reduced stem, traditionally called the
\term{zero grade}, is selected before the licensed case-number endings and,
in these nouns, before direct anchoring. Stable stems retain their vowels in
the same environments (§\,\ref{sec:vowel-zero}).

Raised subclasses additionally select \ird{e/ě}/\ird{i} or \ird{o}/\ird{u}
in the bound stem, with corresponding long-vowel pairs \ird{é}/\ird{í} and
\ird{ó}/\ird{ú}. Height changes preserve quantity: \ird{lobek}/\ird{lubk-}
and \ird{pěštel}/\ird{pištl-} combine reduction with raising, whereas
\ird{perek}/\ird{perk-} combines reduction with stable root height. The raised
grade is inherited lexical information, not a repair for the consonant cluster
or an automatic effect of the current suffix vowel.

Case-number exponents that select the palatal/front grade independently
mutate the final consonant of the selected stem. Their front vocalism often
corresponds to that grade, but does not itself determine it. Ordinary nominal
mutation gives \ird{k $\rightarrow$ c} and coronal
\ird{t/d/n/l $\rightarrow$ ť/ď/ň/ľ}; \ird{perek} therefore yields
\ird{perk-} plus \ird{-e}, with velar mutation producing \ird{perce}.
Lexical allomorphs such as \ird{red}/\ird{rez-} in \ird{rezy} are specified
with their class and do not redefine productive coronal softening. The same
palatal grade supplies alveolopalatal realisations of final /s, z, ts/ and
secondary palatalisation of labials; these changes are distinct from the
contextual neutralisation of sibilants before /ɪ, iː/
(§\,\ref{sec:palatal-sibilants}). Grade selection applies to the boundary
input once: inherited /k/ $\rightarrow$ /ts/ does not feed a second application
of /ts/ $\rightarrow$ [tɕ]. Thus \ird{perce} retains its alveolar affricate
before /ɛ/.

Liquid-bearing stems require no separate syllabic grade. In \ird{uzel}
$\rightarrow$ \ird{uzla}, \ird{l} begins the second syllable; in
\ird{perek} $\rightarrow$ \ird{perku}, \ird{r} closes the first.
If reduction would prevent syllabification around vowel nuclei without
unlicensed consonant loss, the vowel is retained
(§\,\ref{sec:vocalic-nuclei}; §\,\ref{sec:cluster-repair}).

Coda /l/ vocalisation follows stem selection, consonant mutation, and
suffixation. Thus \ird{byl} [bɨ̞w] retains the same underlying lateral as
\ird{byla} [ˈbɨ̞.la], where the case ending places it in an onset; soft
\ird{ľ} retains its lateral realisation. Prevocalic /v/ surfaces as [ʋ],
while coda /v/ in zero-ended \ird{tráv} gives [traːw]. These positional
realisations leave the class-selected stems and case-number exponents intact
(§§\,\ref{sec:l-vocalisation} and~\ref{sec:v-realisation}).

Quantity classes are independent of both reduction and raising. In Class~III,
\ird{stráz}/\ird{straz-} and \ird{tráv}/\ird{trav-} select long and short
grades in specified case-number cells. The root \ird{rán-}, by contrast,
retains its long vowel throughout. Analogical levelling is reflected in these
stored stem relations; it is not applied after the case form has been spelled
out.

\subsection{Declension class as the lexical specification of KP}
\label{sec:nm-declension}

\synhead{KP} is not realised by a single invariant suffix. Rather, the noun
lexically selects a declension class whose case exponents interact with the
morphophonemic processes described in §\,\ref{sec:nm-stemform}. That lexical
information is part of the nominal root's stored feature bundle: the root enters
the derivation already specified for which K-exponents it realises and which
stem grades those exponents select.

Six macroclasses are recognised. Five are broadly productive structural classes;
the sixth is a small closed relic class of inherited stems with front-vocalic
oblique exponents not generalisable to the main classes. The macroclasses are
identified by stem type and by the exponents they use across the direct,
transitive, and indirect cases.

\paragraph{Class I: Hard consonant stems.}
The core class for ordinary consonant-final nouns, including personal names and
a large body of lexical nouns. The direct singular is the bare citation stem.
The transitive singular adds a back-vocalic exponent, typically \ird{-a} (and
\ird{-u} in certain subclasses). The indirect singular adds a back or
front-vocalic exponent, typically \ird{-am} or a related ending, and may
select the palatal grade in lexically specified subclasses. The spelling of
the ending alone does not establish whether a consonant alternates. Plural
forms are built on a distinct set of exponents: \ird{-e}
(direct), \ird{-óv} (transitive), \ird{-ac} (indirect).

\pex
\a \ird{byl} \textsc{sg}: \ird{byl} / \ird{byla} / \ird{bylam}
\a \ird{byl} \textsc{pl}: \ird{byle} / \ird{bylóv} / \ird{bylac}
\xe

\noindent This class is semantically open and is one of the chief productive
defaults for consonant-final nouns. Subclasses differ in the precise vocalism of
their oblique exponents, but share the fundamental property of a bare-stem
singular direct and suffixal case-number endings.

\paragraph{Class II: Fleeting-vowel consonant stems.}
The citation form contains a short unstressed \ird{e} which is absent from the
bound stem. Unraised \ird{perek}/\ird{perk-} and raised
\ird{lobek}/\ird{lubk-} represent two subclasses. Their overt case-number
endings select the bound stem; direct anchoring selects it as well. The
singular transitive has \ird{-u}; the indirect has \ird{-í} in the raised
subclass illustrated here and \ird{-e} in the unraised subclass. These are vowel
endings: the \ird{c} in \ird{lubcí} and \ird{perce} belongs to the mutated
stem. Plural endings are \ird{-e}, \ird{-ec}, and \ird{-em}, with
the palatal/front grade in each of these plural cells. Its inherited velar
correspondence is /k/ $\rightarrow$ /ts/; the consonant is not changed merely
because the next written vowel is front.

\pex
\a \ird{lobek} \textsc{sg}: \ird{lobek} / \ird{lubku} / \ird{lubcí}
\a \ird{lobek} \textsc{pl}: \ird{lubce} / \ird{lubcec} / \ird{lubcem}
\a \ird{perek} \textsc{sg}: \ird{perek} / \ird{perku} / \ird{perce}
\a \ird{perek} \textsc{pl}: \ird{perce} / \ird{percec} / \ird{percem}
\xe

The reduced relation can be productively assigned by native nominal formations,
whereas raised-grade membership is inherited. A new noun does not acquire a
raised root merely by taking \ird{-u}.

\paragraph{Class III: \ird{-a} stems and long-vowel alternants.} The largest
open class among ordinary lexical nouns. Its most transparent members have a
singular direct in \ird{-a}; the singular transitive replaces that final vowel
with \ird{-y}, and the singular indirect takes the regular front-vocalic
oblique exponent. Alongside this regular subtype stands a smaller alternating
subtype in which the singular direct and plural transitive preserve a long
vowel, while the overtly suffixed forms show the corresponding short-vowel
stem. In the plural, the direct is \ird{-y} (syncretic with the singular
transitive), the transitive is the bare stem with zero ending, and the
indirect takes \ird{-ám}.

\pex
\a \ird{trava} \textsc{sg}: \ird{trava} / \ird{travy} / \ird{travě}
\a \ird{trava} \textsc{pl}: \ird{travy} / \ird{tráv} / \ird{travám}
\a \ird{stráz} \textsc{sg}: \ird{stráz} / \ird{strazy} / \ird{strazě}
\a \ird{stráz} \textsc{pl}: \ird{strazy} / \ird{stráz} / \ird{strazám}
\xe

\paragraph{Class IV: Soft and sibilant stems.}
This class combines front-vocalic endings with lexically specified soft or
sibilant stem grades. In \ird{red}, the singular transitive selects
\ird{rez-}, whereas the singular indirect and plural direct select
\ird{reď-}; neither alternation requires vowel deletion. The singular
indirect takes \ird{-e}, and the plural exponents are \ird{-e} (direct),
\ird{-óv} (transitive), and \ird{-ím} (indirect).
Where /d/ alternates with [ɟ], the latter is written \ird{ď}; the
spelling records the selected consonant rather than producing it.

\pex
\a \ird{red} \textsc{sg}: \ird{red} / \ird{rezy} / \ird{reďe}
\a \ird{red} \textsc{pl}: \ird{reďe} / \ird{redóv} / \ird{redím}
\xe

\paragraph{Class V: Bare-root and thematic stems.}
The bare-root subtype represented by \ird{rán} has singular endings zero,
\ird{-ou}, and \ird{-e}, and plural endings \ird{-a}, zero, and \ird{-om}.
Its root is invariant \ird{rán-}; terminal vowels belong to the endings.
The indirect \ird{-e} selects the plain consonant grade, hence \ird{ráne},
with /n/, rather than a palatal nasal.
The long root vowel survives throughout the paradigm. These endings are
class-specific and do not automatically invoke the mutation of an ordinary
front-coded consonant-stem suffix.

\pex
\a \ird{rán} \textsc{sg}: \ird{rán} / \ird{ránou} / \ird{ráne}
\a \ird{rán} \textsc{pl}: \ird{rána} / \ird{rán} / \ird{ránom}
\xe

The related thematic subtype has a direct singular in \ird{-o}, as in
\ird{bro}/\ird{bra}/\ird{brum}. Here \ird{br-} combines with \ird{-o},
\ird{-a}, and \ird{-um}; the alternation lies in the endings rather than in
the root (§\,\ref{sec:thematic-vowels}).

\paragraph{Class VI: Relic front-vocalic stems.}
A small, closed class of inherited stems whose singular oblique exponents — a
transitive in \ird{-im} and an indirect in \ird{-y} — are not generalisable to
the main productive classes. These items form a tight microclass learned as a
lexical residue. Plural exponents are \ird{-ove} (direct), \ird{-óv}
(transitive), \ird{-ovim} (indirect), all built on an extended stem in
\ird{-ov-}.

\pex
\a \ird{pět} \textsc{sg}: \ird{pět} / \ird{pětim} / \ird{pěty}
\a \ird{pět} \textsc{pl}: \ird{pětove} / \ird{pětóv} / \ird{pětovim}
\xe

\noindent Declension class specifies how a noun realises the K head supplied by
the syntax, so that class-sensitive stem selection and exponence are properties
of PF within a uniform \synhead{KP}.

\subsection{Stem grades as allomorph selection under K}
\label{sec:nm-stem-grades}

Case endings do not simply attach to a single invariant stem. They select among
recurring stem grades: the full stem, reduced stem, raised bound stem,
quantity grade, and consonant-mutated stem. These are contextually
conditioned allomorphs of the same root, triggered by the K head and by the
morphophonemic processes described in §\,\ref{sec:nm-stemform} above.

\subsection{Vocative and proper-name behaviour}
\label{sec:nm-vocative}

The vocative is the most restricted member of the case system. It is
nonproductive in the modern language. A marked vocative is viable only for
lexically specified items: inherited proper names, older integrated loan proper
names, kinship terms, and a restricted residue of concrete and abstract nouns.
The latter class is lexically determined item by item and does not follow
systematically from membership in the inherited vocabulary. Outside those
lexicalised items, direct address uses the unmarked direct case. In other words,
in the absence of a marked vocative, the language defaults to the direct case.
The simplest analysis is that the vocative realises the low nominal domain in
direct relation to the speech-act layer, bypassing the ordinary anchoring
opposition. This is why the vocative rejects \ird{-to}: direct address
identifies the referent deictically by the act of address itself.

\section{Anchoring and definiteness}
\label{sec:nm-def}

\subsection{The suffix \ird{-to} and its allomorphy}
\label{sec:nm-def-allomorph}

The anchoring suffix \ird{-to} has two surface-distinct allomorphs whose
distribution is conditioned by the phonological shape of the immediately
preceding morpheme — the case ending, or the bare stem where no case ending is
present.

\begin{enumerate}[wide]
  \item The full allomorph \ird{-te/-to} is inserted when the immediately
  preceding segment is a \textbf{vowel}. The full form is preserved because no
  cluster-breaking is required.
  \item A reduced allomorph \ird{-e/-o} is inserted when the immediately
  preceding segment is a \textbf{consonant}. This vocalic exponent avoids the
  cluster created by full \ird{-to/-te} after a consonant. Its vowel realises
  anchoring rather than general phonological epenthesis; historically, loss of
  the stop in \ird{*-to/-te} left the vocalic allomorph.
\end{enumerate}

In both allomorphs, the choice between front (\ird{-e}, \ird{-te}) and back
(\ird{-o}, \ird{-to}) vocalism is governed by class-conditioned harmony rather
than purely by the surface vocalism of the immediately preceding case ending.
Some declension classes preserve front anchoring in the direct case but
generalise back-vocalic anchoring in the oblique cases; see
§\,\ref{sec:nm-def-nondirect} for the full paradigmatic account.

The traditional descriptive label for this suffix is \emph{definiteness}, but in
the present discussion the theoretical feature is anchoring. In Distributed
Morphology terms (Halle \& Marantz 1993; Embick \& Noyer 2007), the reduced and
full variants are two Vocabulary Items competing for insertion at the same
\textsc{Anch\textsuperscript{0}} node. The choice is made by phonological
context at PF: the reduced allomorph (\ird{-e/-o}) is the consonant-context
spellout, while the full allomorph (\ird{-te/-to}) is the vowel-context
spellout.

Allomorph selection precedes the positional consonant rules. The final /l/
of \ird{byl} [bɨ̞w] selects consonant-context \ird{-o}, yielding
\ird{bylo} [ˈbɨ̞.lɔ], whose lateral is syllabified as an onset before
vocalisation applies. The surface glide in the bare noun therefore does not
select the vowel-context allomorph. Coda /v/ in bare \ird{tráv} [traːw]
likewise corresponds to a prevocalic approximant in anchored \ird{tráve},
whose vocalic anchor places /v/ in an onset. The consonant-context allomorph
is selected before either coda segment vocalises
(§\,\ref{sec:v-realisation}).

Alternating nouns select their bound stem in the anchored direct, as in
\ird{lobek}/\ird{lubko} and \ird{perek}/\ird{perko}; the anchor then
selects its allomorph at the resulting boundary. In non-direct cases it follows
the already formed case-number word. A quantity grade selected by case-number
is retained under anchoring; suffixation does not automatically shorten it.

\subsection{Case stacking and the ordering \synhead{AnchP} $>$ KP}
\label{sec:nm-def-stacking}

The surface fact is straightforward: \ird{-to} attaches to the \emph{already
case-inflected} noun. The morphological order is therefore:

\begin{center}
\textsc{root} $+$ \textsc{case-ending} $+$ \textsc{anch}
\end{center}

\noindent The interpretive consequence is that case determines the noun's
clausal role, while anchoring is added outside that case layer. The simplest
structural analysis is therefore \synhead{KP} dominated by \synhead{AnchP}:

\begin{center}
\begin{forest}
  syntax tree
  [\synhead{AnchP}
    [\synhead{KP}
      [\synhead{NP}
        [{\textit{root}}]
      ]
      [{K\textsuperscript{0}\\{\footnotesize case ending}}]
    ]
    [{Anch\textsuperscript{0}\\\ird{-to} / $\varnothing$}]
  ]
\end{forest}
\end{center}

\noindent This tree deliberately omits the higher left-peripheral layers.
Demonstratives are distinct edge material outside \synhead{AnchP}.
Anch\textsuperscript{0}[\textsc{+anch,\,-shr}] receives left-edge
precedence within the extended nominal domain and is then realised as
\ird{si}; its position in the syntactic spine is unchanged
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-si-linearisation}). The right edge of \synhead{KP}
is the only place where the suffixal exponents \ird{-to}/\ird{-te} surface.

The suffixal order parallels the verbal domain. The Asp.Align morpheme — the
verbal analogue of the overt nominal D-layer — is also the sole obligatory
element of the finite verb, surfaces at the right edge of the verbal complex,
and mediates between the lexical root and the higher inflectional layers
(§\,\ref{sec:vm-asp}). In both the verbal and nominal domains, the rightmost
morpheme is the head of the highest overt suffixal projection, and the
directionality of morphological layering is predicted by head-finality.


\subsection{Anchoring in non-direct case forms}
\label{sec:nm-def-nondirect}

The morphological template places anchoring outside \synhead{KP}, so that
 direct, transitive, and indirect case forms all admit an anchor. Its meaning
 remains shared-discourse accessibility across the paradigm. An anchored
 transitive agent or indirect participant may continue a backgrounded referent
 without becoming the aboutness topic; contrast and topical promotion depend
 on the clause and its context (§\,\ref{sec:disc-def}).

The alternation between full and reduced anchoring follows the final segment of
the case-number form. Class~I \ird{byl} `child' has the following singular
series:

\pex
\a \ird{byl} [\Dir{}] $\rightarrow$ \ird{bylo}
\a \ird{byla} [\Trs{}] $\rightarrow$ \ird{bylato}
\a \ird{bylam} [\Ind{}] $\rightarrow$ \ird{bylamo}
\xe

\noindent All three cells select back-coded anchoring, but only vowel-final
\ird{byla} takes full \ird{-to}; consonant-final \ird{byl} and \ird{bylam}
take reduced \ird{-o}. The indirect therefore patterns with the direct at the
immediate boundary even though its ending contains a vowel. Stem class and the
case-number exponent determine front or back vocalism independently of this
stop alternation.

Class~III illustrates the same separation with vowel-final forms:
\ird{trava}/\ird{travato} has back-coded direct anchoring, whereas
\ird{travě}/\ird{travěte} has front-coded indirect anchoring. The case ending
and its quantity are preserved, while the anchor contributes the class-selected
full exponent.

\subsection{Stem classes and anchoring domains}
\label{sec:nm-def-history}

The bound stem selected in direct anchoring is the one used in the noun's
inflectional paradigm: \ird{Marek}/\ird{Marko}, \ird{perek}/\ird{perko},
and \ird{lobek}/\ird{lubko}. This distribution extends stem selection beyond
overt case-number suffixes without making the anchoring vowel an automatic
trigger of root raising. The corresponding Old Iridian reconstruction is given
in §\,\ref{sec:old-iridian-vocalism}.

Stable stems retain their vocalism, as in \ird{byl}/\ird{bylo}, while the
quantity grade selected by case-number survives under the outer anchor, as in
\ird{tráv}/\ird{tráve} and \ird{stráz}/\ird{stráze}. In non-direct forms,
anchoring follows the already inflected word: \ird{byla}/\ird{bylato} and
\ird{bylam}/\ird{bylamo} differ in stop retention because their case endings
have different final segments.

Class~VI has the same boundary-sensitive distribution despite its restricted
case endings. Consonant-final \ird{pět} and \ird{pětim} take reduced
\ird{-o}, yielding \ird{pěto} and \ird{pětimo}, whereas vowel-final
\ird{pěty} takes full front-coded \ird{-te}, yielding \ird{pětyte}. None of
these operations changes the root vowel.

\subsection{Anchoring and the deverbal \ird{-ov-} stem}
\label{sec:nm-def-contraction}

Deverbal nouns with a direct singular in \ird{-ou} select the anchored
allomorph \ird{-ovat}, as in \ird{piaščikou}/\ird{piaščikovat}. The
oblique singular forms contain \ird{-ov-}, giving transitive
\ird{piaščikovy} and indirect \ird{piaščikově}; their anchored counterparts
follow the ordinary vowel-context rule: \ird{piaščikovyte} and
\ird{piaščikověte}. The direct anchored form is a construction-conditioned
exponent of the nominal stem, case, and anchoring features. It is not the
output of a general phonological contraction of \ird{-ou} with \ird{-to}.
Class~V transitive \ird{ránou} therefore retains ordinary \ird{ránouto}.

Anchoring a dependent such as \ird{travěte} in
\ird{Marcí travěte piaštou} marks that dependent as discourse-anchored.
It may help establish the intended interpretation when the context identifies
the bread as the theme, but it does not assign thematic role. The \ird{-ovat}
allomorph of the containing deverbal noun is a separate morphological
operation (§\,\ref{sec:nm-derived-gerund}).

\subsection{Anchored reference and the [\textsc{+anch}] feature}
\label{sec:nm-def-semantics}

The descriptive generalisation is that nouns with \ird{-to} normally pick out a
referent that the speaker presents as already recoverable or singled out in the
shared discourse. Its uses include anaphoric continuation, situational
identification, and the reintroduction of a referent, whether that referent
functions as topic or remains within the comment.

The interpretive effect is therefore best described as \term{anchoring}. A noun
phrase marked by \ird{-to} is construed as an anchored discourse token
whose identity is presented as shared. The following contexts illustrate this
contribution:

\begin{enumerate}[wide]
\item Anaphoric:The anchored noun resumes a referent introduced in prior
  discourse.
\item Situationally unique: The anchored noun refers to an entity that is
  salient in the speech situation, though Iridian is more selective here than
  languages with obligatory articles.
\item Contrastive or resumptive: The suffix appears where the noun is being
  re-identified, resumed, or singled out as the discourse-relevant token.
\item Generic/kind: The bare noun is the ordinary vehicle for generic and
  kind-denoting reference. A noun with
  [\textsc{+anch}] generally resists a generic reading.
\end{enumerate}

The suffix realises \textsc{Anch\textsuperscript{0}}[\textsc{+anch,+shr}]:
the shared feature instructs the addressee to recover the referent from the
common discourse model. Speaker-anchoring \ird{si}, which shares
[\textsc{+anch}] but lacks [\textsc{+shr}], instead presents the speaker's
selection of a particular referent (§\,\ref{sec:nm-spec}). The feature
[\textsc{+anch}] alone therefore does not require shared identifiability.


\subsection{Distribution restrictions}
\label{sec:nm-def-restrictions}

Several contexts exclude the anchoring suffix:

\paragraph{The vocative.}
The vocative case is incompatible with \ird{-to}. It is also nonproductive: only
lexically specified vocatives survive, especially with inherited and older
integrated loan proper names, kinship terms, and a small residue of concrete and
abstract nouns. Elsewhere direct address uses the direct case. The simplest
analysis is that direct address already identifies its referent at the
speech-act level and therefore does not enter the ordinary anchoring opposition.

\paragraph{Generic and kind-denoting NPs.}
As noted in §\,\ref{sec:nm-def-semantics}, [\textsc{+anch}] is incompatible with
the generic reading. Generics are expressed by the bare noun because they denote
a class or kind rather than a discourse anchored token.



\subsection{Double-marking: demonstrative + \ird{-to}}
\label{sec:nm-def-double}

A demonstrative may co-occur with the anchoring suffix, as in
\ird{tak bylo} \trsl{this very child; this particular child here}
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-def}). Whereas the demonstrative contributes deixis, the
suffix contributes anchoring, so their combination is typically more emphatic,
resumptive, contrastive, or strongly individuating than the plain demonstrative
phrase.

The theoretical analysis adopted here is that demonstratives occupy a
\synhead{DemP} above \synhead{AnchP} (§\,\ref{sec:nm-dem}). Co-occurrence is
grammatical because the two layers are distinct; it is marked because the noun
phrase is being both deictically located and additionally singled out as the
anchored discourse token.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla tak byl//
\glb \Dem{}.\Prox{} child.\Dir{}//
\glft \trsl{this child}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla tak bylo//
\glb \Dem{}.\Prox{} child.\Dir{}.\Def{}//
\glft \trsl{this very child; this particular child here}//
\endgl
\xe

\section{Demonstratives and quantification}
\label{sec:nm-upper}

\subsection{The demonstrative system and noun-internal DemP}
\label{sec:nm-dem}

Iridian demonstratives form a three-way deictic system with proximal, medial,
and distal values. The same paradigm has three distinct morphosyntactic
realisations: an adnominal demonstrative, a pronominal demonstrative, and an
indirect-case possessive demonstrative. The noun-internal analysis developed
here concerns the adnominal realisation, namely \synhead{DemP}, but the wider
demonstrative system must be acknowledged because the same case-inflecting
paradigm is also used pronominally and possessively; see §\,\ref{sec:nm-dem}
for the fuller descriptive discussion and §\,\ref{sec:nm-np-structure} for the
overt noun-phrase order.

\noindent Within the noun phrase, the adnominal demonstrative realises
\synhead{DemP}, the outermost noun-internal demonstrative projection above
\synhead{AnchP}. The hierarchy required by the overt ordering facts is therefore
\synhead{DemP} $>$ \synhead{AnchP} $>$ \synhead{KP} $>$ \synhead{PossP} $>$

\textsc{poss}.\Ind{} $>$ \textsc{head} given in §\,\ref{sec:nm-np-structure}.
Demonstratives stand outside the possessive grouping, whereas possessors remain
inside the \synhead{KP}/\synhead{NP} domain. The co-occurrence facts in
§\,\ref{sec:nm-dem}, including \ird{tuto že tom}, show directly that
demonstratives and possessors cannot be analysed as occupants of one and the
same structural slot.

\begin{forest}

The core semantics of the demonstrative layer is deictic: proximal indexes a
referent near the speaker, medial a referent near the addressee, and distal a
referent away from both. The topic-proximal, addressee-linked medial, and
obviative or backgrounded distal uses described in §\,\ref{sec:nm-dem} are
pragmatic extensions of those lexical deictic values, not replacements for them.
The formal analysis therefore keeps lexical deixis and discourse tendency
distinct.

Because \synhead{DemP} and \synhead{AnchP} are distinct layers, the relation
between a demonstrative and \ird{-to} is one of marked layering rather than true
exclusion. A plain demonstrative phrase is structurally unmarked. A
demonstrative plus anchoring suffix yields a marked emphatic, resumptive,
contrastive, or strongly individuating interpretation.

\paragraph{Indirect-case demonstratives as possessors.}
The same demonstrative paradigm also feeds possessive syntax. The indirect forms
\ird{ten}, \ird{taly}, and \ird{jení} may occupy the ordinary possessor slot
before the noun, as in \ird{ten dum}. In such forms the demonstrative is not the
outer deictic determiner of the noun phrase; it is an indirect-case possessor
inside \synhead{PossP}. The grammar therefore allows two distinct noun-internal
positions fed by the same demonstrative system: adnominal \synhead{DemP} above
\synhead{AnchP}, and demonstrative possessors inside \synhead{PossP}.

\begin{center}
\begin{forest}
  syntax tree

          [{\synhead{NP}\\\textit{head noun}}]
        ]
        [{K\textsuperscript{0}\\{\footnotesize case}}]
      ]
      [{Anch\textsuperscript{0}\\\ird{-to} / $\varnothing$}]
    ]
  ]
\end{forest}
\end{center}

\subsection{Pronominal demonstratives and third-person reference}
\label{sec:nm-dem-pron}

Iridian lacks independent third-person personal pronouns. The full demonstrative
paradigm therefore serves as the ordinary third-person referential series
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-dem}). This pronominal use is not identical to noun-internal
\synhead{DemP}, because no lexical head noun is present inside the expression,
but it is derived from the same underlying demonstrative system and must be kept
distinct from the noun-internal ordering facts summarised in
§\,\ref{sec:nm-np-structure}.

The descriptive discourse tendencies can be compressed as follows. Proximal
forms commonly track the current topic or most accessible referent, medial forms
a secondary or addressee-linked referent, and distal forms a backgrounded,
contrastive, or obviative referent. In formal terms, these effects are best
treated as interpretive extensions available to pronominal demonstratives, not
as separate lexical paradigms (§\,\ref{sec:nm-dem}).

\ex
\begingl
\gla Tak vrbnik, tuto spasnik, je těsnik.//
\glb \Prox{}.\Dir{} stand.up-\Pf{}, \Med{}.\Dir{} sit.down-\Pf{}, \Dist{}.\Dir{} leave-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{He stood up, he sat down, that one left.}//
\endgl
\xe

\subsection{The reduced anaphoric series}
\label{sec:nm-dem-reduced}

Alongside the full paradigm, Iridian has a reduced anaphoric series
\ird{i}/\ird{ji}/\ird{ni}, corresponding to distal
\ird{je}/\ird{jaky}/\ird{jení}. These forms are restricted to pronominal use,
only when the referent is already highly accessible in the discourse, and they
are excluded as ordinary adnominal demonstratives of the type described in
§\,\ref{sec:nm-np-structure}.

Semantically and pragmatically, the reduced series signals weak anaphoric
resumption rather than full deictic pointing. Formally, it belongs to the
demonstrative system, but it is not the spellout of noun-internal
\synhead{DemP}. A natural DM treatment is to analyse \ird{i}/\ird{ji}/\ird{ni}
as specialised pronominal Vocabulary Items competing with the full distal series
under a discourse-anaphoric high-accessibility condition; see again
§\,\ref{sec:nm-dem}.

\pex
\a \ird{je} \trsl{that one; fully deictic distal}
\a \ird{i} \trsl{he/she; weak anaphoric resumption}
\xe


\subsection{Number morphology and the plural paradigm}
\label{sec:nm-num}

Iridian encodes number morphologically through fused case-number endings rather
than through a dedicated number particle. Each declension class provides six
distinct exponents, covering the three structural cases in both singular and
plural values, with no independent morpheme reserved for plurality alone. This
pattern is cross-linguistically common: compare Latin nominative-plural
\textit{-ae} versus accusative-singular \textit{-am}, where number and case
values are similarly inseparable.

In Distributed Morphology terms (Halle \& Marantz 1993; Embick \& Noyer 2001),
Num\textsuperscript{0} is syntactically projected and carries the feature
[\textsc{+pl}] when the noun phrase denotes a plurality. At PF, Morphological
Merger applies obligatorily between Num\textsuperscript{0} and the adjacent
K\textsuperscript{0}, fusing them into a single [K$+$Num] node. The fused node
is spelled out by a single Vocabulary Item from each class's six-slot inventory.
Number semantics is thus recoverable from the form of the case-number ending as
a whole, not from any dedicated substring.

Several structural observations follow from the paradigm. First, syncretisms
are systematic and class-specific. In Class III, the plural direct is
phonologically identical to the singular transitive (\ird{travy}); the two
slots are syntactically distinguishable by the argument structure of the clause
and by context. In Class V, the plural transitive is identical to the singular
direct (\ird{rán}); again, the positions are formally distinguished by their
syntactic licensor. The syncretisms belong to the class-specific inventory of
case-number exponents and do not imply identity of syntactic case.

Second, plural indirect endings differ in vowel quality and quantity:
\ird{-ac}, \ird{-em}, \ird{-ám}, \ird{-ím}, \ird{-om}, and \ird{-ovim}.
Each class realises the [ind.pl] slot with its own exponent; the root grade is
selected independently and cannot be inferred from the ending vowel alone.

Third, Class VI plurals employ an extended stem in \ird{-ov-} (\ird{pětove},
\ird{pětóv}, \ird{pětovim}). The extension has a long-vowel grade in the
transitive plural; its distribution is fixed by the case-number construction,
while the root vowel remains unchanged. This is a lexical microclass rather
than a productive raising of the root before every plural suffix.

\paragraph{Anchored plural forms.}
Anchoring applies to plural forms on the same structural principles as to
singular forms: \textsc{Anch\textsuperscript{0}}[\textsc{+anch,+shr}] is
realised by \ird{-to}/\ird{-te} in V-final environments and by \ird{-o}/\ird{-e}
in C-final environments. Class~I \ird{byl}/\ird{bylo} and
\ird{byle}/\ird{bylete} illustrate reduced and full anchoring at consonantal
and vocalic boundaries, respectively. In Class~III, zero-ended plural transitive
\ird{tráv} and \ird{stráz} take reduced front \ird{-e}, while vocalic
\ird{travy} and \ird{strazy} take full \ird{-te}. Anchoring preserves the
quantity selected by case-number in every cell of
Table~\ref{tab:nm-def-pl-classiii}.

\paragraph{Singulative requirement under cardinals.}
Cardinal numerals require the noun to appear in its \emph{singular} form,
regardless of the quantity expressed. This is the cardinal-singulative pattern,
typologically paralleled by Hungarian, Finnish, and Turkish. The numeral phrase
as a whole denotes a plurality, but the locus of that plurality is the cardinal
head, not the noun: the noun's case-number slot is realised by the singular
Vocabulary Item.

\pex
\a \ird{hroná lobek} \trsl{three apples} \hfill\textit{(cardinal $+$ \textsc{sg})}
\a \ird{hroná lubce} \hfill\textit{(cardinal $+$ \textsc{pl}, ungrammatical)}
\xe

\noindent In DM terms, the fused [K$+$Num] head inside a cardinally-quantified
\synhead{DP} is spelled out by the singular Vocabulary Item: the Q head
(\synhead{QP}) saturates the number semantics of the expression, so
Num\textsuperscript{0} within \synhead{KP} receives no [+pl] feature to trigger
a plural VI. The distribution of plural morphology across nominal contexts is
therefore:

\medskip
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
{\sc context} & {\sc number on noun} \\
\midrule
Unquantified count noun (free plural) & \textsc{pl} \\
Definite group / discourse-prominent plural & \textsc{pl} \\
Kind-denoting / generic plural & \textsc{pl} \\
Collective or aggregate noun & \textsc{pl} \\
Cardinal-quantified noun & \textsc{sg} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\medskip

\paragraph{Number and count-mass distinctions.}
Outside the cardinal context, plural morphology is most natural with count nouns.
With mass nouns the plural forms are ordinarily excluded unless a covert measure
noun is understood. A small set of pluralia-tantum nouns, especially names of
paired body parts and paired objects, resist ordinary pluralisation because
the lexical entry already denotes the pair; such items use plural morphology
as their citation form.

\subsection{Quantifiers, numerals, and the prenominal quantificational
  field}
\label{sec:nm-quant}

Iridian places numerals and quantifiers prenominally. They belong to the upper
nominal field, but the presently available evidence does not force a
fine-grained cartographic split among cardinals, adnominal interrogatives, and
universal quantifiers or negative indefinites. I therefore treat them conservatively as
occupants of a single quantificational modifier field between the demonstrative
domain and the lower \synhead{NP}-internal material.

Numerals occupy the prenominal quantificational field and exhibit a decisive
morphological property: they require the noun to appear in the \emph{singular}
case-number form (§\,\ref{sec:nm-num}). The cardinal head is itself the locus
of plurality for the whole expression; the noun inside the quantified DP
receives no [+pl] feature and is therefore spelled out with the singular
Vocabulary Item. This singulative requirement applies to all cardinals
regardless of value.

\pex
\a \ird{hroná lobek} \trsl{three apples} \hfill (\textsc{sg} under cardinal)
\a \ird{ona pive štava} \trsl{one mug of beer}
\xe

\noindent The exact-cardinality interpretation is thus delivered by the numeral
alone, with no contribution from plural morphology on the noun. Approximate
readings arise contextually through intonation and pragmatic hedging, not
through any morphological operation. The separation of \synhead{QP} from the
fused [K$+$Num] layer is preserved: when the \synhead{QP} head is a cardinal, it
supplies the plurality feature; the fused [K$+$Num] slot on the noun is spelled
out with the singular VI because Num\textsuperscript{0} within \synhead{KP}
carries no independent [+pl] feature in that configuration.

Adnominal interrogatives pattern with the same prenominal field. \ird{Jak}
selects from a contextually restricted set, whereas the other interrogatives
simply introduce the relevant open variable. In determiner use they modify the
noun phrase directly rather than occupying the clause-level \emph{wh}-position
themselves.

\pex
\a \ird{jede ruščevní svirkošc} \trsl{what Russian author}
\a \ird{jak ruščevní svirkošc} \trsl{which Russian author}
\xe

Universal quantifiers and negative indefinites can likewise be adnominal: \ird{nět študent}
\trsl{all students}, \ird{niže jec} \trsl{all dogs}, \ird{nách študent}
\trsl{each student}, \ird{žé tóm} \trsl{not one book}. Negative indefinites,
including pronominal \ird{deno} \trsl{nothing}, require clausal negative
licensing (§\,\ref{sec:nm-negative-concord}); their adnominal use restricts
the existential variable to the following noun's denotation. Negative
existence is expressed by the existential predicate
(§\,\ref{sec:neh}), whose suppletive \ird{zad-} realises the same clausal
polarity dependency.


\subsection{Speaker-anchoring: the proclitic \ird{si}}
\label{sec:nm-spec}

A bare nominal leaves its referential interpretation to the construction and
context, whereas \ird{si} explicitly presents a particular referent selected
by the speaker. The suffixal anchor \ird{-to}/\ird{-te} instead marks that
referent as accessible within the shared discourse. These three possibilities
are represented as alternative realisations of Anch\textsuperscript{0}:

\medskip
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{0.88\textwidth}{l l L}
  \toprule
  \textsc{feature bundle} & \textsc{exponent} & \textsc{description} \\
  \midrule
  {[+anch, +shr]} & \ird{-to} / \ird{-te} & suffixal; shared-discourse anchoring \\
  {[+anch, \textminus{}shr]} & \ird{si} & proclitic; speaker-selected, speaker-identifiable referent \\
  {[\textminus{}anch]} & $\varnothing$ & no overt anchoring; contextual reference \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\medskip

\noindent In the speaker-anchored expression, the speaker has a particular
individual or group in mind without presenting its identity as already shared
with the addressee. The feature [\textsc{\textminus{}shr}] concerns this
source of anchoring; it does not assert that the addressee is incapable of
identifying the referent independently. Speaker identifiability is an
established dimension of indefinite interpretation: Kagan's analysis of
Russian distinguishes it from free-choice effects and narrow scope relative
to a sentence-level operator \cite{kagan2011}. The Iridian analysis uses that
distinction without assigning \ird{si} a fixed quantifier scope.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla byl//
\glb child.\Dir{}//
\glft \trsl{a child; the child; child} \lingcontext{interpretation supplied by context}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla si byl//
\glb \Sanch{} child.\Dir{}//
\glft \trsl{a certain child (that I have in mind)}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla bylo//
\glb child.\Dir{}.\Def{}//
\glft \trsl{the child} \lingcontext{explicit shared anchoring}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent The bare noun can refer to a particular child known to the speaker,
to a child whose identity is unspecified, or to the kind in an appropriate
generic construction. Neither speaker knowledge nor ignorance is encoded by
the absence of an anchor. In \ird{si byl}, speaker selection is overt, but
\ird{si} remains optional even when the speaker can identify the child.
First-mention introductions therefore permit a useful distinction: a bare
nominal introduces a child without a conventional commitment about the
speaker's knowledge, while \ird{si byl} presents the speaker as having a
particular child in mind. Shared \ird{bylo} asks the addressee to recover the
child through the common discourse model.

The ordinary incompatibility of \ird{si} with suffixal \ird{-to}/\ird{-te}
is represented by their alternative feature specifications on the same Anch
head. Vocabulary Insertion selects the speaker-anchored proclitic or the
shared-anchoring suffix according to that specification. The independent
demonstrative layer does not itself make every combination felicitous: where
a demonstrative identifies the same referent for the addressee, its
combination with speaker-specific \ird{si} has no established interpretation.

\subsubsection{Nominal-domain linearisation and prosodic attachment}
\label{sec:nm-si-linearisation}

\ird{Si} precedes the material of its nominal expression, while a structurally
external plural \ird{ne} remains before it. The suffixal anchor instead follows
the case-number form. The grammar derives this asymmetry through a
feature-conditioned linearisation instruction: the speaker-anchored terminal
receives precedence at the edge of its nominal domain before Vocabulary
Insertion supplies \ird{si}. Its syntactic position and interpretation remain
those of Anch\textsuperscript{0}. The distinction between a clitic's
structurally defined positioning domain and its phonological host has an
independent precedent in Klavans's treatment of clitic dominance, precedence,
and liaison \cite[§§\,1.2--1.3]{klavans1985}; the particular domain and
instruction below constitute the analysis of Iridian.

For an anchoring terminal $a$, let $D_a$ be the maximal extended projection of
the lexical noun whose case-number layer is selected by $a$. Following that
noun's projection upward includes its \synhead{AnchP}, quantificational field,
and \synhead{DemP}, where present, but stops below a structurally external
\synhead{NumP} headed by \ird{ne}. A larger nominal in which this expression is a possessor, a governing
postpositional phrase, and a containing clause lie outside $D_a$. A possessor therefore
has its own domain and anchoring terminal, although its ordered terminal
sequence occurs as a dependent inside the host noun's domain. This definition uses the
nominal spine to identify ownership; it does not identify a domain by whichever
noun or clitic happens to occur first in the string.

The PF Merger of number with K precedes linearisation. PF then orders
embedded nominal domains before the domain containing them, retaining each
completed dependent as an ordered block. Within $D_a$, the
default order preserves the established precedence of demonstratives,
quantificational material, possessors, and the head noun, with K and Anch
following their complements. If this default sequence is
$\alpha\mathbin{\frown}\langle a\rangle\mathbin{\frown}\beta$, and $a$
bears [\textsc{+anch,\,\textminus{}shr}], nominal-domain linearisation assigns:
\[
  \operatorname{Lin}(D_a)
    = \langle a\rangle\mathbin{\frown}\alpha\mathbin{\frown}\beta.
\]
Here $\frown$ is concatenation, while $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are ordered
sequences that may contain completed dependent domains. The instruction
reorders the single occurrence of the terminal belonging to this nominal
spine; every other precedence relation is retained. An anchoring terminal
inside a possessor remains within that possessor's block, and $a$ receives no
second pronunciation at its syntactic source. A structurally external
\ird{ne} is subsequently concatenated before $\operatorname{Lin}(D_a)$;
\ird{ne} inside a dependent or lower nominal constituent retains its internal
position (§\,\ref{sec:nm-poss-ne}).

Vocabulary Insertion follows this ordering step, realising $a$ as \ird{si}.
The corresponding [\textsc{+anch,+shr}] terminal is subject to ordinary
head-final linearisation and is realised by the suffix selected adjacent to
the case-number form (§\,\ref{sec:nm-def-nondirect}); null anchoring receives
no overt exponent. Thus the feature-conditioned instruction changes neither
case-number exponence nor the local conditioning of suffixal allomorphy.
In the following schemata, $N$--K stands for the root and fused case-number
terminal before Vocabulary Insertion, while $M$ contains the ordered
prenominal material, including any completed possessor. The final strings
abbreviate the phonological realisation of that material:
\[
\begin{aligned}
  [_{D_a}\;M\;N\text{--K}\;a_{[+\mathrm{anch},-\mathrm{shr}]}]
    &\longmapsto a\;M\;N\text{--K}
      \longmapsto \textit{si}\;M\;N\text{--K},\\
  [_{D_a}\;M\;N\text{--K}\;a_{[+\mathrm{anch},+\mathrm{shr}]}]
    &\longmapsto M\;N\text{--K}\;a
      \longmapsto M\;N\text{--K--}\textit{anch}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The first arrow represents linearisation and the second Vocabulary Insertion;
\textit{anch} abbreviates the appropriate suffixal allomorph. The minimal
speaker-anchored derivations yield the established \ird{si byl} and
\ird{si byle}, while their shared-anchoring counterparts yield \ird{bylo}
and \ird{bylete}. The variable $M$ specifies the placement instruction for
otherwise licensed nominal material, without establishing new combinations
of \ird{si} with demonstratives or quantifiers.

The instruction belongs to the mapping from nominal structure to PF
precedence, rather than to syntactic head movement. It therefore leaves the
hierarchy interpreted at LF, including the source of speaker anchoring,
unchanged. It also supplements the ordinary linearisation rules rather than
applying Local Dislocation across an intervening string: in the Distributed
Morphology account of Embick and Noyer, Local Dislocation is restricted to
linear adjacency \cite{embicknoyer2007}. No succession of adjacent swaps or
additional nominal head is required by the Iridian rule.

Prosodification applies to the resulting string. \ird{Si} and plural \ird{ne}
are unstressed proclitics outside the minimal lexical prosodic word
(§\,\ref{sec:stress}); their host is the following independently stressed
word. In \ird{si byl} and \ird{si byle}, the noun retains its initial lexical
stress. Where prenominal material intervenes, the first independent word in
that material supplies the host, which may belong to a possessor even though
that possessor has its own anchoring domain. The dependent's order is protected
during linearisation, but its initial word remains available for prosodic
attachment. Structural ownership, edge precedence, and phonological attachment
therefore contribute separately to the pronunciation of the nominal phrase.

A speaker-anchored nominal may establish a new topic, occur as a focused or
backgrounded argument, or introduce an individual in an existential
presentation. Its subsequent omission depends on recoverability in the
continuing discourse, rather than on the presence of \ird{si} at first
mention (§§\,\ref{sec:tc-ordinary-topics}, \ref{sec:tc-topicless},
\ref{sec:tc-topic-drop}). The contrast between speaker selection and
aboutness is thus preserved when the same nominal expression receives
different clausal interpretations.

\subsection{Personal pronouns as full DPs}
\label{sec:nm-pron-personal}

First- and second-person pronouns are best treated as fully projected DPs whose
nominal content is supplied by person features rather than by a lexical noun
root. They inflect for the same three productive cases as lexical nouns; their
indirect forms serve as oblique possessors in the analytic possessive
construction described in §\,\ref{sec:nm-poss}. Iridian lacks a separate
third-person personal-pronoun paradigm; that function is taken over by the
demonstrative system described in
§§\,\ref{sec:nm-dem}--\ref{sec:nm-dem-reduced}.

Wh-hood ---
the property of introducing an open variable --- is a lexical or
\synhead{DP}-internal feature of the interrogatives: they are operators
that may occupy argument or adjunct positions inside the clause. Questionhood
proper, by contrast, is clausal and is located in \synhead{PolP}: the clause is
typed as a question by the Pol\textsuperscript{0} head (overt \ird{by} or null),
not by the presence of a wh-operator in Spec,\synhead{FocP} (see
§\,\ref{sec:by-pol-head} and §\,\ref{sec:th-wh-interrogation} in
Chapter~\ref{ch:simple}). A wh-expression may remain in its base argument or
adjunct position (in situ) or move to Spec,\synhead{FocP} for marked focus;
neither option alone determines whether the clause is interrogative. The same
interrogative nominals listed in Table~\ref{tab:nm-interrogatives} appear both
in matrix content questions and in embedded non-assertive \ird{poby}-complements
(§\,\ref{sec:poby} of Chapter~\ref{ch:complex}), where they function as
wh-operators inside an issue-denoting embedded clause rather than as
direct-question operators.

\paragraph{Strict negative concord.}
\label{sec:nm-negative-concord}
\ird{Deno} \trsl{nothing}, \ird{zide} \trsl{no one}, \ird{žena}
\trsl{nowhere}, \ird{žemě} \trsl{never}, and adnominal \ird{žé}
\trsl{not one} obligatorily co-occur with the appropriate realisation of
clausal negation in an ordinary finite clause, irrespective of their surface
position. Their co-occurrence expresses a single semantic negation, giving
Iridian \term{strict negative concord} in the distributional sense described
by Giannakidou \cite{giannakidou2006}. Indicative verbal predicates require
\ird{za-}, equatives require \ird{staly}, and existential predicates require
\ird{zad-}; a negative non-indicative clause instead uses its dedicated Mood
exponent (§\,\ref{sec:vm-neg-exponents}; §\,\ref{sec:vm-moodp}). A
negative indefinite cannot itself replace any of these markers.

\pex\label{ex:nm-negative-concord}
\a
\begingl
\gla Deno že zastráda.//
\glb nothing.\Dir{} 1\Sg{}.\Ind{} \Neg{}-lack-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Nothing is lacking to me; I lack nothing.}//
\endgl
\a \ljudge{*}\ird{Deno že stráda.}
\lingcontext{intended: nothing is lacking to me; unlicensed negative indefinite}
\xe

\noindent In this construction, \ird{strád-} takes a direct-case theme and
an indirect-case experiencer, so that \ird{deno} supplies the theme and
\ird{že} the experiencer. The continuous \ird{-a} supplies no future tense;
the rendering \trsl{I shall not want} is available in a context that supports
a prospective interpretation. Abstracting away from aspect, the clause has
the interpretation $\neg\exists x\,[\operatorname{thing}(x)\land
\operatorname{lack}(x,\mathrm{me})]$. Initial \ird{deno} remains within the
scope of negation at interpretation in the lower argument domain
(§\,\ref{sec:tc-quantificational-pivots}).

The same licensing requirement holds with the other predicate types and
with negative Mood:

\pex\label{ex:nm-concord-predication}
\a
\begingl
\gla Zide doktor staly.//
\glb no.one.\Dir{} doctor.\Dir{} \NegCop{}//
\glft \trsl{No one is a doctor.} \lingcontext{equative concord}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Deno zada.//
\glb nothing.\Dir{} \NegExst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Nothing exists.} \lingcontext{existential concord}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Deno piašté.//
\glb nothing.\Dir{} eat-\Proh{}//
\glft \trsl{Do not eat anything.} \lingcontext{prohibitive concord; null addressee}//
\endgl
\xe

The grammar analyses these expressions as existential indefinites bearing
an uninterpretable feature $[u\textsc{neg}]$, licensed by the $[i\textsc{neg}]$ feature of a
clause-local covert negative operator. This adopts the syntactic agreement
approach developed by Penka and Zeijlstra \cite{penka2011,zeijlstra2022}, while preserving the
distinction between licensing and Vocabulary Insertion: the indefinite and
the negative predicate morphology participate in the same dependency, whose
semantic negation is supplied once by the operator. Multiple Agree permits
several $[u\textsc{neg}]$ expressions to share that licenser \cite{penka2012}. Thus the schematic sequence
\ird{zide \ldots{} deno \ldots{} za-V}, with each argument assigned its
appropriate case, expresses one negation over two existential variables;
additional concord items do not introduce further negative operators. The
existential treatment is an analysis of Iridian, rather than a definition of
all cross-linguistic n-words: Giannakidou contrasts
existential and universal treatments \cite{giannakidou2006}.

The licensing domain is the local finite clause. A negative operator in a
matrix clause does not license a negative indefinite inside a finite
complement (§\,\ref{sec:clausal-negation} of
Chapter~\ref{ch:simple}; Chapter~\ref{ch:complex}). Interrogative \ird{by}
and non-assertive complement marking do not supply $[i\textsc{neg}]$, so that questionhood
or non-assertion alone cannot license a member of the negative series.
Ordinary indefinites and the universal series lack $[u\textsc{neg}]$ and do not enter
this concord dependency; the wider distribution of polarity-sensitive
indefinites is a separate descriptive question.

Short answers such as \ird{deno} or \ird{zide} are analysed as remnants of an
elliptical negative clause whose operator and predicate morphology are
recoverable. Their negative interpretation therefore does not license an
independent negative-quantifier use in a fully pronounced affirmative clause.
Genuine logical double negation requires two semantic negative operators,
ordinarily in distinct clauses or propositions; the combination of a negative
indefinite with its local predicate negative cannot produce it.

\pex
\a \ird{jede bych ščenžkou?} \trsl{who arrived yesterday?}
\a \ird{jak na ne ruščevní svirkošta} \trsl{which of these Russian authors}
\a \ird{nět študent} \trsl{all students}
\a \ird{zide byl} \trsl{no child} \lingcontext{adnominal phrase; requires clausal negative licensing}
\xe

\section{Possessors, the indirect case, and the \synhead{PossP} layer}
\label{sec:nm-poss}

\subsection{The oblique possessor in the DP}
\label{sec:nm-poss-struct}

Iridian expresses possession by means of an oblique (indirect-case) noun phrase
in prenominal position (§\,\ref{sec:nm-ext-indirect}). The oblique possessor
\ird{Marcí} in \ird{Marcí dum} \trsl{Marek's house} occupies a structural
position immediately above the head \synhead{NP} and inside the \synhead{KP}
domain, below \synhead{AnchP} and below noun-internal \synhead{DemP}. In terms
of the \synhead{DP} hierarchy, this is the prenominal dependent position of
\synhead{PossP}.

The indirect case of the possessor is not a special possessive case but the
language's general non-core case, deployed here as it is throughout the grammar
for dependents that are not the main absolutive argument. There is no separate
structural Case assignment operation for possessors: the possessor's indirect
case arises from the same K head that assigns case to other indirect-case NPs in
the clause. The structural uniformity of the indirect case across nominal and
clausal environments is a design feature of the grammar, not an accident.

\begin{center}
\begin{forest}
  syntax tree
  [\synhead{AnchP}
    [\synhead{KP}
      [\synhead{PossP}
        [{\textit{oblique possessor}.\Ind{}}]
        [{\synhead{NP}\\\textit{head noun}}]
      ]
      [{K\textsuperscript{0}\\{\footnotesize case ending}}]
    ]
    [{Anch\textsuperscript{0}\\\ird{-to} / $\varnothing$}]
  ]
\end{forest}
\end{center}

\noindent The possessor appears before the head noun in the surface string,
consistent with head-final base-generation of specifiers. The entire possessive
phrase [\synhead{PossP} oblique-N head-N] forms a constituent that is then
inflected as a whole for case by K and optionally for anchoring by
\textsc{Anch\textsuperscript{0}}.

\subsection{Distribution of \ird{ne} across the possessive DP}
\label{sec:nm-poss-ne}

The plural clitic \ird{ne} has two attachment sites within a possessive
\synhead{DP}, yielding different scope interpretations. In the following
schematic pair, plural possessors are several people named Marek:

\pex
\a \ird{ne Marcí tóm} --- \trsl{the Mareks' book} (possessors
plural; book possibly singular)
\a \ird{Marcí ne tóm} --- \trsl{Marek's books} (book plural;
possessor singular)
\xe

\noindent In the first phrase, \ird{ne} marks the plurality of the
possessors expressed by \ird{Marcí}; in the second it marks the plurality of
the possessed books. These interpretations distinguish the possessor's nominal
domain from the lower head-noun domain within the possessive construction.
During PF linearisation, \ird{ne} belonging to a possessor remains inside that
possessor's ordered block, while \ird{ne} in the lower head-noun position
retains that internal position. Neither is identified as external merely
because it precedes a noun: the external ordering \ird{ne} $>$ \ird{si}
applies only when the \synhead{NumP} headed by \ird{ne} lies outside the
relevant $D_a$ (§\,\ref{sec:nm-si-linearisation}).

\section{The head-final \synhead{DP}: word order and phrase structure}
\label{sec:nm-np-structure}

\subsection{The functional sequence and linear order}
\label{sec:nm-funcseq}

The canonical linear order of the Iridian noun phrase —

\begin{center}
\ird{ne} $>$ \ird{si} $>$ \textsc{dem} $>$ \textsc{quant/num} $>$
\textsc{poss}.\Ind{} $>$ \textsc{prop.root / stative / -ový} $>$
\textsc{head} + \textsc{case} + \ird{-to}
\end{center}

— combines the functional hierarchy in Table~\ref{tab:nm-projections} with
the placement of prenominal exponents. The sequence records relative slots,
whose interpretation also constrains which combinations are available
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-spec}). The suffixal morphology follows the head-final
principle, while Anch[\textsc{+anch,\,\textminus{}shr}] receives precedence
at the edge of $D_a$ before it is realised as \ird{si}
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-si-linearisation}).
The rightward drift of functional morphology (case suffix, then anchoring suffix) corresponds
to the innerward drift of those same heads in the syntactic projection: the case
head K\textsuperscript{0} is lower than \textsc{Anch\textsuperscript{0}}, but
its suffix appears closer to the root because the root is the complement that
K\textsuperscript{0} takes, and in a head-final system the complement precedes
the head.

Prenominal position consequently does not identify syntactic height in every
case: \ird{si} precedes material whose projections dominate
\synhead{AnchP}. The linear sequence and the head-final suffixal spine must
therefore be distinguished from the hierarchy of semantic scope.

The prenominal left field contains plural \ird{ne}, speaker-anchoring
\ird{si}, demonstratives, and quantificational material, while possessors stand
closer to the noun and property roots, stative modifiers, and \ird{-ový}
phrases immediately precede the head. The linearisation instruction fixes the position of \ird{si} relative to
this material and to a structurally external \ird{ne}; it does not determine
the finer ordering of every combination within the quantificational field.

\pex
\a \ird{tak študencí tóm} \trsl{this student's book}
\a \ird{jak na ne ruščevní svirkošta} \trsl{which of these Russian authors}
\xe

\subsection{Stative verbs and property roots as \synhead{NP} modifiers}
\label{sec:nm-prop}

Iridian's primary attributive modification strategy deploys stative verbs in the
dedicated attributive form in \ird{-á} directly before the head noun. The small
set of lexical property roots that feed those stative verbs are analysed as
heads of a \synhead{PropP} projection that immediately precedes the \synhead{NP}
and is itself immediately preceded by the \synhead{PossP} layer.

The head-final principle correctly predicts that all prenominal modifiers —
whether stative-verb-based or property-root-based — appear before the head noun,
never after it. Postnominal modification is not a productive construction in
standard Iridian; the prenominal restriction on modifiers is a direct
consequence of the head-final parameter.

\pex
\a \ird{mrocná nuž} \trsl{a beautiful city}
\a \ird{hradná byl} \trsl{a good child}
\a \ird{svorná dum} \trsl{a big house}
\xe

\noindent This \ird{-á} form is restricted to adnominal attributive use within
the \synhead{DP}. Clause-level manner modification is expressed instead by the
derived adverbs in \ird{-ě} discussed in §\,\ref{sec:stative-adv} of
Chapter~\ref{ch:verbs}; the attributive itself is not extended to ordinary
adverbial use.

\subsection{The \ird{-ový} attributive and its embedded DP}
\label{sec:nm-ovni}

The relative head \ird{-ový} selects the same voice--aspect
configuration as a participant nominal in \ird{-ou}, but forms a prenominal
modifier of an overt noun (§\,\ref{sec:th-relativization}). Its
\synhead{RelP} contains a silent operator linked to the privileged participant
gap in the embedded predicate; abstraction over that position supplies the
property intersected with the head noun's denotation. The modifier precedes the
noun, and its suffix does not agree with the noun in case.

The embedded configuration retains viewpoint and participant orientation,
while nominal relational heads license indirect-case thematic dependents
independently of matrix case. Thus perfective \ird{piaščikový} describes an
agent and \ird{vednikový} a patient. The head noun receives case from its
matrix clause or, when it is itself a dependent within another relative,
from that containing nominal domain. It does not receive the case that its
silent counterpart would bear in a finite clause.

\pex
\a \ird{lubcí piaščikový pres} \trsl{the man who ate the apple}
\a \ird{[Jancí vednikový lubcí] piaščikový pres}
   \trsl{the man who ate the apple that Janek saw}
\xe

The operator in each relative corresponds to the participant privileged by its
own voice--aspect configuration. In the nested example, \ird{Jancí} is an
agent dependent of patient-oriented \ird{vednikový}, whereas \ird{lubcí},
modified by that relative, is the theme dependent of agent-oriented
\ird{piaščikový}. Neither relative assigns matrix case to \ird{pres}.
The exact attachment of \synhead{RelP} relative to possessive material remains
undetermined; its prenominal position and independent case domain do not depend
on that choice. Continuous statives instead use the dedicated \ird{-á}
attributive (§\,\ref{sec:stative-attr}); relatives selecting \synhead{AspP}
exclude internal clausal \ird{za-}.

\subsection{The \ird{ty}-supplement}
\label{sec:nm-appos}

Iridian possesses an appositional particle \ird{ty} that introduces a
right-peripheral supplement: a phrase that is coreferent with a preceding
nominal and presents it as a clarification, identification, or elaboration. The
semantics are those of identity---\textit{that is}, \textit{namely}---and the
\ird{ty}-phrase is always postnominal, set off from its antecedent by a prosodic
break.

The \ird{ty}-phrase is not a restrictive modifier. It does not narrow the
denotation of the head noun the way an adjective or relative clause does: a
phrase like \ird{Karel ty mlazka} does not pick out a subset of Karels who are
brothers, it simply adds the information that the referent already identified as
Karel is the speaker's brother. The \ird{ty}-phrase is therefore best analysed
as a \term{supplement}---a nominally-anchored, non-restrictive phrase that is
coreferent with its antecedent but structurally peripheral to it, not integrated
into the \synhead{NP}'s extended projection. This distinguishes \ird{ty}-phrases
categorically from adjectives and relative modifiers, which are adjuncts within
the \synhead{NP} and compositionally restrict the head. Because \ird{ty}-phrases
are supplements rather than adjuncts, they are not governed by the head-final
principle: modifiers fall within the \synhead{NP} and must precede their head;
supplements are peripheral and their position---always to the right of the
antecedent---is determined by information structure and discourse organisation.

The antecedent nominal is introduced first; \ird{ty} then opens the supplement
phrase. When the supplement consists of a bare nominal, no further element is
required: the nominal is itself the head and the identity relation is immediate.

\pex
\a \ird{Karel ty mlazka}
   \trsl{Karel---that is, my brother}
\xe

When the supplement consists of non-nominal material---an adjective, a
\ird{-ový}-relative, a postpositional phrase---a pronominal head is required to
host it. Modifiers in Iridian must precede a nominal head; without one, the
modifier cannot form a well-formed phrase. This head is supplied by the
anaphoric pronoun \ird{i}, which appears at the right edge of the supplement and
corefers with the antecedent nominal.

\pex
\a \ird{nuž ty mrocná i}
   \trsl{the city---that is, the beautiful one}
\a \ird{pres ty že vednikový i}
   \trsl{the man---that is, the one I saw}
\xe

\noindent The internal structure of the \ird{ty}-phrase respects head-finality
throughout: \ird{mrocná} and \ird{že vednikový} precede their head \ird{i} in
the ordinary left-to-right modifier-before-head order. Head-finality is not
suspended in the supplement; it applies within it. The distribution of \ird{i}
follows directly: it is required whenever the \ird{ty}-phrase contains a
non-nominal modifier that needs a nominal head to attach to, and absent when the
\ird{ty}-phrase already contains a nominal head.

Ordinary attributive modification---adjectives, \ird{-ový}-relatives, and
similar prenominal material---remains the unmarked strategy for adnominal
description and carries no special discourse implication. The
\ird{ty}-supplement is marked for deliberate elaborative intent: it presents the
clarification as a distinct informational act, separate from the introduction of
the referent. In spoken language this implicature is weak, since production
pressures naturally favour establishing the referent before specifying it; in
formal and written language the implicature is stronger, and a \ird{ty}-phrase
reads as emphatic, contrastive, or rhetorically foregrounded. The construction
is not excluded from formal writing but is marked there, and is most natural for
heavy adnominal material---long nominalised relative clauses, for
instance---that would be unwieldy in prenominal position.

The full discourse-interactional properties of the \ird{ty}-supplement,
including its use as a repair and afterthought device in spoken interaction, are
treated in Chapter~\ref{ch:spoken} (\S\,\ref{sec:spoken-ty}).

\section{Indirect-case nominal dependence and postpositional phrases}
\label{sec:nm-ext}

\subsection{Material, partitive, source, and other \synhead{NP}-external
  dependencies}
\label{sec:nm-ext-indirect}

The indirect case is the general dependency form of the nominal
system. Possessors are simply its most tightly integrated \synhead{DP}-internal
use. The same K-form appears with material nouns, partitives, source
expressions, and other semantically dependent nominals that are not the
privileged head of the phrase. In this wider domain the indirect does
not mark a special inventory of separate morphological cases; rather, it
is the single oblique realisation of K whose interpretation is narrowed
by the lexical relation between nouns or by the head that selects the
dependent nominal.

\pex
\a \ird{kuní prosc} \trsl{silver spoon}
\a \ird{ona pive štava} \trsl{one mug of beer}
\a \ird{Študencí na hroná} \trsl{three of the students}
\a \ird{Marcí z houba} \trsl{a gift from Marek}
\xe

Material, partitive, and source dependents therefore stand on a
continuum with possessors. The possessor is the most tightly integrated
prenominal use of indirect \synhead{KP} inside the \synhead{DP}. Material and partitive
dependents are looser nominal relations. Postpositional phrases are the
next step outward: the same indirect nominal domain serves as the
complement of a relational head that closes the phrase from the right.

\subsection{Postpositions as heads selecting an indirect nominal
  complement}
\label{sec:nm-postpositions}

Iridian adpositions are head-final and are best analysed as
\term{postpositions}. The constant generalisation is that the nominal
complement bears indirect case. In the smallest representation, the
postposition combines with an indirect \synhead{KP}. In fuller noun phrases, that
\synhead{KP} may itself be embedded inside \synhead{AnchP} or higher nominal layers, but the
selectional requirement remains the same: the nominal core is oblique,
and the postposition closes the complement phrase at its right edge.

The indirect nominal complement combines with a following postposition whose
lexical meaning specifies a spatial, participant, source, privative, or
substitutive relation.

The bound lexical preverbs \ird{de-} and \ird{mně-} share directional and
traversal meanings with postpositional \ird{de} and \ird{mně}, but attach
to a verbal root rather than selecting an indirect nominal complement
(§\,\ref{sec:lexical-preverbs}). Replacement and privation are expressed
by the preverbs \ird{pře-} and \ird{ne-} in verbal derivation, while the
corresponding postpositions remain \ird{pale} and \ird{nam}. These
synchronic relations do not establish a direction of historical derivation.
Accumulating \ird{na-}, as in \ird{navad-} \trsl{synthesise considerations
into a judgement}, likewise belongs to a verbal stem, whereas locative
\ird{na} follows an indirect nominal complement. Their formal resemblance
permits a hypothesis of common origin, but neither that hypothesis nor their
spatial associations identify the prefix with a nominal relational head.
Prefixal accumulation also supplies no comitative participant of the kind
expressed by \ird{še}; any applicative argument depends on the ordinary
verbal derivation rather than on \ird{na-} itself.

\pex
\a \ird{dumě na} \trsl{in the house; at home}
\a \ird{hraďe de} \trsl{into the town; to the town}
\a \ird{Jancí še} \trsl{with Janek}
\a \ird{Marcí z} \trsl{from Marek}
\xe

\subsection{Internal structure of the postpositional phrase}
\label{sec:nm-postp-structure}

The structural asymmetry is straightforward. The nominal complement is
assembled first: the root combines with \synhead{PossP}, receives indirect case in
K, and may then be expanded by anchoring or other higher nominal
material. Only after that nominal phrase is complete does the
postposition merge as the head of the larger postpositional phrase.
Head-finality then yields the surface order \textit{complement} +
\textit{postposition}.

\begin{center}
\begin{forest}
  syntax tree
  [PostP
    [\synhead{AnchP}
      [\synhead{KP}
        [\synhead{PossP}
          [{\textit{oblique possessor} / $\varnothing$}]
          [{\synhead{NP}\\\textit{head noun}}]
        ]
        [{K\textsuperscript{0}\\\Ind{}}]
      ]
      [{Anch\textsuperscript{0}\\\ird{-to} / $\varnothing$}]
    ]
    [{Post\textsuperscript{0}\\\ird{na} / \ird{de} / \ird{še} / \dots}]
  ]
\end{forest}
\end{center}

\noindent The tree is intentionally conservative. What matters is not whether
every postpositional complement is always projected up to \synhead{AnchP}, but
that the complement is at least an indirect \synhead{KP} and that any higher
nominal material remains inside the complement phrase rather than intervening
between the complement and the postposition.

\subsection{Attachment to the right edge of the host phrase}
\label{sec:nm-postp-attachment}

The postposition attaches to the right edge of the governed phrase as a whole,
not to the noun stem in isolation. A bare noun, an anchored noun, a possessed
noun phrase, and a noun phrase with prenominal modifiers all behave alike in
this respect: the nominal material comes first, and the postposition closes the
entire phrase.

\pex
\a \ird{dumě na} \trsl{in the house; at home}
\a \ird{bylamo my} \trsl{for the child}
\a \ird{Marcí dumě na} \trsl{at Marek's house}
\a \ird{svorná mosťe nad} \trsl{over the big bridge}
\xe

\noindent The same logic extends to larger complements. Demonstratives,
possessors, property-root modifiers, stative modifiers, and \ird{-ový} phrases
all belong to the nominal complement if they modify the noun phrase itself. The
postposition does not split them apart or attach to the lexical noun before they
are assembled. This is the chief diagnostic for analyzing the forms as
postpositions over phrasal complements rather than as suffixes on nouns or as
free adverbs loosely associated with them.

\subsection{Semantic classes of postpositions}
\label{sec:nm-postp-classes}

The common postpositions form a compact but semantically varied system. Their
lexical meanings differ, but their syntax is uniform.

\begin{table}[ht]
  \footnotesize
  \caption{Core postpositions and their main semantic classes.}
  \label{tab:nm-postpositions}
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{l l L}
    \toprule
    {\sc class} & {\sc members} & {\sc core relation} \\
    \midrule
    Locative & \ird{na} & location, interiority, surface contact \\
    Goal & \ird{de} & direction, endpoint \\
    Proximity & \ird{u} & nearness \\
    Accompaniment & \ird{še} & accompaniment, comitative association \\
    Benefactive & \ird{my} & benefit, intended recipient, purpose-oriented relation \\
    Source / separation & \ird{z} & source, origin, movement away, extraction \\
    Privative & \ird{nam} & absence \\
    Replacement & \ird{pale} & substitution, `instead' \\
    Exception & \ird{ahte} & exclusion, `except' \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\noindent The core spatial and participant relations are illustrated
below:

\pex
\a \ird{dumě na} \trsl{in the house}
\a \ird{hraďe de} \trsl{to the town}
\a \ird{hraďe u} \trsl{near the town}
\a \ird{bylam my} \trsl{for the child}
\a \ird{Jancí še} \trsl{with Janek}
\a \ird{Marcí z} \trsl{from Marek}
\a \ird{záhárí nam} \trsl{without sugar}
\a \ird{Jancí pale} \trsl{instead of Janek}
\a \ird{Jancí ahte} \trsl{except for Janek}
\xe

\subsection{Specialized spatial and path postpositions}
\label{sec:nm-postp-extended}

Alongside the broad locative and goal markers, Iridian also admits a set of more
specialised spatial and path postpositions. These behave like the core set in
selecting the same indirect nominal complement and in appearing at the right
edge of the phrase.

\pex
\a \ird{stoľe pod} \trsl{under the table}
\a \ird{mosťe nad} \trsl{over the bridge}
\a \ird{domě měz} \trsl{between the houses; among the houses}
\a \ird{hraďe do} \trsl{up to the town}
\a \ird{lesě mně} \trsl{through the forest; via the forest}
\xe

\noindent These extended forms matter analytically because they show that the
postpositional construction is not a tiny lexical quirk tied only to \ird{na}.
The same head-final syntax generalises across broad location, bounded path,
vertical relation, interstitial relation, and traversal.

\subsection{Postpositional phrases in clause structure}
\label{sec:nm-postp-clausal}

The postpositional phrase is not confined to nominal modification. The same
structure appears as a clausal adjunct, as the location complement of
existential predication, and as a dependent nominal expression inside larger
noun phrases. The PP discussion therefore belongs in the nominal chapter because
of its internal structure, but it interfaces directly with clause structure as
well.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek dumě na jeca.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} house.\Ind{} in \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek is at home.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Kroumašťe na pivo s jeca.//
\glb refrigerator.\Ind{} in beer.\Dir{} still \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is still some beer left in the refrigerator.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Bylam my Jancí še stojách.//
\glb child-\Ind{} for Janek-\Ind{} with go-\Ctpl{}//
\glft \trsl{(I am) going for the child with Janek.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Marcí z houba.//
\glb Marek-\Ind{} from gift.\Dir{}//
\glft \trsl{a gift from Marek}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent The existential examples connect directly to §\,\ref{sec:jec} in
Chapter~\ref{ch:simple}: the entity whose existence is asserted stands in the
direct case, while the locative PP contributes the place relation. More broadly,
postpositional phrases behave like ordinary head-final dependent phrases in the
clause. They precede the final predicate and preserve their internal noun-first,
head-last organisation.

\subsection{Analytical status: postpositions, clitics, and orthographic
  practice}
\label{sec:nm-postp-status}

The common postpositions attach prosodically to the preceding independent word
at the right edge of their nominal complement. They remain outside that word's
minimal lexical stress domain and ordinarily bear no primary stress of their
own (§\,\ref{sec:stress}). In \ird{dumě na}, \ird{na} leans on
\ird{dumě}; in \ird{Marcí dumě na}, its immediate host is again
\ird{dumě}, while \ird{Marcí} retains its separate lexical stress. The
syntactic complement is the whole nominal phrase, whereas the immediate
phonological host is its final independent word.

These \term{postposed clitic adpositions} are written separately. Prosodic
attachment neither requires orthographic fusion nor turns the postposition into
a nominal suffix. The construction combines a phrase that bears indirect case
with a head-final relational marker; its weak pronunciation and its phrasal
syntax describe different properties of the same element.
Head-final order and obligatory adjacency to the nominal complement distinguish
this construction from a free clause adverb.

\section{Derived nominals, gerunds, and participant reference}
\label{sec:nm-derived}

\subsection{Event nominals and the \ird{-ou} class}
\label{sec:nm-derived-event}

Event and participant nominals enter the ordinary nominal distribution,
although their internal interpretation and their inflectional specification
must be distinguished. Lexical \ird{piaštou} \trsl{food} has a stored
substance reading; eventive \ird{piaštou} \trsl{eating} permits thematic
dependents; \ird{piaščikou} \trsl{the one who ate} describes a participant
selected by embedded voice and aspect (§\,\ref{sec:nominalised}). Possessive
or demonstrative modification and anchoring apply outside these differences:
none of those operations creates an event argument structure in a lexical noun.

\subsection{Unexpanded and expanded event nominals}
\label{sec:nm-derived-gerund}

An event nominal may occur without overt participants or may be expanded by
indirect-case dependents specifying the actor and theme. These are unexpanded
and expanded uses of the event construction; omission of a dependent does not
turn it into a lexical result noun. The terms do not presuppose that the number
of pronounced dependents diagnoses the theoretical distinction between simple
and complex event nominals.

\pex
\a \ird{piaštou} \trsl{food} \lingcontext{lexical substance noun}
\a \ird{piaštou} \trsl{eating} \lingcontext{unexpanded event nominal}
\a \ird{Marcí travěte piaštou} \trsl{Marek's eating of the bread}
   \lingcontext{expanded event nominal}
\a \ird{Jancí podohletou} \trsl{Janek's remembering / the remembering of Janek}
\xe

The indirect case neutralises the distinction between actor and theme within
the nominal domain. Actor-before-theme order favours the corresponding
interpretation of \ird{Marcí travěte piaštou}; anchoring the theme may support
that reading when its discourse referent is already recoverable. Neither the
case ending nor \ird{-to} uniquely assigns a thematic role, and a single
dependent such as \ird{Jancí} may accordingly have more than one
interpretation. A possessor shares the indirect case but need not be an actor
or theme, while a postpositional adjunct supplies its own relation. Nominal
case neutralisation has a close comparison in Basque derived event nominals,
whose external and internal arguments may both be genitive, although larger
nominalised clauses retain a different case pattern \cite{artiagoitia2023}.
The comparison supports treating nominal case as a separate domain; it does
not determine which verbal layers Iridian event nominals contain.

\subsection{Participant nominals and privileged-participant reference}
\label{sec:nm-derived-participant}

Participant \ird{-ou} preserves the participant-selection features of
embedded aspect and Voice while nominal heads license its overt dependents.
With transitive bases, perfective and retrospective agents require antipassive
Voice, while continuous, progressive, and contemplative agents use the active
stem. An intransitive base describes its sole participant without antipassive
morphology. Patient-oriented
\ird{-n-} occurs throughout the paradigm, and applicative morphology selects
the promoted participant (Table~\ref{tab:participant-nominals}).

\pex
\a \ird{piaščikou} \trsl{the one who ate}
\a \ird{piaštova} \trsl{the one who eats}
\a \ird{piaštnikou} \trsl{that which was eaten}
\a \ird{piaštébikou} \trsl{the one who was fed}
\xe

The nominal relational head licensing an indirect theme in
\ird{lubcí piaščikou} adds a restriction to the event associated with the
participant. It does not restore an overt patient within antipassive
\synhead{VP}. The same participant description occurs in an identificational
predicate, as in \ird{Janek lubcí piaščikou}, and in an existential pivot,
as in \ird{Lubcí piaščikou jeca}; the difference concerns external
predication rather than nominal derivation (§\,\ref{sec:exst-vs-ident}).

The continuous direct \ird{-ova} is a joint exponent of Asp and nominal
features, rather than evidence that this construction belongs to ordinary

An event plural, if available, would require countable event units, whereas
lexical \trsl{food} has the mass interpretation described for substance nouns
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-derived-event}). The morphology presently specified does not
determine a distinction among event tokens, repeated occurrences, and event
kinds. Anchoring identifies a discourse-relevant event, participant, or
substance according to the noun's interpretation; it neither changes lexical
\trsl{food} into an argument-taking event nominal nor imposes uniqueness on
every participant description.

\subsection{A possible development of the nominalising family}
\label{sec:nm-derived-history}

A reconstructed deverbal \ird{*-ov-} formation could provide a common source
for event and lexical nouns, with later extension to participant descriptions
in voice-marked structures and thence to adnominal relatives. Positional /v/
vocalisation offers a possible phonetic basis for direct \ird{-ou} beside
prevocalic \ird{-ov-}: modern /v/ has a coda [w] realisation
(Chapter~\ref{ch:phon}). Modern spelling nevertheless preserves \ird{v}
in such contexts, so this reconstruction would also require conventionalisation
of the specialised \ird{-ou} spelling. Anchored \ird{-ovat} would then
involve morphological remodelling on \ird{-ov-}, rather than automatic
synchronic contraction. This sequence is a possible history of the family,
not established Iridian historical evidence; the contemporary structures and
allomorphs do not depend on it. A comparative model for the connection between
role-oriented nominalisation and relatives is the nominalisation-based
Austronesian pattern discussed by Kaufman \cite[§3.4]{kaufman2026}; that
comparison does not establish the proposed Iridian chronology.

\section{The discourse interface: anchoring, topicality, and
  the absolutive}
\label{sec:nm-topic-interface}

\subsection{Anchoring and the topic-absolutive connection}
\label{sec:nm-topic-abs}

A direct-case patient in a perfective transitive may be both anchored and
 topical, as in \ird{Travato Marku piaštnik}. The suffix identifies the bread
 within the shared discourse, while the clause can predicate what happened to
 it. The two contributions belong to independent domains: nominal
 [\textsc{+anch}] concerns accessibility, whereas clausal [\textsc{+top}]
 establishes aboutness (§\,\ref{sec:tc-anchoring-interface}). A direct-case
 argument in a topicless existential need not carry either feature, and the
 initial negative pivot \ird{deno} remains below \synhead{TopP}
 (§\,\ref{sec:tc-quantificational-pivots}).

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2em, labelwidth=!, labelindent=0em]
\item[\upshape Topicality--Anchoring Harmony:] An ordinary topic whose
 referent is shared and accessible is compatible with suffixal anchoring.
 This discourse preference imposes no Agree requirement between Top and Anch,
 and does not exclude bare, speaker-anchored, or newly introduced topics.
\end{description}

\noindent The preference provides no converse inference that a non-topic must
 be unanchored, and no quantitative claim about the frequency of anchoring by
 case. Shared reference can be maintained anywhere in the argument structure.

\subsection{Anchoring in non-direct cases}
\label{sec:nm-topic-oblique}

Transitive and indirect nominals retain the same anchoring contrasts as
 direct-case nominals. The anchored agent \ird{Markuto}, for instance,
 identifies Marek as accessible while preserving his transitive case. In
 \ird{Markuto tak tóm koupnik}, his initial position supports a linked-topic
 interpretation, but the anchor itself does not encode that dependency.
 Anchored indirect recipients, locatives, and other participants likewise
 need not receive contrastive prominence merely because they are identifiable.
 Their topical interpretation depends on the surrounding construction and
 discourse rather than on case-conditioned meanings of \ird{-to}/\ird{-te}.

\subsection{The bare noun as an \synhead{AnchP} with null anchoring}
\label{sec:nm-bare-noun}

A nominal without either \ird{si} or a suffixal anchor projects an
\synhead{AnchP} whose head lacks [\textsc{+anch}] and receives no overt
Vocabulary Item at PF. Its reference may be indefinite, contextually definite,
or generic; absence of overt anchoring leaves speaker identifiability
unmarked rather than requiring an unidentified or unindividuated referent.
The absence of overt anchoring thus corresponds to a feature distinction within the same
nominal structure, rather than to a smaller phrase. Where
[\textsc{+anch,+shr}] is present, Vocabulary Insertion supplies the suffix
in the form required by
the case-number ending (§\,\ref{sec:nm-def-nondirect}).

Null anchoring and topic drop both distinguish syntactic structure from its
phonological expression, although their interpretations differ. A silent
continuing topic retains a discourse dependency on a recoverable referent
(§\,\ref{sec:th-topic}); a null Anch head carries no anchoring
feature. Neither silence nor overt exponence in one domain determines the
feature content of the other.

\section{Theoretical tensions and open questions}
\label{sec:nm-tensions}

\subsection{The projection of \synhead{AnchP}: \synhead{DP} or a separate anchoring layer?}
\label{sec:nm-tension-dp}

The standard \synhead{DP} Hypothesis (Abney 1987) places D\textsuperscript{0} as
the single highest head of the nominal extended projection, unifying articles,
demonstratives, and similar functional elements under one projection. An
alternative tradition (Longobardi 1994; Alexiadou et~al. 2007) distinguishes a
dedicated definiteness layer from a higher \synhead{DP} layer (which may encode
referential anchoring, logophoricity, or other discourse properties).

The grammar represents the two overt anchoring strategies within a single
functional layer, Anch\textsuperscript{0}, with suffixal
exponents \ird{-to}/\ird{-te} and proclitic exponent \ird{si}
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-spec}). The demonstrative system occupies its own projection,
\synhead{DemP}, above \synhead{AnchP}. Whether \synhead{AnchP} and
\synhead{DemP} should be unified under an articulated \synhead{DP} along the
lines of Longobardi (1994) or kept as separate projections within the nominal
left periphery remains unspecified; the morphological ordering alone does not
determine whether the upper nominal domain contains a single articulated
projection or several distinct layers.

\subsection{The anchoring suffix and Case theory}
\label{sec:nm-tension-case}

A potential tension arises within Case theory. In the standard Minimalist
account (Chomsky 2001), case is assigned to DPs by an Agree relation with a
functional head (v*, T, or an applicative head). If the nominal extended
projection is capped by \synhead{AnchP}, then the outermost head of the
\synhead{DP} is D\textsuperscript{0}, not K\textsuperscript{0}. Case should in
principle be visible on the \synhead{DP} as a whole (from the perspective of the
external clause structure), but the morphological encoding of case is on the K
layer inside the \synhead{DP}, below D\textsuperscript{0}.

This can be resolved by one of two strategies: (a) the D head is transparent to
case, meaning the Agree probe from the clausal head targets K\textsuperscript{0}
or the \synhead{NP} root directly through \synhead{AnchP}; or (b) case features
percolate from K\textsuperscript{0} to D\textsuperscript{0} by feature
inheritance within the \synhead{DP}, and D presents the valued case feature to
the external probe. Strategy (b) has the advantage of preserving the standard
Agree architecture; strategy (a) requires relaxing the usual opacity of
\synhead{DP} boundaries.

\subsection{The anchoring suffix and phase theory}
\label{sec:nm-tension-phase}

Nominal-domain linearisation applies when $D_a$ is complete, including the
higher quantificational and demonstrative material associated with its noun
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-si-linearisation}). The PF derivation therefore retains the
anchoring terminal until that domain has been assembled; \synhead{AnchP}
alone does not close a separate pronunciation cycle. Embedded possessors
receive their own ordering before they are incorporated as dependents in the
containing nominal string.

This PF commitment does not identify \synhead{AnchP} with the edge of a
syntactic DP phase. Whether the completed outer nominal domain is also a phase,
and how its internal case features remain accessible to clausal probes,
concern syntactic locality (§\,\ref{sec:nm-tension-case}). The precedence of
\ird{si} follows from its source-conditioned linearisation within $D_a$,
without requiring the possessor to move to a phase edge merely to supply a
phonological host.

\subsection{Participant-oriented relatives and their nominal embedding}
\label{sec:nm-tension-rel}

The \ird{-ový} relative forms an independent case domain within the host
\synhead{DP}, while the head noun remains in the matrix nominal spine
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-ovni}). Its matrix case and optional anchoring are therefore
independent of the participant gap inside \synhead{RelP}. The exact attachment
of \synhead{RelP} relative to \synhead{PossP} is unresolved; the common
analysis in Chapter~\ref{ch:complex} requires only a prenominal modifier with
its own participant-selection and case relations.
