complements precede the matrix predicate, converb adjuncts precede the matrix

\subsection{Head-finality in clause combining}
\label{sec:th-subord-headfinal}

The unmarked pattern in clause combining is as follows:

\begin{center}
\term{[subordinate clause] \quad [matrix-clause material] \quad [matrix verb]}
\end{center}

The subordinate clause occupies the left side of the construction; the matrix
predicate closes it. This ordering holds across all of the major subordinate
clause types: converb chains, finite complement clauses, embedded wh-clauses,
and relative clauses all precede the higher predicate they are embedded under.
The same head-final logic applies inside the subordinate clause itself, which is
internally verb-final.

A converb chain places finite viewpoint and alignment contrasts on its
final verb, while each converb licenses a nominative-domain case frame without
finite viewpoint morphology. The boundedness or prospectivity supplied by a
linking ending does not create an additional finite aspect position. This
asymmetry separates the dependent predicate's event relation from the final
predicate's inflection (§\,\ref{sec:converb-chains}).

\subsection{Subordination types: a typological note}
\label{sec:th-subord-types}

Iridian distinguishes the morphological finiteness of a dependent predicate
from the syntactic and semantic relation between clauses. Converbs supply
non-finite event linkage, while conjunctive \ird{-e} preserves a finite
predicate under a clause-edge dependency. Finite \ird{po}/\ird{poby}
clauses are selected as arguments; prenominal \ird{-ový} relatives instead
select an aspect-bearing participant description without an overt relative
pronoun or complementiser (§\,\ref{sec:th-relativization}). Their embedded
aspect distinguishes them from ordinary adverbial converbs. Temporal event
nominals furnish reference expressions rather than finite dependent clauses.

Independent discourse continuation belongs to a different domain: \ird{nu}
introduces a discourse contribution without attaching to a dependent verb.
This distinction is comparable in function to the separation of conjunctive
endings, conjunctive adverbials, and juxtaposition in Sohn's Korean
classification, although those three mechanisms do not classify Iridian
constructions by finiteness or dependency \cite[291--292]{sohn2009}.

Converb chains concentrate overt finite aspect and alignment at their right
edge; finite conjunctive chains retain the specifications of each finite
predicate. This difference does not identify a discourse topic with a
grammatical pivot. Participant recovery and the default continuity relation in
adverbial chains are described in §\,\ref{sec:switch-reference}, while
independent discourse reference is treated in Chapter~\ref{ch:discourse}.

\section{Converbs}\label{sec:converb-morphology}


Converb suffixes replace the aspect-alignment slot entirely. A verb carrying a
converb suffix accordingly bears no finite viewpoint or mood exponent and
no aspect-conditioned alignment contrast. Its clause nevertheless licenses
nominal case through the converb construction (§\,\ref{sec:vm-converb-case}). The
morphological structure of a converb is:

\medskip
\begin{center}
(\textsc{lex-prefix}) + \Stem{} + (\Appl{}) + (\Caus{}) + \textsc{converb-suffix}
\end{center}
\medskip

The productive converb paradigm comprises eight endings, whose core relations
and stem effects are set out in Table~\ref{tab:converb-morph}. The restricted
state-frame suffix \ird{-eň} (§\,\ref{sec:state-frame}), finite clause-edge
linker \ird{-e} (§\,\ref{sec:conjunctive-linker}), and independent discourse connective
\ird{nu} (§\,\ref{sec:spoken-nu}) belong to separate constructions.

\begin{table}[ht]
	\footnotesize
	\caption{The eight productive converb suffixes and their phonological effects.}\label{tab:converb-morph}
	\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{L L L L}
		\toprule
		{\sc converb} & {\sc suffix(es)} & {\sc stem mutation} & {\sc notes} \\
		\midrule
        Sequential & \ird{-u} & none & completed anterior event \\
        Circumstantial & \ird{-ení} & none & \ird{-ní} immediately after applicative; full form after causative \\
        Simultaneous/manner & \ird{-ěc} & front grade & integrated overlap or manner \\
        Open conditional & \ird{-ej} & plain grade & no palatalisation \\
        Expected conditional & \ird{-ezmy} & plain grade & anticipated condition \\
        Purposive & \ird{-it} & stem-class alternants & purpose and selected event complement \\
        Concessive & \ird{-ouc} & none & counter-expectation \\
        Prospective anterior & \ird{-esny} & none & matrix event precedes reference event \\
		\bottomrule
	\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The simultaneous/manner converb \ird{-ěc} selects the front grade,
irrealis \ird{-ěv} the mixed grade, and antipassive \ird{-aš-} its separate
replacement classes. Conditional \ird{-ej} selects the plain grade
(§\,\ref{sec:cond-nopala} of Chapter~\ref{ch:complex}); its initial vowel
leaves alveolar sibilants unchanged. Circumstantial \ird{-ení} is also
plain-grade, unlike the homophonous front-coded nominal ending. The shortened
\ird{-ní} is selected immediately after applicative \ird{-éb-}; a following
rhotic causative instead selects full \ird{-ení}, as in \ird{pjáštrení}
and \ird{pjaštébrení}, beside the alternative in \ird{-u}. Simultaneous
\ird{-ěc} leaves causative /r/ unchanged and is written with plain
\ird{e}: \ird{pjáštrec}.
Grade membership must therefore be recorded with each construction.


\subsection{Inflectional independence and scope}
\label{sec:converb-inflection-scope}

All eight productive endings occupy the non-finite linking position rather
than following an independently inflected finite verb. None takes finite
viewpoint, mood, alignment, or inner evidential suffixes in its own clause.
Lexical prefixes and licensed applicative or causative structure remain
available below the converb ending. Boundedness in \ird{-u}, expectation in
\ird{-ezmy}, and prospectivity in \ird{-it} or \ird{-esny} consequently
belong to the linking construction, without creating another finite Asp or
Mood domain. A matrix progressive, perfective, contemplative, or irrealis
retains its own contribution; its suffix does not supply an unpronounced
finite exponent inside the dependent predicate.

Independent negation of converb predicates, including its attachment and
concord domain, remains unspecified (§\,\ref{sec:cond-negation}). Neither
negative finite mood suffixes nor indicative \ird{za-} can be added by
analogy alone. Finite \ird{-e} clauses instead preserve their independently
licensed inflection and inner evidential morphology
(§\,\ref{sec:conjunctive-linker}). This difference concerns morphological
independence rather than the strength of a contextual temporal or causal
connection.

\subsection{Formal analysis: the syntactic status of converbs}
\label{sec:vm-converbs}
\label{sec:vm-converb-aspless}\label{sec:vm-converb-case}\label{sec:vm-converb-adjunct}


Converbs lack finite Asp.Align and its viewpoint distinctions, while retaining
the lower predicate and any licensed applicative or causative structure.
The analysis treats the converb suffix as the exponent of a converb head,
Conv, with a defective aspectual specification. Its features specify the
linking relation without supplying
the finite IPFV/ERG-domain opposition. Conv selects the lower verbal
predicate directly, excluding the finite perfective/retrospective configuration
that licenses ergative alignment.
The independent absence of outer patient-selecting Voice, including both
ordinary \ird{-n-} and causative-conditioned \ird{-an-}, from the
established converb forms is described in §\,\ref{sec:converb-chains};
its exclusion does not follow from the absence of ergative case alone, since
the same Voice head also occurs in finite nominative-domain passives.


Within this reduced structure, converb clauses license the NOM-domain case frames without finite viewpoint
aspect. An ordinary active selects its agent or sole participant as direct and
assigns a retained patient indirect case; a simple applicative selects the
applied participant, with actor and retained theme indirect, while a higher
causative selects its causer. No converb licenses transitive-case agents.
These are constructional licensing conditions: absence of perfective or
retrospective Asp excludes the ERG frame, but does not by itself determine
which participant is privileged.


At the clause-combining level, ordinary event-linking converb clauses function
as adverbial adjuncts to the matrix clause. Selected purposive complements in
\ird{-it} instead occupy the complement position of their governing predicate
(§\,\ref{sec:purposive}). These adjunct converbs are merged as adjuncts to the matrix
\synhead{IP} or \synhead{vP}, not as arguments in Spec or Complement positions.
This explains their preverbal (and pre-matrix) position: as adjuncts to
\synhead{IP}, they precede all material within \synhead{IP} and surface before
the matrix arguments in the linear string, consistent with the head-final
adjunction pattern.

Reference across converb chains follows a default same-pivot relation
 (§\,\ref{sec:switch-reference}). Distinct overt nominals or discourse
 context can support different-pivot readings, without a switch-reference
 clitic. Since an aboutness topic need not be the absolutive pivot, topic
 continuity and this dependency are separate relations. These constructions
 do not by themselves establish the availability of embedded hanging topics
 or high topicalisation in every non-finite clause.

\section{Converb chains: structure and alignment}\label{sec:converb-chains}

\subsection{The finite-verb-final rule}

In an ordinary dependent converb chain, all verbs except the last are
non-finite; the final predicate supplies finite aspect, mood, and alignment.
A finite conjunctive chain in \ird{-e} is a separate construction in which
non-final predicates retain finite inflection
(§\,\ref{sec:finite-conjunctive-linkage}). It therefore does not constitute
an exception to the internal form of a converb chain. Discourse-licensed
conditional and intentional uses without a following finite predicate retain
their construction-specific distributions
(§§\,\ref{sec:cond-insub}, \ref{sec:purp-intentional}).

\subsection{Prosodic integration of the chain}\label{sec:converb-phrasing}

A converb chain ordinarily integrates into one intonational phrase containing
several phonological phrases (§\,\ref{sec:prosodic-domains}). Each lexical
word retains its own initial primary stress and subsidiary footing; integration
at phrase level does not create a single lexical stress domain for the chain.
A nonfinal phonological phrase may carry the continuation tone H-, while the
principal nuclear contour and final boundary belong to the intonational
phrase as a whole
(§\,\ref{sec:intonation}). A converb ending therefore requires neither a
pause nor a full pitch reset; converb morphology does not assign a fixed
pitch contour to its clause.

More independent discourse units may form separate intonational phrases, whose
boundaries can reinforce a shift of topic or a new discourse move. Their
placement depends on information structure and speech rate as well as syntactic
organisation, so neither a clause boundary nor a change of pivot alone fixes
an intonational boundary. The independent contour of an insubordinate
conditional is a construction-specific case (§\,\ref{sec:cond-insub}).

The established string \ird{Marek zdravení Janek znóvime}
 \trsl{While Marek sleeps, Janek is studying}
 (§\,\ref{sec:sequential}) permits both an integrated reading, in which
 the first clause ends with H- and the principal H* L\% contour belongs
 to \ird{znóvime}, and a deliberately separated reading, in which each
 clause has its own nuclear prominence and intonational boundary. The first
 clause may then receive a complete fall before the following phrase resets
 the pitch range. These schematic readings preserve primary stress on each
 lexical word's first syllable, the simultaneous interpretation of
 \ird{-ení}, and the overtly distinct pivots; prosodic separation supplies
 no change to the syntactic dependency.

\subsection{Accusative alignment within converb clauses}

Converb clauses belong to the nominative-accusative domain. In an ordinary
active converb the agent or sole participant is direct and a retained patient
is indirect, while a simple applicative selects its applied participant as
direct and assigns indirect case to the remaining arguments. A higher
causative selects the causer as direct. Converbs carry no finite viewpoint
aspect, so they license neither transitive-case agents nor the
ergative-domain antipassive.

\subsection{Switch-reference and topic continuity}\label{sec:switch-reference}

By default, the absolutive pivot of a converb clause is coreferent with the
absolutive pivot of the following clause. Because ordinary active converb
clauses privilege their agents, this default often resembles a same-subject
(SS) pattern, while the broader generalisation concerns \term{same-pivot}
 continuity. A discourse topic can be distinct from either pivot, so topical
 continuity does not by itself determine converb coreference. When
the pivots differ (different-subject/different-pivot, DS), the contrast is
signalled by overt lexical NPs, demonstratives, or the discourse structure
itself; Iridian has no grammaticalised switch-reference clitic. The presence of a
high non-direct topic does not independently change this default relation.
The distribution of linked topicalisation and external hanging topics inside
converb clauses is not specified by the same-pivot rule. Where both
clauses supply full nominal pivots, disambiguation is immediate; where one pivot
is omitted, recoverability from discourse context determines the SS/DS reading.

\section{Sequence and circumstance}\label{sec:sequential}

The lexical-slot notation used below is defined in
§\,\ref{sec:lexical-slot-schemata}.

\subsection{Bounded sequence: \ird{-u}}

The sequential converb \ird{-u} presents a completed, bounded eventuality
before the eventuality described by the following clause. An activity such as
eating may receive a contextually bounded interpretation, although its lexical
predicate need not describe an inherently telic event. Completion belongs to the
local linking relation: \ird{-u} neither supplies finite perfective aspect nor
changes the nominative-domain case frame of its own clause. The matrix verb
retains its independently selected aspect and alignment.

For an affirmative episodic complex, the analysis represents this relation
schematically as follows, where $P(e_1)$ and $Q(e_2)$ describe the dependent and
following eventualities and $\tau(e)$ denotes an eventuality's temporal interval:
\[
P(e_1)\land Q(e_2)\land \mathrm{BOUNDED}(e_1)
\land \mathrm{end}(\tau(e_1))\leq\mathrm{start}(\tau(e_2)).
\]
The boundary is interpreted within the relevant temporal or modal context;
the schema does not assert that a hypothetical complex has occurred in the
actual world. A completed preliminary act may enable or cause the next event,
but causation is inferred from the predicates and context rather than encoded
by \ird{-u}. Likewise, completion does not require the first event's result to
persist throughout the second.

\subsection{Circumstantial linkage: \ird{-ení}}

The circumstantial converb \ird{-ení} supplies an eventuality relevant as a
frame or circumstance for the following clause. Its conventional contribution
is circumstantial relevance, while temporal overlap, antecedence, and causal
connection depend on the eventualities described and their context:
\[
P(e_1)\land Q(e_2)\land \mathrm{CIRC}(e_1,e_2).
\]
Here $\mathrm{CIRC}$ requires an interpretable circumstantial relation rather
than unrestricted addition. A prior event may furnish that relation through a
salient continuing result; result persistence is then part of the contextual
interpretation, without being entailed by the ending itself. Narrative
progression is available where the prior event supplies a frame for what
follows, whereas a mere list of unrelated facts supplies no such relation.

The different-pivot construction below retains the established simultaneous
interpretation: Marek's sleeping frames Janek's studying without making the
two participants coreferent.

\ex\label{ex:circ-different-pivot}%
\begingl
\gla Marek zdravení Janek znóvime.//
\glb Marek sleep-\Circ{} Janek study-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{While Marek sleeps, Janek is studying.}//
\endgl
\xe

These semantic schemata specify the analysis of Iridian. They do not identify
its endings with Korean connectives: Korean \textit{-ko} permits temporal
relations, including unordered addition, which are broader than the bounded
anteriority of Iridian \ird{-u}, while Korean \textit{-ese/-ase} illustrates
how preconditional or causal relevance may constrain a multifunctional
connective \cite[300--301]{sohn2009}.

\subsection{The copula converb and the discourse particle \ird{nu}}\label{sec:seq-copula}

The marked copular verb \ird{neh-} (§\,\ref{sec:neh}) has the suppletive
sequential converb \ird{nesu}, established independently of its aspectual
paradigm. Its colloquial reduced continuation \ird{nu} has a discourse function
extending beyond event sequence (§\,\ref{sec:spoken-nu}). The source in
\ird{nesu} does not require a silent copular event in every use of \ird{nu},
which introduces a discourse segment rather than realising a productive
converb ending.

\subsection{Interaction with applicative and causative}

\pex
\a \ird{pjašt-u} \trsl{having eaten}; \ird{pjašt-ení} \trsl{with eating as the circumstance}
\a \ird{pjašt-éb-u} / \ird{pjašt-éb-ní} \trsl{having eaten / eating for someone's benefit}
\a \ird{pjášt-r-u} / \ird{pjášt-r-ení} \trsl{having made / making someone eat}
\xe

Circumstantial \ird{-ení} retains its full form after rhotic causative
morphology, yielding \ird{pjáštrení}; shortened \ird{-ní} occurs
only after an immediately preceding applicative \ird{-éb-}. Thus an applied
causative has \ird{pjaštébr-ení}, with the causative determining the
outer suffixal environment (§\,\ref{sec:converb-morphology}). The shared
attachment domain does not make \ird{-u} and \ird{-ení} semantic variants of a
single sequential suffix.

\section{Temporal constructions}\label{sec:temporal}

\subsection{Integrated simultaneity and manner: \ird{-ěc}}
\label{sec:integrated-simultaneity}

The converb \ird{-ěc} relates overlapping eventualities within an integrated
episode, commonly where one action supplies the manner, means, or accompaniment
of the other. The schematic relation is:
\[
P(e_1)\land Q(e_2)\land
\mathrm{OVERLAP}(\tau(e_1),\tau(e_2))\land\mathrm{INTEGRATED}(e_1,e_2).
\]
Overlap alone does not establish integration: two people's unrelated activities
at the same time are more naturally framed by \ird{-ení}. A shared pivot or
principal participant favours integrated manner and means, without constituting
an obligatory identity condition. Conversely, identity of participants does not
by itself turn two temporally separate actions into a simultaneous episode.
The distinction between participant continuity and the semantic function of a
linker also has a comparative basis in Korean clause chaining
\cite{choi2020}; Iridian retains the discourse-conditioned reference mechanisms
specified in §\,\ref{sec:switch-reference}.

The suffix selects the front consonant grade
(§\,\ref{sec:suffix-classes}), independently of antipassive replacement.
It also differs morphologically from the derived manner-adverb suffix
\ird{-ě} (§\,\ref{sec:stative-adv}): a form such as \ird{skorně}
\trsl{quickly} is a lexical adverb, whereas a converb retains a predicate and
its licensed arguments. A counter-expectational interpretation of simultaneous
\ird{-ěc} depends on the context and remains subject to the overlap and
integration requirements; general concession belongs to \ird{-ouc}
(§\,\ref{sec:concessive}).

\subsection{Controlled contrasts and semantic diagnostics}
\label{sec:converb-diagnostics}

The following constructed contrasts keep the eating and motion predicates
constant. In each, \ird{Marek} is direct, \ird{travě} is the indirect patient
of the converb, and \ird{stójek} is the finite intransitive perfective. The
matrix journey can therefore be bounded regardless of which linking relation
holds within it. Its omitted mover is recovered as Marek under the same-pivot
default and the stated context; this reading does not impose obligatory
pivot identity on all adverbial converbs.

\pex\label{ex:core-converb-contrast}
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek travě pjaštu stójek.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} bread.\Ind{} eat-\Seq{} go-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek ate bread and then went.}
\lingcontext{the eating is completed before the journey}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek travě pjaštení stójek.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} bread.\Ind{} eat-\Circ{} go-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek was eating bread when he went.}
\lingcontext{the eating supplies a temporally overlapping background}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek travě pjaštěc stójek.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} bread.\Ind{} eat-\Sim{} go-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek went whilst eating bread.}
\lingcontext{the eating accompanies the journey within an integrated episode}//
\endgl
\xe

The overlapping interpretation in (b) is supplied by the context; neither
causal necessity nor a maintained result follows from \ird{-ení}. Whereas
(b) backgrounds the eating, (c) integrates it as an accompaniment of the
journey. In (c), eating need overlap only the relevant portion of the journey, rather
than continue throughout its entire interval. Front-grade \ird{pjaštěc}
retains the stem's postalveolar and selects soft final \ird{t}; it does not
import the antipassive stem \ird{pjášč-}.

Completion, maintained results, and means can be separated more directly
when the predicates describe different kinds of event. Washing supplies a
bounded preliminary activity in (\ref{ex:slot-preliminary}a), whereas the
completed report in (b) merely precedes another participant's departure.
The latter sequence remains compatible with `but the departure had nothing
to do with the report'.

\pex\label{ex:slot-preliminary}
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek \textnormal{[[hands]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}} sporu \textnormal{[[get-dressed]]}\textsubscript{\Pf{}}.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} hands.\Ind{} wash-\Seq{} get.dressed-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Having washed his hands, Marek got dressed.}
\lingcontext{the context identifies the hands as Marek's; washing is completed first}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek \textnormal{[[report]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}} [[complete]]-u Janek \textnormal{[[depart]]}\textsubscript{\Pf{}}.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} report.\Ind{} complete-\Seq{} Janek.\Dir{} depart-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek completed the report, and then Janek departed.}
\lingcontext{ordered events; no causal connection is required}//
\endgl
\xe

Opening a door can instead be relevant through the passage it leaves
accessible. The two schemata in (\ref{ex:slot-door-result}) share their
predicates, although only \ird{-u} conventionally requires a completed opening
before entry; \ird{-ení} permits the context to supply that interpretation.

\pex\label{ex:slot-door-result}
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek \textnormal{[[door]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}} [[open]]-u \textnormal{[[enter]]}\textsubscript{\Pf{}}.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} door.\Ind{} open-\Seq{} enter-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Having opened the door, Marek entered.}
\lingcontext{opening is completed before entry}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek \textnormal{[[door]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}} [[open]]-ení \textnormal{[[enter]]}\textsubscript{\Pf{}}.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} door.\Ind{} open-\Circ{} enter-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{With the door opened, Marek entered.}
\lingcontext{the accessible passage supplies the relevant continuing result}//
\endgl
\xe

In (b), the completed-opening interpretation and its persisting result
come from the stated context. If the door closes before entry, that particular
enabling relation is lost, although another circumstantial relation could
still support \ird{-ení}. In (a), the door may close and be reopened before
the later entry without contradicting the suffix's completion and precedence
requirements. Neither form itself asserts uninterrupted accessibility.

Integrated overlap also permits a means interpretation, which differs from
mere accompaniment. The walking stem \ird{oceň-} selects reduced
\ird{ocň-}, retaining its soft nasal; the Class~A progressive
\ird{ócňime} lengthens the retained root vowel. Singing can accompany
walking, whereas paddling can be the means by which forward motion is achieved:

\pex\label{ex:slot-integrated-activities}
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek kaněc ócňime.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} sing-\Sim{} walk-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek is walking whilst singing.}
\lingcontext{overlapping activities within one episode}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek [[paddle]]-ěc \textnormal{[[advance]]}\textsubscript{\Pf{}}.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} paddle-\Sim{} advance-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek advanced by paddling.}
\lingcontext{paddling is construed as an intransitive activity supplying the means of advance}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek \textnormal{[[hands]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}} sporení Janek \textnormal{[[practise]]}\textsubscript{\Prog{}}.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} hands.\Ind{} wash-\Circ{} Janek.\Dir{} practise-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{While Marek washes his hands, Janek is practising.}
\lingcontext{two independent activities share a temporal frame}//
\endgl
\xe

Replacing \ird{-ěc} with \ird{-u} in (a) would require the singing
to finish before the walking, while replacing \ird{-ení} with \ird{-ěc}
in (c) would require an integrated episode beyond coincidence in time. A
shared participant favours integration in (a--b), but the different pivots in
(c) are independently compatible with circumstantial framing.

\begin{table}[ht]
\footnotesize
\caption{Encoded relations and contextual inferences in the principal converbs.}
\label{tab:converb-encoded-inferred}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{l L L}
\toprule
Ending & Required relation & Contextual interpretation \\
\midrule
\ird{-u} & bounded completion before the following eventuality & preparation, enabling, consequence; no necessary result persistence \\
\ird{-ení} & relevant circumstance or frame & overlap, relevant prior event, continuing result, event-level cause \\
\ird{-ěc} & overlap within an integrated episode & manner, means, accompaniment, restricted counter-expectation \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

Cancellation distinguishes the required relation from a plausible inference.
A continuation equivalent to `but the first event did not cause the second'
can cancel a causal inference with \ird{-u} or \ird{-ení}; with \ird{-ení},
some other circumstantial relation must remain available. By contrast,
`the eating continued throughout the journey and was never completed before it'
excludes the bounded-anterior interpretation in (\ref{ex:core-converb-contrast}a), while `the two activities
did not overlap at all' excludes the simultaneous interpretation in (\ref{ex:core-converb-contrast}c).
These are semantic exclusions under the stated event identifications, rather
than claims that every surface sentence containing those words is
morphosyntactically ill formed.

Changing a participant tests continuity independently of time: the two overt
pivots in (\ref{ex:circ-different-pivot}) permit circumstantial overlap, whereas
replacing its ending with \ird{-ěc} would additionally require a coherent
integrated episode. Reversing the two predicates tests the direction of
\ird{-u}, since it reverses the required temporal ordering; neither overlap
nor circumstantial relevance entails that reversal preserves discourse
coherence. If a negative predicate is independently licensed in such a
construction, its interpretation would have to supply a bounded interval or
circumstance rather than inherit the positive event's completion. These
diagnostics do not license internal converb negation
(§\,\ref{sec:cond-negation}). The interpretation of negated activity predicates with
\ird{-u}, including any coercion to a bounded period of non-occurrence,
remains unspecified.

\subsection{Prospective anteriority: \ird{-esny}}
\label{sec:prospective-anteriority}

The converb \ird{-esny} places the matrix eventuality before an anticipated
reference eventuality, whose occurrence is not entailed. The reference event
may subsequently occur, remain merely projected, or be prevented by the
matrix event. Thus `She left before help arrived' permits later arrival,
whereas `She died before help could arrive' can make nonarrival central to the
interpretation. These English stimuli distinguish temporal ordering from
actualisation; they supply no additional Iridian mood morphology.
Noncommittal and anti-veridical `before' interpretations are independently
distinguished in contemporary temporal semantics
\cite[§1]{ogiharasteinert2024}; the retained Iridian construction does not
adopt a particular cross-linguistic modal derivation.

\ex\label{ex:prospective-before}%
\begingl
\gla Marek zomenesny, Janek pjášček.//
\glb Marek leave-\Cv{} Janek eat-\Antip{}-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek ate before Marek left.}
\lingcontext{constructed; Marek's departure need not actually occur}//
\endgl
\xe
The perfective matrix reports Janek's bounded eating, while \ird{-esny}
locates it before the projected departure. A continuation `but Marek never
left' consequently does not contradict the connective. Replacing \ird{-esny}
with \ird{-u} would reverse the temporal relation and require a completed
anterior departure in the local interpretation. Anteriority to an anticipated
event is therefore distinct from completion before another event.

An anticipated arrival provides a reference point even when assistance
never materialises:

\pex\label{ex:slot-before-help}
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[help]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}} ščenesny Marek \textnormal{[[depart]]}\textsubscript{\Pf{}}.//
\glb help.\Dir{} arrive-\Cv{} Marek.\Dir{} depart-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek departed before help arrived.}
\lingcontext{compatible with either later arrival or the continuation `but help never arrived'}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[help]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}} ščenu Marek \textnormal{[[depart]]}\textsubscript{\Pf{}}.//
\glb help.\Dir{} arrive-\Seq{} Marek.\Dir{} depart-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{After help arrived, Marek departed.}
\lingcontext{the arrival is completed before departure in this affirmative episodic reading}//
\endgl
\xe

The continuation denying arrival is compatible with the prospective
reference in (a), whereas it contradicts the completed arrival described in
(b) when the same help and occasion are intended. Neither contrast requires
an irrealis suffix inside the dependent predicate.

\subsection{Event nominals as temporal reference expressions}
\label{sec:temporal-event-nominals}

Temporal \ird{cy}, \ird{do}, \ird{na}, and \ird{u} follow an indirect-case
event nominal, extending the source, bounded-path, location, and proximity
relations of the spatial postpositions (§\,\ref{sec:nm-postp-temporal}).
The construction locates a matrix eventuality against a nominal reference
expression rather than adding a non-finite verb to a chain. Its complement
must have an event interpretation: \ird{pjaštově} here denotes eating, rather
than the lexical substance `food'. The following phrases instantiate the
temporal extension with the independently established indirect singular.

\begin{table}[ht]
\footnotesize
\caption{Temporal event-nominal constructions.}\label{tab:tempconverbs}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{l L L}
\toprule
Construction & Temporal relation & Boundary or interval \\
\midrule
\ird{pjaštově cy} & since/after eating & completion by default; explicit context may select onset \\
\ird{pjaštově do} & until eating & onset by default; a different boundary must be identified \\
\ird{pjaštově na} & during eating & overlap with the eating interval \\
\ird{pjaštově u} & around the time of eating & contextually near the eating interval \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The nominal supplies an event description, whose participants and temporal
reference are resolved in the nominal construction
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-derived-temporal}). It supplies neither finite tense nor an
independent evidential. An ordinary affirmative episodic interpretation uses
an established reference event, whereas a prospective or modal clause may
locate its event relative to a projected occasion. Actuality follows from that
interpretive environment rather than from nominal \ird{-ou} alone.

\subsection{Temporal source: \ird{cy}}

Temporal \ird{cy} establishes a lower reference boundary, normally the
completion of the nominal event. A subsequent bounded matrix event receives
an `after' interpretation; a state or activity extending from that boundary
towards a contextual reference time receives a `since' interpretation. These
readings differ through the temporal extent of the matrix eventuality, without
requiring two unrelated lexical entries for \ird{cy}. Where discourse explicitly
identifies an event's beginning as the source, that onset can replace the
completion default. Without that contextual identification, the nominal
expression retains its completion-based reading.

\ex\label{ex:temporal-source}%
\begingl
\gla Marcí travěte pjaštově cy Janek znóvime.//
\glb Marek.\Ind{} bread.\Ind{}.\Def{} eating.\Ind{} from Janek study-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek has been studying since Marek ate the bread.}
\lingcontext{constructed; the meal is an established event and completion is the source}//
\endgl
\xe
The progressive matrix describes an interval continuing from the source into
the contextual reference time. The English finite clause renders the event
nominal, while the English perfect expresses the interval's relation to the
reference time; neither adds finite inflection to the nominal or a
retrospective suffix to the Iridian matrix verb. A bounded subsequent
departure would instead favour `after'. The postpositional construction
identifies its reference event within a nominal expression, whereas \ird{-u}
links two predicates and requires a completed anterior eventuality. Neither
construction asserts causation.

The same event nominal supports `since' and `after' when the matrix
predicates supply different temporal extents:

\pex\label{ex:slot-source-extent}
\a
\begingl
\gla sporově cy Marek méžime.//
\glb washing.\textsc{evt}.\Ind{} from Marek.\Dir{} wait-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek has been waiting since washing.}
\lingcontext{the washing is complete; waiting extends from that boundary to the reference time}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla sporově cy Marek \textnormal{[[depart]]}\textsubscript{\Pf{}}.//
\glb washing.\textsc{evt}.\Ind{} from Marek.\Dir{} depart-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek departed after washing.}
\lingcontext{departure is a subsequent event; no claim of an interval extending to speech time}//
\endgl
\xe

The washer is identified as Marek in these contexts, although the
postposition does not impose that identity. Both readings retain completion
as the source boundary; changing the matrix predicate changes the interval
profile without covertly moving the boundary to the beginning of washing.

\subsection{Temporal endpoint: \ird{do}}

Temporal \ird{do} supplies the right boundary of a relevant extended matrix
interval, normally at the onset of the nominal event. An explicit context may
instead identify completion, as in waiting until a meal is finished; the
nominal's lack of finite aspect does not independently select that boundary.
A state, activity, or repeated series can extend towards the endpoint, whereas
a single punctual occurrence requires an extended or iterative construal.
The boundary closes the interval being described, without asserting that the
same activity can never resume or that every consequence of it ceases.

\ex\label{ex:temporal-endpoint}%
\begingl
\gla Marcí travěte pjaštově do Janek znóvime.//
\glb Marek.\Ind{} bread.\Ind{}.\Def{} eating.\Ind{} until Janek study-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek is studying until Marek starts eating the bread.}
\lingcontext{constructed; the anticipated meal supplies the endpoint}//
\endgl
\xe
Here the studying extends towards a projected meal; the nominal expression does
not assert that the meal has begun. In a future or modal environment, the
boundary is evaluated in the relevant projected alternatives. If the meal is
cancelled, a planned time can still identify the endpoint where that schedule
is independently available; cancellation does not create an actual eating
event. This interpretation requires an identifiable projected boundary, rather
than an arbitrary nonexistent event.

Matrix negation can deny the matrix eventuality over the pre-boundary
interval: the English stimulus `Janek did not eat until Marek began eating'
places non-eating before the endpoint. It does not, in this construction,
assert Janek's eating at or after the boundary. An explicit positive sequel
must supply that occurrence. Negation of the entire interval claim is a
different scope, and neither reading places verbal \ird{za-} inside the event
nominal. Cross-linguistic `not until' constructions vary in their aspectual and
polarity behaviour; those profiles must be established construction by
construction \cite[§§1--2]{deswarttellingswalchli2022}.

Washing also separates an event's onset from its completion. In the
following schemata, the first endpoint is the beginning of the wash, while
the second context explicitly selects its end:

\pex\label{ex:slot-endpoint-boundaries}
\a
\begingl
\gla sporově do Janek méžime.//
\glb washing.\textsc{evt}.\Ind{} until Janek.\Dir{} wait-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek is waiting until the washing begins.}
\lingcontext{default onset boundary}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla sporově do Janek méžime.//
\glb washing.\textsc{evt}.\Ind{} until Janek.\Dir{} wait-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek is waiting until the washing is finished.}
\lingcontext{context explicitly identifies completion as the endpoint}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla sporově do Janek zatrástime.//
\glb washing.\textsc{evt}.\Ind{} until Janek.\Dir{} \Neg{}-work-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Janek is not working until the washing begins.}
\lingcontext{matrix non-working throughout the relevant pre-onset interval}//
\endgl
\xe

A continuation `and he does not work afterwards either' remains
compatible with (c), since a positive onset of work is not asserted. A single
instantaneous jump cannot replace waiting in (a--b) with its punctual reading
unchanged; a repeated series of jumps could instead furnish the extended
interval. The negative cell in (c) belongs to the finite matrix predicate,
while the washing nominal remains positive and contains no internal verbal
negation.

\subsection{Temporal location and proximity: \ird{na} and \ird{u}}

Temporal \ird{na} requires overlap with the interval described by the event
nominal. The matrix eventuality may occupy only part of that interval; full
containment, identical duration, and exact coincidence are not encoded.
Temporal \ird{u} instead requires contextual proximity to the reference
interval, allowing an occasion shortly before or after it as well as overlap.
The relevant tolerance depends on the scale of the events and the established
discourse frame.

The constructed substitution pair \ird{pjaštově na Janek znóvime}
\trsl{Janek is studying while someone is eating} and
\ird{pjaštově u Janek znóvime}
\trsl{Janek is studying around the time someone eats} keeps the nominal and
matrix predicate constant. A context in which studying ends shortly before
the meal excludes the overlap reading of \ird{na}, while remaining compatible
with \ird{u} if that distance counts as near. The eater is supplied by context,
without obligatory identity with Janek. Postpositional \ird{u} is written
separately and retains the postpositions' established prosodic attachment;
its homophony with suffixal \ird{-u} supplies no shared origin or temporal
identity (§\,\ref{sec:nm-postp-status}).

The same reference event permits contrasting location and proximity
relations:

\pex\label{ex:slot-location-proximity}
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[rehearse]]}\textsubscript{\textsc{evt.ind}} na Marek ščének.//
\glb rehearsing.\textsc{evt}.\Ind{} during Marek.\Dir{} arrive-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek arrived during the rehearsal.}
\lingcontext{arrival overlaps the rehearsal interval}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[rehearse]]}\textsubscript{\textsc{evt.ind}} u Marek ščének.//
\glb rehearsing.\textsc{evt}.\Ind{} near Marek.\Dir{} arrive-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek arrived around the time of the rehearsal.}
\lingcontext{arrival may be shortly before, during, or shortly after the rehearsal}//
\endgl
\xe

An arrival five minutes before the rehearsal can satisfy (b) when
that distance counts as near, but it cannot satisfy (a) under the same
identification of the rehearsal interval. Neither expression entails that
Marek participates in the rehearsal.

\subsection{Synthetic and analytic temporal relations}

Bounded \ird{-u} encodes a completed preliminary eventuality;
circumstantial \ird{-ení} frames another eventuality without requiring
completion; expected \ird{-ezmy} treats an antecedent as an anticipated
trigger. Temporal nominals instead identify a source, endpoint, overlapping
interval, or nearby occasion. Neither sequence nor expectation alone entails
immediacy: `as soon as' requires an independently expressed immediacy condition
or a context fixing a negligible delay. Prospective \ird{-esny} remains
distinct because the event before which the matrix eventuality occurs need
never be realised (§\,\ref{sec:prospective-anteriority}).

Cross-linguistic temporal systems vary in the degree to which they use
specialised devices: in a 218-language comparison, `after', `before', and
`until' are especially associated with monofunctional expression, whereas
`when' more often permits polyfunctionality
\cite[60--61]{olguinmartinez2023}. Iridian's source, endpoint, location, and
proximity constructions retain the same nominal case and postpositional
structure as their spatial counterparts, while their temporal interpretations
are specified by the relations described here.

\section{Causal and concessive relations}

\subsection{Causal constructions}\label{sec:causal}


Event-level causation relates eventualities in the represented situation:
rain may make a road slippery. An epistemic justification instead supplies
grounds for a conclusion, as when a wet road supports an inference that it has
rained; a speech-act justification supplies grounds for an instruction, as
when rain motivates advice to take an umbrella. This analytical distinction
between content, epistemic, and conversational relations follows
\cite[ch.~4]{sweetser1990}; it does not posit three causal suffixes in Iridian.

Circumstantial \ird{-ení} can support a causal interpretation when its
predicates and context supply the connection, whereas bounded \ird{-u}
requires precedence without encoding causation. Denying that the first event caused the
second therefore leaves a temporal sequence possible, and may leave a
noncausal circumstance available. A premise justifying a conclusion or an
instruction must instead be related at the propositional or discourse level
(§§\,\ref{sec:conjunctive-linker}, \ref{sec:spoken-nu}); an
event-oriented converb does not acquire scope over a question or command
merely because an English translation contains `because'. No dedicated causal
converb exponent is specified.

An event-level consequence can arise from circumstantial relevance
without making causation part of the ending:

\pex\label{ex:slot-event-cause}
\a
\begingl
\gla pozebževení \textnormal{[[road]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}} \textnormal{[[become-slippery]]}\textsubscript{\Pf{}}.//
\glb rain-\Circ{} road.\Dir{} become.slippery-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{With rain falling, the road became slippery.}
\lingcontext{rain is understood as the cause in the stated situation}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla pozebževení \textnormal{[[road]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}} \textnormal{[[remain-dry]]}\textsubscript{\Cont{}}.//
\glb rain-\Circ{} road.\Dir{} remain.dry-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{While it is raining, the road remains dry.}
\lingcontext{the road is covered; rainfall supplies a contrasting circumstance}//
\endgl
\xe

The second context blocks the inference from rainfall to a slippery
road while preserving a relevant circumstance. A reason for issuing an
instruction instead relates discourse acts, as illustrated in
§\,\ref{sec:spoken-justification-examples}.

\subsection{Concessive constructions}\label{sec:concessive}

Concessive \ird{-ouc} presents an actual or entertained circumstance against
which the following clause defeats a salient expectation. Factual concession
backgrounds accepted content, whereas concessive conditionality entertains
that content without asserting it. Both retain counter-expectation, which
ordinary adversative coordination need not encode, and neither requires the
simultaneity characteristic of \ird{-ěc}. Contextual surprise during an
integrated activity therefore does not make \ird{-ěc} interchangeable with
\ird{-ouc}.

The following constructed pair uses the same predicates with two contextual
interpretations of \ird{-ouc}:
\pex\label{ex:concession-actuality}
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek zomenouc, Janek pjaštáž.//
\glb Marek leave-\Cncs{} Janek eat-\Ctpl{}//
\glft \trsl{Although Marek is leaving, Janek will eat.}
\lingcontext{departure accepted; Janek's eating defeats the expectation}//
\endgl
\a \ird{Marek zomenouc, Janek pjaštáž.}
\trsl{Even if Marek leaves, Janek will eat.}
\lingcontext{departure entertained rather than asserted}
\xe
The irrealis is available in a concessive main clause, but concession neither
requires that mood nor invariably makes the dependent eventuality actual.
The appropriate reading depends on whether the discourse accepts or merely entertains the
conceded circumstance.

A context in which fatigue is expected to prevent work supplies the
counter-expectation required by concession. The following schemata distinguish
accepted fatigue from a condition merely entertained, while the concessive
ending itself requires no temporal overlap. Here \ird{[[feel-tired]]} names a
verbal stative whose holder is the direct-case pivot, rather than a nominal
predicate in the restricted \ird{-eň} construction:

\pex\label{ex:slot-concession-obstacle}
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek [[feel-tired]]-ouc trástime.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} feel.tired-\Cncs{} work-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Although Marek feels tired, he is working.}
\lingcontext{fatigue is accepted; work defeats the salient expectation}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek [[feel-tired]]-ouc trastáž.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} feel.tired-\Cncs{} work-\Ctpl{}//
\glft \trsl{Even if Marek feels tired, he will work.}
\lingcontext{fatigue is entertained rather than asserted}//
\endgl
\xe

The difference in actuality follows from the stated contexts, while
both retain counter-expectation. Merely surprising accompaniment, as when
someone unexpectedly sings during work, can satisfy integrated \ird{-ěc};
without an expectation linking the singing to the work's non-occurrence, that
surprise alone does not supply the concessive relation.

\section{Conditional constructions}\label{sec:conditional}

\subsection{The two conditional converbs}

Iridian distinguishes two conditional clause-linking relations: an open or
neutral conditional and an expected or predictive conditional. These are
expressed by two converb suffixes, summarised in Table~\ref{tab:conditional}.

\begin{table}[ht]
	\footnotesize
	\caption{Conditional converb suffixes.}\label{tab:conditional}
	\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{L l L L}
		\toprule
		{\sc suffix} & {\sc core meaning} & {\sc discourse profile} & {\sc notes} \\
		\midrule
		\ird{-ej}   & if             & open, neutral, contingent, generic &
			plain stem grade; contextual phonetics apply \\[4pt]
		\ird{-ezmy} & if / when      & expected, likely, scheduled, predictive &
			plain stem grade; see §\,\ref{sec:cond-nopala} \\
		\bottomrule
	\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The conditional converb paradigm distinguishes open \ird{-ej} from expected
\ird{-ezmy}, without dedicated negative suffixes
(§\,\ref{sec:cond-negation}).

\subsection{Semantic distinction: open vs.\ expected conditional}
\label{sec:cond-semantics}

The opposition between \ird{-ej} and \ird{-ezmy} is grounded not in truth-value
alone but in the speaker's \emph{discourse stance toward fulfilment} of the
antecedent. The two forms can be distinguished as follows.

The suffix \ird{-ej} marks an \textbf{open conditional}: the speaker does not
commit to the fulfilment of the antecedent. It covers (a)~open real conditions,
where the outcome is uncertain; (b)~generic or habitual conditions,
where the antecedent expresses a recurrent situation type; (c)~hypothetical or
weakly counterfactual conditions, where speaker commitment is withheld; and
(d)~logical or instructional conditionals, where the if-clause functions as a
premise or rule rather than a prediction. In all these uses, the speaker
positions the antecedent as one possibility among others, without implying that
its occurrence is expected or imminent.

The suffix \ird{-ezmy} marks an \textbf{expected} or \textbf{predictive
conditional}: the speaker treats the fulfilment of the antecedent as likely,
scheduled, or inferable from context. It is used for (a)~events that are
anticipated or planned, (b)~natural-sequence conditionals in narrative or
procedural discourse where the antecedent is presented as a lead-in to the main
event, (c)~predictive \ird{if/when} constructions where occurrence is
anticipated without being asserted, and (d)~conditionals embedded in instructional
or procedural contexts where the antecedent is treated as a standard triggering
condition. The form is closer in discourse function to English \trsl{when} or
\trsl{once} in many of its uses, though the underlying relation remains
conditional.

The distinction thus pairs an epistemic dimension (speaker commitment to
antecedent fulfilment) with a discourse-pragmatic one (how the antecedent is
presented to the interlocutor). Neither suffix encodes temporal sequence
directly; temporal sequencing, where relevant, follows from the nature of the
main-clause event and its interaction with the mood system.

\pex \label{ex:cond-open}
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek zomenej, Janek pjáščev.//
\glb Marek leave-\Cond{} Janek eat-\Irr{}//
\glft \trsl{If Marek leaves, Janek might eat.} \lingcontext{open conditional; speaker uncommitted to Marek leaving; irrealis apodosis}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Dana šegenej, novy zďíčev.//
\glb Dana ask-\Cond{} new bring-\Irr{}//
\glft \trsl{If Dana asks, [one] might bring a new one.} \lingcontext{generic/instructional; open conditional}//
\endgl
\xe

\pex \label{ex:cond-expected}
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek zomenezmy, Janek pjaštáž.//
\glb Marek leave-\ECond{} Janek eat-\Ctpl{}//
\glft \trsl{When/once Marek leaves, Janek will eat.} \lingcontext{expected conditional; departure treated as imminent or scheduled; indicative contemplative apodosis}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Šaltezmy, dá pjaštáž.//
\glb finish-\ECond{} \textsc{1sg} eat-\Ctpl{}//
\glft \trsl{Once it finishes, I will eat.} \lingcontext{natural-sequence; procedural; antecedent is a standard triggering condition}//
\endgl
\xe

\subsection{Negation in conditional antecedents}\label{sec:cond-negation}

The conditional converb suffixes \ird{-ej} and \ird{-ezmy} have no dedicated
negative counterparts. Negation within an embedded finite indicative clause
follows the ordinary verbal pattern with \ird{za-}
(§\,\ref{sec:negation} of Chapter~\ref{ch:negation}), while the attachment and
scope of negation with a conditional converb itself are undocumented. The
finite negative mood suffixes \ird{-é}, \ird{-ku}, and \ird{-ir} do not belong
to the conditional converb paradigm. Conditionality itself does not license
negative indefinites: neither \ird{-ej} nor \ird{-ezmy} supplies the
negative operator required for concord (§\,\ref{sec:nm-negative-concord}).
The locality rule for finite clauses (§\,\ref{sec:complex-negative-concord})
does not establish concord across a converb boundary; the licensing of
negative indefinites inside non-finite conditional antecedents remains
unspecified.

\subsection{Morphophonology: no palatalisation with \ird{-ej}}\label{sec:cond-nopala}

The open conditional \ird{-ej} /ɛj/ selects the plain consonant grade.
Its initial /ɛ/ does not by itself license the palatal/front or mixed
alternants selected by other suffixes (§\,\ref{sec:suffix-classes});
the suffix preserves plain coronal stops, alveolar sibilants, and
inherited velars.

The expected conditional \ird{-ezmy} likewise selects the plain consonant
grade. Neither conditional suffix invokes the antipassive's replacement
pattern. Their grade membership is part of the morphological specification;
their historical development remains to be established independently.

\subsection{Clause type and pivot reference}\label{sec:cond-subj}

Conditional converb clauses are adjunct clauses within the general converb
system described in §\,\ref{sec:converb-chains}. Their internal alignment
follows the ordinary converb alignment: nominative-accusative case marking
applies inside the converb clause. An ordinary active has a direct-case actor
and indirect-case patient, while applicative and causative morphology select
the participant specified in §\,\ref{sec:converb-chains}. The
finite-verb-final rule holds: the conditional converb clause is non-final, and
the main clause supplies the sole finite verb.

Pivot reference in conditional converb clauses follows the default same-pivot
(SS) interpretation established for converbs generally. The absolutive pivot of
the \ird{-ej} or \ird{-ezmy} clause is by default coreferent with the absolutive
pivot of the matrix clause. Different-pivot (DS) readings require overt nominal
pivots or demonstratives in one or both clauses, or must be supported by
discourse context; Iridian has no grammaticalised switch-reference clitic (see
§\,\ref{sec:switch-reference}). In many everyday examples this will coincide
with same-agent reference because ordinary active converbs privilege the
actor; an applicative can instead maintain reference to its applied
participant. The generalisation remains one of pivot continuity.

\subsection{Interaction with irrealis in the main clause}\label{sec:cond-irrealis}

The choice between \ird{-ej} and \ird{-ezmy} correlates with, though does not
strictly determine, the mood of the apodosis. The open conditional \ird{-ej} is
the natural antecedent for main clauses in the irrealis (\ird{-ěv}) or the
negative irrealis (\ird{-ir}), because the speaker's non-commitment to
antecedent fulfilment is compatible with the irrealis framing of the consequent
as counterfactual, hypothetical, or weakly probable. This is the standard
configuration for hypothetical and counterfactual conditional constructions in
Iridian.

The expected conditional \ird{-ezmy} more naturally pairs with an indicative or
predictive main clause, because the antecedent is presented as likely or
scheduled. Such a context favours a predictive consequent, without making
its certainty follow from expectation of the antecedent. The marked
combination of \ird{-ezmy} with an irrealis consequent retains
the anticipated antecedent while presenting the consequent as possible or
counterfactual. Uncertainty about the consequent need not retract expectation
of the antecedent; an explicitly impossible antecedent would instead conflict
with that stance unless the discourse perspective changed.

For the irrealis system and its lexical negative counterpart \ird{-ir},
see §\,\ref{sec:mood} in Chapter~\ref{ch:verbs}.

\pex \label{ex:cond-irr}
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek zomenej, Janek pjáščev.//
\glb Marek leave-\Cond{} Janek eat-\Irr{}//
\glft \trsl{If Marek were to leave, Janek might eat.} \lingcontext{open conditional + irrealis apodosis; standard hypothetical}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek zomenej, Janek pjaštir.//
\glb Marek leave-\Cond{} Janek eat-\NIrr{}//
\glft \trsl{If Marek were to leave, Janek would not eat.} \lingcontext{open conditional + negative irrealis apodosis}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek zomenezmy, Janek pjaštáž.//
\glb Marek leave-\ECond{} Janek eat-\Ctpl{}//
\glft \trsl{When Marek leaves, Janek will eat.} \lingcontext{expected conditional + indicative contemplative apodosis; predicted outcome}//
\endgl
\xe

\subsection{Discourse function}\label{sec:cond-discourse}

Open \ird{-ej} permits a premise to restrict the alternatives in which the
consequent is evaluated, without presenting the premise as expected. Generic
rules, contingent plans, and hypothetical reasoning therefore need not share a
prediction about a particular event. Expected \ird{-ezmy} instead presents the
antecedent as an anticipated trigger, although the resulting `when' or `once'
translation does not assert its occurrence. A procedure may accommodate that
trigger for the purposes of an instruction, without guaranteeing that the
procedure will ever be carried out.

The following constructed counterpart to (\ref{ex:cond-expected}a) holds the
contemplative consequent constant, so that the connective alone changes the
stance towards Marek's departure:
\ex\label{ex:cond-constant-consequent}%
\begingl
\gla Marek zomenej, Janek pjaštáž.//
\glb Marek leave-\Cond{} Janek eat-\Ctpl{}//
\glft \trsl{If Marek leaves, Janek will eat.}//
\endgl
\xe
With \ird{zomenezmy}, departure is anticipated; with \ird{zomenej}, it is
left open. Neither antecedent becomes an assertion simply because the
consequent is predictive. The opposition concerns modal and discourse status,
rather than truth-functional implication; the broader distinction between
natural-language conditions and material implication is discussed by
\cite[ch.~5]{sweetser1990}. Korean conditional endings likewise differ in
modality and consequent restrictions, although their distributions do not
specify the Iridian paradigm \cite[303--306]{sohn2009}.

Completion of a report can be left open or treated as a planned trigger
without changing the following predicate:

\pex\label{ex:slot-condition-expectation}
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek \textnormal{[[report]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}} [[complete]]-ej Janek \textnormal{[[depart]]}\textsubscript{\Ctpl{}}.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} report.\Ind{} complete-\Cond{} Janek.\Dir{} depart-\Ctpl{}//
\glft \trsl{If Marek completes the report, Janek will depart.}
\lingcontext{completion is one contingent possibility}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Marek \textnormal{[[report]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}} [[complete]]-ezmy Janek \textnormal{[[depart]]}\textsubscript{\Ctpl{}}.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} report.\Ind{} complete-\ECond{} Janek.\Dir{} depart-\Ctpl{}//
\glft \trsl{Once Marek completes the report, Janek will depart.}
\lingcontext{completion is anticipated as the departure trigger}//
\endgl
\xe

Abandoning the report frustrates the expectation in (b), whereas (a)
does not conventionally supply that expectation. Neither conditional
antecedent asserts completion of the report. A generic instruction using
(a) can instead apply to each relevant occasion
of completing a report, without predicting any particular completion.

\subsection{Formal analysis: converb features in Distributed Morphology}\label{sec:cond-dm}

In DM terms, the two conditional suffixes are Vocabulary Items inserted at the
defective converb head that underlies all adverbial converb constructions in
Iridian. This head differs from the ordinary finite verbal head in lacking
Asp.Align features (it does not drive perfective/imperfective interpretation or
the nominative-ergative case alternation), and it lacks the \synhead{MoodP}
layer that licenses directive, hortative, and irrealis readings. What the
conditional converb suffixes add is a set of clause-linking and discourse-stance
features beyond the bare \textsc{[conv]} feature shared by all adverbial
converbs. The two conditional exponents have the following feature specifications:

\pex
\a \ird{-ej}: \quad [\textsc{conv}, \textsc{cond}, \textsc{open}]
\hfill non-presuppositional; speaker uncommitted to antecedent fulfilment
\a \ird{-ezmy}: [\textsc{conv}, \textsc{cond}, \textsc{expected}]
\hfill antecedent anticipated or scheduled, without asserted occurrence
\xe

The \textsc{open}/\textsc{expected} opposition encodes discourse stance
(§\,\ref{sec:cond-semantics}), while neither suffix marks negative polarity.
The expression of negation with a conditional converb is undocumented
(§\,\ref{sec:cond-negation}).

\section{Insubordinate conditional clauses}\label{sec:cond-insub}

A conditional converb clause ordinarily functions as an adverbial adjunct to a
matrix clause, as established in §\,\ref{sec:cond-subj}. Under specific
discourse conditions, however, a conditional converb clause may occur without an
overt apodosis. Such \textbf{insubordinate conditional clauses} are not analysed
here as ordinary adjuncts lacking their host through performance ellipsis.
Instead, the default analysis treats them as conditional clauses merged directly
into the discourse periphery as independent, licensed discourse units.

\subsection{Two structures}

The ordinary biclausal conditional follows the structure established in
§\,\ref{sec:converb-chains}: the conditional converb clause is merged as an
adjunct to the matrix \synhead{TP}/\synhead{IP}, scoping over the matrix
proposition (Figure~\ref{fig:cond-ordinary}).

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\begin{forest}
for tree={align=center, l sep=10pt, s sep=14pt}
[\synhead{TP}
  [CondConvP
    [{$\ldots$}]
    [{V$_{\textsc{cond}}$}]
  ]
  [{T$'$}
    [{matrix material}]
    [{V$_{\textsc{finite}}$}]
  ]
]
\end{forest}
\caption{Ordinary conditional: converb clause as adjunct to \synhead{TP}.}
\label{fig:cond-ordinary}
\end{figure}

In the insubordinate use, no matrix \synhead{TP} is projected. Instead, the
conditional converb phrase is selected by a null framing head
(Frame\textsuperscript{0}) and the resulting FrameP is integrated into the
speech-act projection (Figure~\ref{fig:cond-insub}).

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\begin{forest}
for tree={align=center, l sep=10pt, s sep=14pt}

\end{forest}

\label{fig:cond-insub}

The null Frame\textsuperscript{0} head licenses the conditional converb phrase
as an independent discourse act without projecting an overt consequent. In
this analysis, it selects a conditional converb phrase in a warning, offer,
topic frame, or suspended inference. The construction presents the antecedent
as a hypothesis under which the discourse is evaluated; where a consequence
is intended, the interlocutor supplies it pragmatically. These framing
conditions belong to the construction and its discourse context, without
adding a lexical consequence to the conditional ending.

\subsection{Pragmatic functions}

Insubordinate conditional clauses in Iridian cover several pragmatic functions.
In warning or caution use, the speaker presents a potential triggering condition
and leaves the consequence implicit, often to heighten its force. In offer or
invitation use, the antecedent names a condition available to the addressee,
with the result left for inference. In relevance-framing or topic-shift use, the
conditional clause restricts the discourse domain of what follows without
asserting an ordinary conditional proposition. In counterfactual suspension, the
speaker invites the interlocutor to consider a hypothetical space without
committing to any specific outcome.

\pex \label{ex:cond-insub}
\a
\begingl
\gla Zomenej\ldots//
\glb leave-\Cond{}//
\glft \trsl{If he leaves\ldots} \lingcontext{warning; consequence withheld; insubordinate}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Dana šegenej\ldots//
\glb Dana ask-\Cond{}//
\glft \trsl{If Dana asks\ldots} \lingcontext{offer or relevance frame; insubordinate}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Šaltezmy\ldots//
\glb finish-\ECond{}//
\glft \trsl{Once it finishes\ldots} \lingcontext{expected-conditional insubordinate; consequence strongly inferrable from context}//
\endgl
\xe

An infrastructure warning and a scheduled event illustrate the same
contrast without fixing a lexical consequence. Both are lexical-slot
schemata whose conditional forms leave stem allomorphy unspecified:

\pex\label{ex:conditional-slot-standalone}
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[dam]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}} [[leak]]-ej\ldots//
\glb dam.\Dir{} leak-\Cond{}//
\glft \trsl{If the dam leaks\ldots}
\lingcontext{warning; evacuation, inspection, and repair remain different possible responses}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[signal]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}} [[sound]]-ezmy\ldots//
\glb signal.\Dir{} sound-\ECond{}//
\glft \trsl{Once the signal sounds\ldots}
\lingcontext{a scheduled trigger; the discourse makes the next procedural step recoverable}//
\endgl
\xe

The warning in (a) can be understood without recovering one unique omitted
sentence, whereas (b) depends on a salient procedure for its intended sequel.
The expected ending presents the signal as anticipated; it does not itself
identify that sequel or assert that the signal has sounded.

Tamano Okayama Japanese separates a motion question from a suspended
condition through the construction containing \textit{nara}. Takubo's
sentence-final example requires the interrogative \textit{dare} and the
nominalised prejacent \textit{iku N}; its interpretation is a question
about the mover, rather than an antecedent awaiting a consequence
\cite[p.~68, (28)]{takubo2025conditionals}:

\ex\label{ex:japanese-final-nara}%
\begingl
\gla dare ga iku N nara?//
\glb who \textsc{nom} go.\textsc{adn} \Compl{} \textsc{nara}//
\glft \trsl{Who is going?}
\lingcontext{Tamano Okayama Japanese; source gloss typography regularised}//
\endgl
\xe

Ordinary conditional \textit{nara} in the same variety permits a
non-interrogative antecedent and need not select a nominalised clause
\cite[pp.~67--68, §§2.1--2.2]{takubo2025conditionals}.
Iridian expresses the corresponding motion question through a finite
predicate and interrogative \ird{jede}, while conditional \ird{-ej}
can suspend a non-interrogative motion antecedent:

\pex\label{ex:iridian-motion-question-condition}
\a
\begingl
\gla Jede stójime?//
\glb who.\Dir{} go-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Who is going?}
\lingcontext{a finite content question with null Pol and ordinary placement}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[traveller]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}} stojej\ldots//
\glb traveller.\Dir{} go-\Cond{}//
\glft \trsl{If the traveller goes\ldots}
\lingcontext{a warning whose particular consequence is left open}//
\endgl
\xe

Class~A \ird{stoj-} supplies progressive \ird{stójime} with the
ordinary lengthened root grade and open conditional \ird{stojej} with
the plain stem (§§\,\ref{sec:vm-asp}, \ref{sec:cond-nopala}).
The finite question requests the mover's identity, whereas the conditional
frames a possible departure whose relevance is supplied by the exchange.
Iridian thus distinguishes these speech acts through finite clause typing
and conditional converb morphology. In the Japanese variety, the contrast
also involves the selectional and wh-requirements of sentence-final
\textit{nara}; the shared motion situation makes that distributional
difference visible without treating either construction as the translation
of a conditional ending alone.

\subsection{Default form and the -ej/-ezmy contrast in insubordinate use}

The open conditional \ird{-ej} is the natural default form for insubordinate
conditionals. Because \ird{-ej} marks a non-presuppositional,
speaker-uncommitted condition ([\textsc{open}]), it is maximally compatible with
the suspended-inference pragmatics of insubordinate use: the speaker presents
the antecedent as a live possibility without foreclosing the consequent, and the
interlocutor is left to supply it. Warning, offer, and counterfactual-suspension
uses are therefore especially natural with \ird{-ej}.

The expected conditional \ird{-ezmy} can occur in standalone use, most naturally
in predictive or narrative contexts where the antecedent is treated as imminent
or scheduled (see (c) in~\ref{ex:cond-insub} above). This is the marked option:
the anticipated trigger supports this use when the discourse independently
makes the consequence recoverable. The expectation feature concerns the
antecedent, rather than encoding accessibility of an apodosis. Insubordinate
\ird{-ezmy} can also
frame a topic or sequence in procedural discourse (\trsl{once it
finishes\ldots}, \trsl{when they arrive\ldots}), functioning as a
natural-sequence framing rather than a suspended warning.

\subsection{Secondary ellipsis analysis}

A secondary derivation is available where the consequence is locally recoverable
from the preceding discourse. In such cases, a conditional converb clause may be
followed by ellipsis of a recoverable matrix predicate at PF under discourse
identity:

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\begin{forest}
for tree={align=center, l sep=10pt, s sep=14pt}
[\synhead{TP}
  [CondConvP
    [{$\ldots$}]
    [{V$_{\textsc{cond}}$}]
  ]
  [{T$'$\\{\footnotesize [deleted at PF]}}, roof]
]
\end{forest}
\caption{Secondary analysis: matrix clause deleted under recoverability.}
\label{fig:cond-ellipsis}
\end{figure}

This secondary derivation is appropriate where the apodosis is uniquely
recoverable from the immediate discourse context, and it should not be extended
as the default analysis. The primary reason is structural: conditional converbs
are already non-finite, reduced forms that project at most a
\synhead{vP}/\synhead{VoiceP}-level adjunct clause, not a full finite clause.
Positing a silent finite matrix after every insubordinate conditional would be a
stronger claim than the evidence requires, and would treat as syntactically
deficient what is in many discourse contexts a fully licensed, pragmatically
complete utterance. The insubordinate FrameP structure is therefore primary,
with the ellipsis derivation reserved for locally recoverable cases.

\subsection{Diagnostics for the insubordinate conditional}

Four properties distinguish insubordinate conditional clauses from
ordinary adjuncts whose matrix has been omitted in performance.

\textit{Prosodic independence.} An insubordinate conditional carries its own
intonation contour and closes an intonational phrase in spoken Iridian. An
ordinary dependent converb can share an intonational phrase with its matrix,
although internal phonological phrasing remains available
(§\,\ref{sec:converb-phrasing}). Prosodic independence does not require
silence at the boundary or prescribe a particular terminal melody.

\textit{Independent illocutionary force.} An insubordinate conditional can carry
its own illocutionary force---warning, challenge, offer--- independently of any
overt apodosis. A dependent converb adjunct has no independent force; it merely
conditions the illocutionary force of the matrix.

\textit{Non-recovery of a unique apodosis.} Some insubordinate conditionals do
not recover a unique apodosis at all. A warning framing (\trsl{if he
comes\ldots}) or a counterfactual suspension (\trsl{if only\ldots}) opens an
inferential space rather than pointing to a specific recoverable proposition. A
matrix-ellipsis analysis is too strong in such cases.

\textit{Preference for \ird{-ej}.} Insubordinate conditionals show a strong
preference for the open conditional suffix over \ird{-ezmy}. This follows
directly from the feature analysis: [\textsc{open}] conditional stance is
maximally compatible with suspended-inference pragmatics; [\textsc{expected}]
stance concerns the anticipated antecedent, while a recoverable consequence
must be supplied independently by the discourse.

\section{Purposive constructions}\label{sec:purposive}

Purposive \ird{-it} presents an intended or prospective event. A purpose may
subsequently be fulfilled without changing this contribution: `We ran to catch
up with the bus' permits either `and caught it' or `but never caught it'. In a
selected complement, the matrix predicate contributes further restrictions:
wanting or trying need not bring about the complement event, whereas beginning
asserts its inception without entailing completion. The complement's pivot is
controlled by the matrix pivot; this selectional relation is stronger than the
defeasible continuity preference of adverbial chains.

The purposive suffix selects consonant alternants by stem class rather than
through a general palatal-grade rule: \ird{pjašcit}, for example, is the listed Class~I
purposive alternant of \ird{pjašt-}. This /ʃt/--/ʃts/ correspondence does not
extend to every stem before \ird{-it}. As the canonical non-finite
event-complement form (§\,\ref{sec:th-compl-distinction}), the purposive converb
is licensed wherever the matrix predicate selects an event regarded as
projected, volitional, incipient, or purpose-oriented rather than a full
proposition.

An intended activity can remain unfulfilled after the motion undertaken
for that purpose. The following schema permits continuations in which the
purpose is fulfilled or frustrated:

\ex\label{ex:slot-purpose-outcome}%
\begingl
\gla Marek \textnormal{[[hands]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}} sporit stójek.//
\glb Marek.\Dir{} hands.\Ind{} wash-\Purp{} go-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Marek went to wash his hands.}
\lingcontext{compatible with `and washed them' or `but the water was cut off, so he never washed them'}//
\endgl
\xe

The motion is asserted in this episodic reading, while washing is the
selected prospective event whose actor is controlled by Marek. Replacing
\ird{-it} with \ird{-u} would put a completed washing before the motion,
so that the continuation denying washing would conflict with the resulting
sequence. A matrix predicate meaning `begin' would add inception of its
complement activity, whereas `want' or `try' would not by itself guarantee
its fulfilment.

\subsection{The intentional future reanalysis}\label{sec:purp-intentional}

In colloquial speech, the purposive converb has been reanalysed as a
future/intentional marker when no governing verb is present. The first-person
subject pronoun \ird{dá} is typically retained overtly in this construction, as
the purposive suffix alone does not encode person:

\ex
\begingl
\gla Dá travě pjašcit.//
\glb \textsc{1sg} bread.\Ind{} eat-\Purp{}//
\glft \trsl{I'm gonna eat bread.} \lingcontext{colloquial intentional future}//
\endgl
\xe

\section{The purposive-motion construction}\label{sec:purp-motion}

A productive extension of the event-complement pattern is the
\term{purposive-motion construction}, in which a motion verb---\ird{stoj-} `go',
\ird{prit-} `come', \ird{žedet-} `go out', or a closely related verb of directed
or undirected motion---serves as the matrix predicate and the purposive converb
supplies the complement naming the purpose of the motion. The construction
expresses motion undertaken in order to bring about a stated event:

\begin{center}
\term{[\textsc{purp.conv} complement] \quad [motion verb\textsubscript{fin}]}
\end{center}

\noindent No new structural mechanism is required. The purposive converb already
functions as the complement of modal, desiderative, and inchoative matrix
predicates; motion predicates constitute a natural extension of this
complement-selecting class (§\,\ref{sec:th-compl-distinction}). The
complement precedes the motion predicate in accordance with Iridian's
head-final architecture. The selected complement's implicit participant is
controlled by the matrix pivot, whereas ordinary adjunct chains permit the
defeasible continuity relation described in §\,\ref{sec:switch-reference}.

Three overlapping semantic domains are distinguished in the subsections that
follow.

\subsection{Literal purposive motion}\label{sec:purp-motion-literal}

In its core use the construction describes concrete, physically real motion
undertaken for a stated purpose. The mover departs or arrives; the purposive
converb names the reason.

\ex\label{ex:purp-swim}%
\begingl
\gla Tězně knářit stójek.//
\glb lake.\Ind{} swim-\Purp{} go-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{(They) went swimming in the lake.}//
\endgl
\xe

\ex\label{ex:purp-meet}%
\begingl
\gla Josifam všetit žedétek.//
\glb Josif.\Ind{} meet-\Purp{} go.out-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{(He) went out to meet Josif.}//
\endgl
\xe

\ex\label{ex:purp-smoke}%
\begingl
\gla Raznit žedétek.//
\glb smoke-\Purp{} go.out-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{(He) went out to smoke.}//
\endgl
\xe

In each example the matrix mover is null and recovered from discourse;
the implicit mover of the purposive clause shares its reference through
the control relation. Discourse recoverability and pivot control are
therefore separate components of the interpretation. Case marking inside the purposive
complement observes nominative-accusative alignment
(§\,\ref{sec:converb-chains}): the sole argument of an intransitive converb such
as \ird{knář-} `swim' is the direct-case absolutive, and the patient of a
transitive converb such as \ird{všet-} `meet' appears in the indirect (oblique)
case---hence \ird{Josifam} (Josif.\Ind{}) in (\ref{ex:purp-meet}), and
\ird{tězně} (lake.\Ind{}) as the locative argument of `swim' in
(\ref{ex:purp-swim}). The matrix motion verbs \ird{stoj-} and \ird{žedet-} are
themselves intransitive; the mover is their sole participant and surfaces in the
absolutive (direct) case when overt. The ordinary intransitive perfective suffix \ird{-ek} is
inserted at the clause-final finite position, concentrating aspect and alignment
at the right edge of the clause chain in accordance with the finite-verb-final
rule (§\,\ref{sec:converb-chains}).

The stem \ird{razeň-} `smoke' selects reduced \ird{razn-} before the
purposive suffix, yielding \ird{raznit} (§\,\ref{sec:purp-motion-structure}).

\subsection{Prospective motion and purposive
foregrounding}\label{sec:purp-motion-prospective}

A closely related reading arises when motion is directed toward the speech-act
participants and the purposive content is communicatively prominent. The
physical motion recedes and the purpose is foregrounded:

\ex\label{ex:purp-come}%
\begingl
\gla Že pomadit by prítek?//
\glb \textsc{1sg}.\Ind{} talk.to-\Purp{} \textsc{q} come-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Did you come here to talk to me?}//
\endgl
\xe

In (\ref{ex:purp-come}), \ird{prit-} `come' encodes deictic arrival toward the
speaker. The polar question particle \ird{by} realises matrix Pol, while
the selected purposive complement \ird{že pomadit} `to talk to me' undergoes
ordinary placement as a whole before the matrix particle field. Its internal
argument is not independently extracted into the matrix clause; \ird{že} is the indirect-case (oblique) form of the first-person singular
pronoun, appearing here as the patient of the transitive converb \ird{pomad-}.
The null subject (\textsc{2sg}) is recoverable from the interrogative context.
The reading is licensed directly by the semantics of event complementation: the
purposive converb already presents its complement as a prospective,
purpose-oriented event rather than a proposition
(§\,\ref{sec:th-compl-distinction}), and no reanalysis is required to produce it
with deictic-motion verbs.

\subsection{Colloquial extension: intention and
imminence}\label{sec:purp-motion-colloquial}

In informal and colloquial registers, \ird{stoj-} and \ird{prit-} may appear
with reduced or bleached motion semantics, and the purposive-motion construction
approximates intention or near-future imminence. Where the motion component
contributes little to the interpretation, the purposive converb can carry the
prospective content without an overt matrix verb
(§\,\ref{sec:purp-intentional}). This register-marked construction contrasts
with the concrete motion reading, in which departure or arrival remains part of
the asserted event.

\subsection{Structural analysis}\label{sec:purp-motion-structure}

Within the Distributed Morphology framework adopted throughout this grammar, the
motion verbs \ird{stoj-}, \ird{prit-}, \ird{žedet-}, and \ird{nočk-} are
ordinary Vocabulary Items inserted at V\textsuperscript{0}. They are
complement-selecting lexical predicates, not auxiliaries; they are not merged at
Asp\textsuperscript{0} or any higher functional head. The purposive converb
suffix \ird{-it} is the exponent of a converb head (Conv\textsuperscript{0})
bearing [\textsc{+purposive}] features; it heads the purposive complement phrase
merged in the complement position of the motion verb's VP projection. The
resulting structure is identical in all relevant respects to any other
event-complement construction in Iridian: the motion verb selects a non-finite
purposive complement in exactly the way that desiderative or inchoative
predicates do, and no special licensing rule is required.

Morphophonology within the construction follows independently motivated rules.
For the fleeting-vowel stem \ird{razeň-} `smoke', the purposive selects
\ird{razn-}; its hard nasal is part of that lexical bound grade. Appending
\ird{-it} gives \ird{raznit}, with short unstressed \ird{e} absent and the
stressed vowel preserved. The clause-final intransitive perfective
suffix \ird{-ek} is inserted at
Asp\textsuperscript{0}\,$\circ$\,Align\textsuperscript{0} by the general rules
of the aspect-alignment paradigm, with intransitive motion verbs receiving
absolutive alignment (direct-case mover). No additional morphological licensing
is specific to the purposive-motion construction.

\subsection{Limits of the construction: contrast with aspectual
periphrasis}\label{sec:purp-motion-limits}

The motion predicate retains its lexical event and selects a prospective
complement; viewpoint and alignment remain properties of its finite verbal
form. The reduced intentional uses described above do not establish a general
auxiliary construction encoding continuative or directional aspect. The
ordinary motion construction therefore retains the selected-complement
analysis without extending the independently specified aspect inventory.

A final example with an undirected motion verb confirms that the construction is
not restricted to displacement predicates:

\ex\label{ex:purp-run}%
\begingl
\gla Mé autobusam zrolit nóčkek.//
\glb \textsc{1pl} bus.\Ind{} catch.up-\Purp{} run-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{We ran to catch up with the bus.}//
\endgl
\xe

Here \ird{nočk-} `run' is the matrix motion verb; it denotes undirected
locomotion rather than displacement, yet it selects a purposive complement
without difficulty. The object of the converb \ird{zrol-} `catch up with' is
\ird{autobus} `bus', which takes the indirect case by virtue of its patient
role inside the nominative-accusative converb clause. The first-person plural
subject \ird{mé} is overtly expressed, as nothing in the preceding discourse
supplies a recoverable plural topic.

\section{Contrastive linkage and coordination}

\subsection{Contrastive constructions}\label{sec:contrastive}

Contrasting clauses may be coordinated with adversative \ird{má} or
\ird{ozná}; where one clause defeats an expectation associated with the other,
concessive \ird{-ouc} supplies the dependent construction
(§\,\ref{sec:concessive}). Parallel contrast and counter-expectation thus
need not have the same grammatical form.

\subsection{Coordination}\label{sec:coordination}


Coordination is expressed by \ird{a} (additive), \ird{še} (additive, also
complementizer), \ird{má} / \ird{ozná} (adversative), and a set of disjunctives.


Conjunctions link coordinated constituents and clauses
(§\,\ref{sec:coordination}), whereas discourse connectives relate an utterance
to the preceding exchange. The connective \ird{nu} is treated in
§\,\ref{sec:spoken-nu}; clause-final discourse particles, which stand outside
the preverbal sequence, are discussed in §\,\ref{sec:disc-final-particles}.


\section{State-frame adverbial clauses}
\label{sec:state-frame}

Iridian possesses a special non-finite suffix -eň, used with nominal predicates
and with a restricted class of stative predicates to form state-frame adverbial
clauses. The construction expresses meanings such as ‘while X’, ‘when in the
state of X’, and ‘as X’. It is functionally parallel to the ordinary converb
system in that it forms a preverbal adverbial dependent clause, but it is not
part of the regular converb inventory and does not replace an ordinary Asp.Align
morpheme in the verbal paradigm.

Semantically, -eň backgrounds a classification or state as the temporal or
circumstantial frame for the matrix event. The clause does not assert entry into
the state, progression toward it, or completion of a transition. In this it
contrasts sharply with neh-, whose function is explicitly transitional and
inchoative: neh- introduces an event of becoming and projects a full verbal
spine under \synhead{AspP}. A form such as \ird{byl nesu} presents a completed transition, ‘having become
a child’ or ‘after entering child-status’, rather than simply locating the
matrix event within child-status. The state-frame suffix \ird{-eň} supplies
the background reading ‘while a child’ without asserting a prior transition.

Syntactically, a -eň clause behaves like other Iridian adverbial subordinate
clauses. It precedes the matrix clause in accordance with general head-finality,
and its interpretation normally follows the same-pivot continuity pattern unless
context or overt nominal material indicates otherwise. Because the construction
is not an ordinary finite clause, it bears no independent aspect or alignment
morphology; the matrix finite verb remains the sole carrier of finite viewpoint, mood, and
alignment in the chain, following the ordinary converb-chain pattern
(§\,\ref{sec:converb-chains}).


The suffix is primarily used with class-membership nouns and age/status
predicates: byl-eň ‘while a child, as a child’ kral-eň ‘while king, as king’
vdov-eň ‘while a widow’

The phasal particle \ird{so} may occur with the state-frame converb -eň. In such cases
it scopes over the predicative state expressed by the converb and marks that
state as still holding at the reference time of the subordinate clause. Thus
byleň means ‘while a child’, whereas \ird{so byleň} means ‘while still a child’. Any
implication that the state no longer holds at speech time arises pragmatically
from discourse perspective, not from the particle itself.
