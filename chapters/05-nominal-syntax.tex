\chapter{Nominal syntax and reference}\label{ch:nominal-syntax}\label{ch:nouns}

\section{Overview}
\label{sec:nm-overview}

Iridian nouns distinguish direct, transitive, and indirect case in singular
and plural forms, with endings and stem grades distributed across nine
lexical declension classes. A restricted vocative survives in names, kinship
terms, and a small residue of other nouns. Case and number receive cumulative
exponence; definiteness and specificity receive no dedicated article,
proclitic, or suffix. An ordinary nominal can introduce a new participant,
identify a familiar one, or denote a kind, according to the construction and
the discourse in which it occurs.

Prenominal material precedes the noun in the following order:
\begin{center}
(\textsc{dem}) + (\textsc{quant}/\textsc{num}) +
(\textsc{poss}.\Ind{}) + (\textsc{prop.root} / \textsc{stative} / \ird{-ovy}) +
\textsc{root} + (\textsc{case.num})
\end{center}
The template records relative positions rather than independently
segmentable suffixes in every cell; it leaves the finer ordering of
quantificational material unspecified. Its structural interpretation is
developed in §\,\ref{sec:nm-funcseq}.

Schematic clauses use double-bracketed lexical slots with subscripts for
complete inflectional cells (§\,\ref{sec:lexical-slot-schemata}). Nominal
cells specify case and number. A finite transitive perfective cell includes
ordinary patient orientation; neither that orientation nor nominal case
supplies a definite or indefinite interpretation.

\subsection{The root and NP}
\label{sec:nm-root}

The lexical noun occupies the head position of \synhead{NP}. Unlike the verbal
root, which is licensed by a selecting functional head (the Asp.Align morpheme
is obligatory on every finite verb; cf.\ §\,\ref{sec:vm-root}), the nominal root
may surface without an overt case-number ending in consonant-stem and bare-root
classes, whereas thematic classes retain an overt direct ending. Zero
exponence in an inflectional cell does not remove the number and case
structure which licenses its distribution.

The label \synhead{DP} is used as a cover term for the complete extended
nominal expression, including argumental and predicative uses and pronouns,
rather than as a claim that every
nominal contains a determiner head. The independently motivated layers
include \synhead{NP}, \synhead{PossP}, \synhead{NumP}, \synhead{KP}, the
quantificational field, and \synhead{DemP}, where the corresponding material
is present. A \emph{bare nominal} lacks a demonstrative or other overt
reference-specifying expression; it need not be a syntactically unexpanded
NP. No additional silent article is inferred from a definite interpretation.

Absence of an overt article does not independently settle nominal category:
Despić develops an analysis without D for articleless nominals
\cite[§§\,1, 3]{despic2019}, whereas Borik and Espinal argue for DP
structure in Russian bare definite-kind expressions using additional
syntactic diagnostics \cite[§\,3.2]{borikespinal2019}. These alternatives
motivate keeping the structural analysis of Iridian distinct from the
interpretation of any single nominal. The minimum structure selected
within postpositional complements is considered separately in
§\,\ref{sec:nm-postp-structure}.

\section{Articleless nominal reference}
\label{sec:nm-def}

An ordinary nominal supplies descriptive content without requiring the
speaker to select a grammatical definiteness value. Definite, indefinite,
specific, anaphoric, and generic interpretations are distinguished by their
semantic contribution and discourse conditions, not by five nominal
paradigms. A bare noun can therefore identify an accessible referent just
as it can introduce one; an English article in its translation is no
evidence for an unpronounced Iridian counterpart.

\subsection{Contextual identification and discourse introduction}
\label{sec:nm-def-semantics}
\label{sec:nm-bare-noun}
\label{sec:nm-def-restrictions}

A newly noticed participant can be introduced existentially, while prior
discourse or the speech situation can make the same nominal description
sufficient to identify an individual. The following controlled pair holds
the noun, case, predicate, and aspect constant:

\pex\label{ex:nm-contextual-reference}
\a
\begingl
\glpreamble \lingcontext{A child has just been noticed entering the room.}//
\gla Byl zdrávime.//
\glb child.\Dir{} sleep-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{A child is sleeping.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\glpreamble \lingcontext{The interlocutors have already been discussing a particular child.}//
\gla Byl zdrávime.//
\glb child.\Dir{} sleep-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{The child is sleeping.}//
\endgl
\xe

The second reading depends on identifying the relevant child, not on the
word \ird{byl} encoding familiarity. If two children are equally salient,
a bare description may be ambiguous, and a demonstrative, possessor, fuller
description, or explicit clarification can resolve the intended reference.
Neither the absence of such material nor direct case requires an indefinite
reading.

Uniqueness and anaphoric familiarity are distinct sources of definite
interpretation in Schwarz's comparative account
\cite[§§\,2.1, 2.3--2.4]{schwarz2019}, while Coppock and Beaver distinguish
definiteness from individual-denoting determinacy and existential import
\cite[§§\,1--2]{coppockbeaver2015}. These distinctions supply analytical
diagnostics; they do not establish that an Iridian bare nominal has the
lexical semantics of an article in another language. Where a construction
asserts existence, as in an existential presentation
(§\,\ref{sec:existential-presentatives}), that assertion belongs to the
construction and must be distinguished from the identification of its pivot.

\subsection{Kinds, mass reference, and institutional interpretation}
\label{sec:nm-kinds-mass}

Bare \ird{vlak} \trsl{wolf} occurs as the kind-denoting topic of
\ird{Vlak staže na hlučna} \trsl{The wolf howls at night}
(\ref{ex:tc-generic-topic}). This established use combines nominal kind
reference with a generic predication: an episodic reference to one wolf and
a reference to the wolf kind require different interpretations even though
the noun has the same form. Despić's and Borik and Espinal's treatments
derive bare kind interpretations under different structural assumptions
\cite{despic2019,borikespinal2019}; their alternatives do not themselves
settle the structure or range of kind readings in Iridian.

Class~VIII \ird{pivo} \trsl{beer} provides a separate mass--count contrast.
Its direct plural \ird{piva} selects servings, varieties, or other countable
instances (§\,\ref{sec:nm-num}). In either number, contextual identification
is independent of the ordinary inflection: \trsl{the beer} may refer to a
salient quantity, and \trsl{the beers} to identifiable servings or varieties.
The atomic cardinal schema in §\,\ref{sec:nm-semantic-types} supplies no
automatic analysis of mass measurement or mass-kind reference.

Institutional activity interpretations require separate diagnostics from
generic and situationally unique uses. English \trsl{go to the hospital}
can describe a conventional institutional activity without identifying one
particular hospital; these \emph{weak definites} are distinct from the
\emph{weak-article definites} discussed by Schwarz
\cite[§§\,1--2, 4.1]{williams2019}. Their availability with Iridian bare
nominals remains unestablished. Contextual identification of a building
alone does not supply the institutional activity reading.

\subsection{Anaphoric dependencies and situation-relative interpretation}
\label{sec:nm-reference-indices}

A discourse may establish an individual which a later nominal resumes.
An assignment \(g\) and index \(i\) can represent that dependency, while a
resource situation \(s\) restricts the domain in which a description is
evaluated. If \(P_s\) is the relevant property, an anaphoric interpretation
requires its recovered value \(g(i)\) to satisfy \(P_s\); a contextually
unique description can identify its referent without an antecedent bearing
the same nominal wording. The notation records two possible interpretative
routes, not a lexical retrieval instruction or a pronounced or silent article.
Schwarz distinguishes situation-relative uniqueness from indexed
anaphoricity \cite[§§\,2.3--2.4]{schwarz2019}; the comparative contrast
helps identify the dependencies which an Iridian analysis must explain.

Relational interpretation introduces a further dependency. In a description
of someone's head, the possessor must be resolved before the resulting
property of heads can identify a referent (§\,\ref{sec:nm-implicit-poss}).
A house--door or book--author bridging context likewise supplies a relation
to an antecedent rather than identity with it: retrieving a book does not
itself retrieve its author. Schwarz's German contrasts distinguish
part--whole from producer bridging \cite[§\,2.3, examples~(11)--(12)]{schwarz2019};
they do not establish an Iridian morphological division. The availability
and precise licensing of a particular bridging reading require independent
contextual diagnostics, with possessor and possessed referents kept distinct.

A fixed assignment represents one discourse value; it does not derive
covariation with a quantifier. In the schema \trsl{Every child who bought a
book read the book}, the relevant book must vary with the child--book
dependency. This requires assignments \(g_d\), resource situations \(s_d\),
or both to vary with the quantified cases \(d\), together with a binding or
discourse-update mechanism making the appropriate value accessible
\cite[§\,2.4 and note~10]{schwarz2019}. The English schema identifies the
dependency to be explained; it supplies no Iridian acceptability judgement.
Reference resolution is also independent of the clause's aboutness relation
(§\,\ref{sec:tc-anchoring-interface}).

\subsection{Semantic types and nominal composition}
\label{sec:nm-semantic-types}

The lexical noun ordinarily contributes a property, written \(P_s\) at a
fixed evaluation situation. Nominal modification and quantification must
preserve the distinctions between that property, an individual, and a
generalised quantifier. A containing construction can existentially compose
with a scope predicate \(R\), or, where contextual uniqueness is independently
licensed, obtain an individual-denoting interpretation:
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \text{existential composition:}\quad
    &\exists x[P_s(x)\land R(x)],\\
  \text{contextually unique reference:}\quad
    &\iota x P_s(x).
\end{aligned}
\]
The second expression is defined when exactly one relevant individual
satisfies the situation-restricted property. Neither operation is an
automatic lexical meaning of a null determiner. Anaphoric assignment,
quantification, and kind interpretation supply distinct dependencies, which
must be motivated by their respective constructions. Separating nominal
predication from argumental type shifts follows the analytical distinction
in Coppock and Beaver's account \cite[§\,1]{coppockbeaver2015}, without
requiring all Iridian arguments to be derived by one shift.

A property supplied to a universal quantifier remains unsaturated. Writing
\(A\) for its restriction and \(R\) for the predicate in its scope, a
schematic universal can be represented as:
\[
 \mathrm{EVERY}(A)(R)=\forall x[A(x)\mathbin{\rightarrow}R(x)].
\]
This representation illustrates composition in the established quantified
nominals (§\,\ref{sec:nm-quant}); it does not assign identical
distributivity or number semantics to \ird{nět}, \ird{niže}, and
\ird{náh}. A contextually restricted property such as
\(\lambda x[P_s(x)\land C(x)]\) also differs from identity with one
indexed individual. Etxeberria and Giannakidou's non-saturating restrictions
provide a comparative treatment of this distinction
\cite[§§\,2.1, 3]{etxeberriagiannakidou2019}; the Iridian quantifiers do
not acquire an additional determiner merely to express domain restriction.

Numerals supply cardinality independently of the nominal Vocabulary Item.
In \ird{hroná lobėk} \trsl{three apples}, singular noun morphology is
compatible with a plural interpretation. A schematic cardinal modifier can
form the property
\(\lambda X[{}^{*}P_s(X)\land |\operatorname{Atoms}(X)|=3]\), where
\({}^{*}P_s\) contains nonempty sums of atomic apples and
\(\operatorname{Atoms}(X)\) is the set of atomic members of \(X\)
\cite[pp.\,15, 24--25]{marti2020}. An intersective modifier likewise
combines with an unsaturated property, while a possessor supplies the
relevant relational argument (§\,\ref{sec:nm-implicit-poss}). A
demonstrative can then identify a deictically located referent satisfying the
resulting description, without agreement in definiteness.

\subsection{Speaker-specific reference}
\label{sec:nm-spec}

A speaker may have a particular individual or group in mind without making
its identity shared. This distinction is available to an ordinary nominal;
it is not encoded by a separate proclitic or a dedicated specificity
paradigm. Speaker identifiability, free choice, and scope are different
questions: Kagan distinguishes these dimensions in Russian indefinite
interpretation \cite{kagan2011}, and the same distinction is useful when
formulating Iridian diagnostics. Having an intended witness does not by
itself establish wide scope or existence in the actual world under a modal
or negative operator.

The same courier can consequently be introduced with or without a
speaker-specific intention, or referred to after prior discussion, without
changing the nominal cell:

\pex\label{ex:nm-slot-reference-choice}
\a
\begingl
\glpreamble \lingcontext{A courier has just come into view; the speaker has no prior identification.}//
\gla \textnormal{[[courier]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Sg{}} méžime.//
\glb courier.\Dir{}.\Sg{} wait-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{A courier is waiting.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\glpreamble \lingcontext{The speaker is thinking of a particular courier whose identity has not been shared.}//
\gla \textnormal{[[courier]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Sg{}} méžime.//
\glb courier.\Dir{}.\Sg{} wait-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{A certain courier is waiting.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\glpreamble \lingcontext{The interlocutors have already discussed the courier assigned to this delivery.}//
\gla \textnormal{[[courier]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Sg{}} méžime.//
\glb courier.\Dir{}.\Sg{} wait-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{The courier is waiting.}//
\endgl
\xe

The translations make the contextual contrast explicit; no contrast in the
Iridian surface form requires either knowledge or ignorance of the witness.
The scope of a particular interpretation must be established in a complete
construction, not read off the nominal's initial position.

\subsection{Case and reference}
\label{sec:nm-case-reference}

Case identifies a nominal's structural relation, whereas contextual
identification concerns its referent. A perfective transitive patient can
be newly introduced or familiar while retaining direct case; a progressive
patient retains indirect case under the same alternatives. Number and
case are represented by \synhead{NumP} and \synhead{KP}, whose ordinary
exponents and stem selection remain those of Chapter~\ref{ch:nominal-morphology}.
No referential operation requires an additional morphological layer above K.
The relation between these projections and prenominal material is set out
in §\,\ref{sec:nm-funcseq}.

\section{Demonstratives and quantification}
\label{sec:nm-upper}

\subsection{The demonstrative system and noun-internal DemP}
\label{sec:nm-dem}

Iridian demonstratives form a three-way deictic system with proximal, medial,
and distal values. The same paradigm has three distinct morphosyntactic
realisations: an adnominal demonstrative, a pronominal demonstrative, and an
indirect-case possessive demonstrative. The noun-internal analysis developed
here concerns the adnominal realisation, namely \synhead{DemP}, while the pronominal and possessive uses draw on the same
case-inflecting paradigm. Their positions within the noun phrase are set out
in §\,\ref{sec:nm-np-structure}.

\begin{table}[ht]
  \footnotesize
  \caption{Demonstrative paradigm relevant to noun-internal and
  pronominal syntax.}\label{tab:nm-dem-paradigm}
  \begin{tabularx}{0.7\textwidth}{L l l l}
    \toprule
                 & {\sc direct} & {\sc transitive} & {\sc indirect} \\
    \midrule
    Proximal     & \ird{tak}  & \ird{tam}  & \ird{ten} \\
    Medial       & \ird{tuto} & \ird{toho} & \ird{taly} \\
    Distal       & \ird{je}   & \ird{jaky} & \ird{jení} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\noindent Within the noun phrase, the adnominal demonstrative realises
\synhead{DemP}, the outermost noun-internal demonstrative projection. The
working hierarchy is \synhead{DemP} $>$ \synhead{KP} $>$ \synhead{NumP} $>$
\synhead{PossP} $>$ \synhead{NP}, matching the descriptive order
\textsc{demonstrative} $>$ \textsc{poss}.\Ind{} $>$ \textsc{head}
given in §\,\ref{sec:nm-np-structure}. Demonstratives stand outside the
possessive grouping, whereas possessors remain inside the case-bearing
nominal domain. Co-occurrence, as in \ird{tuto že tom}, shows directly
that demonstratives and possessors cannot occupy one structural slot.
The relative ordering of additional quantificational material remains the
conservative field described in §\,\ref{sec:nm-quant}.

\begin{center}
\begin{forest}
  syntax tree
  [\synhead{DemP}
    [{\textit{adnominal demonstrative}}]
    [\synhead{KP}
      [\synhead{NumP}
        [\synhead{PossP}
          [{\textit{oblique possessor}.\Ind{} / $\varnothing$}]
          [{\synhead{NP}\\\textit{head noun}}]
        ]
        [{Num\textsuperscript{0}\\{\footnotesize number}}]
      ]
      [{K\textsuperscript{0}\\{\footnotesize case}}]
    ]
  ]
\end{forest}
\end{center}

The core semantics of the demonstrative layer is deictic: proximal indexes a
referent near the speaker, medial a referent near the addressee, and distal a
referent away from both. The topic-proximal, addressee-linked medial, and
obviative or backgrounded distal uses (§\,\ref{sec:nm-dem-pron}) are
pragmatic extensions of those lexical deictic values.
The formal analysis therefore keeps lexical deixis and discourse tendency
distinct.

The indirect demonstratives
\ird{ten}, \ird{taly}, and \ird{jení} may occupy the ordinary possessor slot
before the noun, as in \ird{ten dum}. In such forms the demonstrative is not the
outer deictic determiner of the noun phrase; it is an indirect-case possessor
inside \synhead{PossP}. The grammar therefore allows two distinct noun-internal
positions fed by the same demonstrative system: adnominal \synhead{DemP} outside
\synhead{KP}, and demonstrative possessors inside \synhead{PossP}.

The difference between an outer demonstrative and a demonstrative
possessor is visible with a single head noun:

\pex\label{ex:nm-slot-demonstrative-ownership}
\a
\begingl
\gla Tak \textnormal{[[boat]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Sg{}} \textnormal{[[leak]]}\textsubscript{\Prog{}}.//
\glb \Dem{}.\Prox{}.\Dir{} boat.\Dir{}.\Sg{} leak-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{This boat is leaking.} \lingcontext{the boat itself is deictically selected}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Ten \textnormal{[[boat]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Sg{}} \textnormal{[[leak]]}\textsubscript{\Prog{}}.//
\glb \Dem{}.\Prox{}.\Ind{} boat.\Dir{}.\Sg{} leak-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{This one's boat is leaking.} \lingcontext{the proximal referent supplies the possessor; the boat remains direct}//
\endgl
\xe

In the first clause, \ird{tak} identifies the boat; in the second,
\ird{ten} identifies its possessor. The indirect demonstrative remains
inside the possessive nominal even though the containing phrase bears
direct case as the sole clausal participant.

\subsection{Demonstratives and referential identification}
\label{sec:nm-dem-reference}

An adnominal demonstrative can identify a referent without an additional
nominal exponent. Thus \ird{tak byl} suffices for \trsl{this child}; the
noun retains its ordinary case-number form. Prosody and discourse may give
the expression an emphatic or contrastive interpretation, but no separate
definiteness agreement is required between the demonstrative and noun.
Deictic identification also differs from familiarity: a speaker can point
out a child not previously mentioned, just as a bare noun can resume an
already familiar child.

\ex
\begingl
\gla tak byl//
\glb \Dem{}.\Prox{} child.\Dir{}//
\glft \trsl{this child}//
\endgl
\xe

\subsection{Pronominal demonstratives and third-person reference}
\label{sec:nm-dem-pron}

Iridian lacks independent third-person personal pronouns. The full demonstrative
paradigm therefore serves as the ordinary third-person referential series
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-dem}). This pronominal use is not identical to noun-internal
\synhead{DemP}, because no lexical head noun is present inside the expression,
but it is derived from the same underlying demonstrative system and must be kept
distinct from the noun-internal ordering facts summarised in
§\,\ref{sec:nm-np-structure}.

In discourse, proximal forms commonly track the current topic or most accessible referent, medial forms
a secondary or addressee-linked referent, and distal forms a backgrounded,
contrastive, or obviative referent. In formal terms, these effects are best
treated as interpretive extensions available to pronominal demonstratives, not
as separate lexical paradigms (§\,\ref{sec:nm-dem}).

\ex
\begingl
\glpreamble \lingcontext{Constructed sequence contrasting three discourse referents.}//
\gla Tak ščének, tuto pjášček, je zemácek.//
\glb \Prox{}.\Dir{} arrive-\Pf{}, \Med{}.\Dir{} eat-\Antip{}-\Pf{}, \Dist{}.\Dir{} run-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{He arrived, he ate, that one ran.}//
\endgl
\xe

\subsection{The reduced anaphoric series}
\label{sec:nm-dem-reduced}

Alongside the full paradigm, Iridian has a reduced anaphoric series
\ird{i}/\ird{ji}/\ird{ni}, corresponding to distal
\ird{je}/\ird{jaky}/\ird{jení}. These forms are restricted to pronominal use,
only when the referent is already highly accessible in the discourse, and they
are excluded as ordinary adnominal demonstratives of the type described in
§\,\ref{sec:nm-np-structure}.

Semantically and pragmatically, the reduced series signals weak anaphoric
resumption rather than full deictic pointing. Formally, it belongs to the
demonstrative system, but it is not the spellout of noun-internal
\synhead{DemP}. A natural DM treatment is to analyse \ird{i}/\ird{ji}/\ird{ni}
as specialised pronominal Vocabulary Items competing with the full distal series
under a discourse-anaphoric high-accessibility condition; see again
§\,\ref{sec:nm-dem}.

\pex
\a \ird{je} \trsl{that one; fully deictic distal}
\a \ird{i} \trsl{he/she; weak anaphoric resumption}
\xe

\subsection{Numerals and the quantificational field}
\label{sec:nm-quant}

Iridian places numerals and quantifiers prenominally. They belong to the upper
nominal field, but the presently available evidence does not force a
fine-grained cartographic split among cardinals, adnominal interrogatives, and
universal quantifiers or negative indefinites. The grammar treats them as
occupants of a single quantificational modifier field between the demonstrative
domain and the lower \synhead{NP}-internal material.

Cardinal numerals require the noun within their own nominal phrase to appear
in its \emph{singular} form, regardless of the quantity expressed. Singular nominal morphology under
cardinals has parallels in Hungarian, Finnish, and Turkish
\cite[§\,1]{marti2020}, although this comparison concerns number marking
rather than the case constructions of those languages. In Iridian, the cardinal
specifies the quantity denoted by the phrase, while the noun's case-number slot
is realised by the singular Vocabulary Item.

\pex
\a \ird{hroná lobėk} \trsl{three apples} \hfill\textit{(cardinal $+$ \textsc{sg})}
\a \ird{hroná lúbce} \hfill\textit{(cardinal $+$ \textsc{pl}, ungrammatical)}
\xe

\noindent In DM terms, the fused [K$+$Num] head inside a cardinally-quantified
\synhead{DP} is spelled out by the singular Vocabulary Item: the Q head
(\synhead{QP}) saturates the number semantics of the expression, so
Num\textsuperscript{0} within \synhead{KP} receives no [+pl] feature to trigger
a plural VI. This morphological specification leaves the cardinal phrase's
plural interpretation intact; semantic number composition is distinguished
from argumental saturation in §\,\ref{sec:nm-semantic-types}.

The exact-cardinality interpretation is supplied by the numeral, while
approximate readings depend on intonation and contextual hedging. The
singular requirement applies within the numeral's own nominal phrase;
it does not extend into an independent partitive complement
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-ext-indirect}). In \ird{ona pivum štava}
\trsl{one mug of beer}, the numeral quantifies the mug, while indirect
\ird{pivum} specifies its contents.

Cardinal singular morphology persists when the quantified phrase changes
its clausal role:

\pex\label{ex:nm-slot-cardinal-case}
\a
\begingl
\gla Hroná \textnormal{[[lantern]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Sg{}} \textnormal{[[swing]]}\textsubscript{\Prog{}}.//
\glb three lantern.\Dir{}.\Sg{} swing-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Three lanterns are swinging.} \lingcontext{the cardinal phrase is the sole participant}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[worker]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Sg{}} hroná \textnormal{[[lantern]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}.\Sg{}} \textnormal{[[carry]]}\textsubscript{\Prog{}}.//
\glb worker.\Dir{}.\Sg{} three lantern.\Ind{}.\Sg{} carry-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{A worker is carrying three lanterns.} \lingcontext{the cardinal phrase is an imperfective patient}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Hroná \textnormal{[[lantern]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Sg{}} \textnormal{[[worker]]}\textsubscript{\Trs{}.\Sg{}} \textnormal{[[carry]]}\textsubscript{\Trs{}.\Pf{}}.//
\glb three lantern.\Dir{}.\Sg{} worker.\Trs{}.\Sg{} carry-\Trs{}-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{A worker carried three lanterns.} \lingcontext{the cardinal phrase is the direct patient of an ordinary active perfective}//
\endgl
\xe

All three phrases denote three lanterns, while the noun uses the
singular cell appropriate to its case. The numeral's quantity remains
constant across the indirect--direct alternation of the patient.

The Iridian singular requirement differs from the optional absence of a
plural marker in the Japanese and Korean classifier phrases compared by
Kawahara. Holding the description \trsl{three children} constant makes the
difference in nominal structure explicit:

\pex\label{ex:nm-comparative-cardinals}
\a
\begingl
\glpreamble \textit{Japanese} \cite[example~6a, p.~341]{kawahara2025}//
\gla sannin-no kodomo(-tati)//
\glb three.\textsc{cl}-\textsc{gen} child(-\textsc{assoc})//
\glft \trsl{three children}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\glpreamble \textit{Korean} \cite[example~5b, p.~341]{kawahara2025}//
\gla ai(-tul) sey myeng//
\glb child(-\textsc{pl}) three \textsc{cl}//
\glft \trsl{three children}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\glpreamble \textit{Iridian}//
\gla hroná byl//
\glb three child.\Dir{}.\Sg{}//
\glft \trsl{three children}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\glpreamble \textit{Iridian; constructed clause}//
\gla Hroná byl ščének.//
\glb three child.\Dir{}.\Sg{} arrive-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Three children arrived.}
\lingcontext{a bounded arrival by three children; the cardinal phrase is the sole participant}//
\endgl
\xe

The Japanese phrase places the numeral--classifier constituent before the noun with
\textit{-no}, whereas the illustrated Korean phrase places the noun before
its numeral and classifier; both permit the parenthesised plural marker.
Iridian directly combines its cardinal with a singular case-number form,
which remains singular when the phrase enters the finite arrival clause.
The verb supplies perfective viewpoint without number agreement. These
contrasts separate numerical interpretation, nominal inflection and the
expression of a counting unit: the Iridian description requires neither a
pronounced classifier nor a plural noun after \ird{hroná}.

Adnominal interrogatives pattern with the same prenominal field. \ird{Jak}
selects from a contextually restricted set, whereas the other interrogatives
simply introduce the relevant open variable. In determiner use they modify the
noun phrase directly rather than occupying the clause-level \emph{wh}-position
themselves.

\pex
\a \ird{jede ruščevní svirkošc} \trsl{what Russian author}
\a \ird{jak ruščevní svirkošc} \trsl{which Russian author}
\xe

Universal quantifiers and negative indefinites can likewise be adnominal: \ird{nět študent}
\trsl{all students}, \ird{niže jec} \trsl{all dogs}, \ird{náh študent}
\trsl{each student}, \ird{žé tóm} \trsl{not one book}. Negative indefinites,
including pronominal \ird{deno} \trsl{nothing}, require clausal negative
licensing (§\,\ref{sec:nm-negative-concord}); their adnominal use restricts
the existential variable to the following noun's denotation. Negative
existence is expressed by the existential predicate
(§\,\ref{sec:existential-constructions}), whose suppletive \ird{zad-}
realises the same clausal polarity dependency.

\subsection{Personal pronouns as full DPs}
\label{sec:nm-pron-personal}

First- and second-person pronouns are best treated as fully projected DPs whose
nominal content is supplied by person features rather than by a lexical noun
root. They inflect for the same three productive cases as lexical nouns; their
indirect forms serve as oblique possessors in the analytic possessive
construction described in §\,\ref{sec:nm-poss}. Iridian lacks a separate
third-person personal-pronoun paradigm; that function is taken over by the
demonstrative system described in
§§\,\ref{sec:nm-dem}--\ref{sec:nm-dem-reduced}.

\subsection{The reflexive as local nominal anaphor}
\label{sec:nm-pron-refl}

The reflexive is not a discourse-tracking pronoun but a true anaphor. Its
interpretation depends on a local antecedent in the clausal domain, and it
competes with demonstrative third-person forms precisely by forcing coreference
where demonstratives would permit disjoint reference.

\begin{table}[ht]
  \footnotesize
  \caption{Reflexive anaphor forms.}
  \label{tab:nm-reflexive}
  \begin{tabularx}{0.5\textwidth}{L l}
    \toprule
    {\sc case} & {\sc form} \\
    \midrule
    Direct     & \ird{svem} \\
    Transitive & \ird{svě}  \\
    Indirect   & \ird{svy}  \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\pex\label{ex:nm-reflexive-connectivity}
\a
\begingl
\gla Svem Marku védnik.//
\glb \Refl{}.\Dir{} Marėk.\Trs{} see-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Marėk saw himself.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla I Marku védnik.//
\glb 3.\Dist{}.\Dir{} Marėk.\Trs{} see-\textsc{trs}-\Pf{}//
\glft \trsl{Marėk saw him.}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent The reflexive reading in (\ref{ex:nm-reflexive-connectivity}a)
can be indexed as \ird{svem}\textsubscript{$i$}
\ird{Marku}\textsubscript{$i$}: the actor is the antecedent of the
direct-case patient. In (\ref{ex:nm-reflexive-connectivity}b),
\ird{i}\textsubscript{$j$} instead permits a distinct referent from
\ird{Marku}\textsubscript{$i$}. These indices specify the readings under
discussion; they are not nominal exponents or a claim that every demonstrative
must have disjoint reference.

The actor's thematic position is structurally above the patient complement
of V (§\,\ref{ch:verbs}). The local relation needed for the reflexive can
therefore be represented independently of the pronounced patient--actor order:
\[
 [_{\mathrm{VoiceP}}\ \mathrm{actor}_{i}
   [_{\mathrm{VP}}\ \mathrm{svem}_{i}\ V]\ \mathrm{Voice}].
\]
This is a representation of argument structure, not an additional surface
order. The reflexive requires connectivity with its local antecedent even
when it precedes that antecedent. Such connectivity does not, by itself,
establish obligatory reconstruction in every interpretive respect. Ordinary
placement pronounces the patient above the actor while preserving the lower
occurrence at which its local anaphoric dependency is interpreted
(§\,\ref{sec:th-particle-framework}); the pronounced order creates no new
antecedent relation. The distribution
across causative, passive and nominal domains requires construction-specific
antecedent conditions; the causative and autobenefactive conditions are
specified in §\,\ref{sec:valency-reflexives}, while discourse prominence
alone supplies no antecedent relation.

The same referential contrast appears with an imperfective indirect
patient, whose case differs from the direct reflexive above:

\pex\label{ex:nm-slot-reflexive-patient}
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[performer]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Sg{}} svy \textnormal{[[paint]]}\textsubscript{\Prog{}}.//
\glb performer.\Dir{}.\Sg{} \Refl{}.\Ind{} paint-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{A performer is painting themself.} \lingcontext{the performer is both painter and painted participant}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[performer]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Sg{}} jení \textnormal{[[paint]]}\textsubscript{\Prog{}}.//
\glb performer.\Dir{}.\Sg{} \Dem{}.\Dist{}.\Ind{} paint-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{A performer is painting that person.} \lingcontext{the distal referent is another participant in this context}//
\endgl
\xe

Indirect \ird{svy} enforces the local reflexive relation; indirect
\ird{jení} instead supplies deictic reference, with distinct reference
specified by the second context. Patient case is constant, so the contrast
concerns anaphoric dependence rather than alignment.

The intensifying \trsl{own} reading of a reflexive is analysed as an extension
of local coreference within the anaphoric domain, without a separate possessive
adjective.

neither ordinary placement nor marked focus alone determines whether the
clause is interrogative. The same

The negative series requires local clausal negative licensing; its distribution,
examples and analysis are given in §\,\ref{sec:nm-negative-concord}.

\subsection{The functional sequence and linear order}
\label{sec:nm-funcseq}

The canonical linear order of the Iridian noun phrase is:

\begin{center}
\textsc{dem} $>$ \textsc{quant/num} $>$
\textsc{poss}.\Ind{} $>$ \textsc{prop.root / stative / -ovy} $>$
\textsc{head} + \textsc{case.num}
\end{center}

The sequence combines a head-final suffixal spine with prenominal
modifiers. It records relative slots without establishing the finer ordering
or interpretation of every possible combination of quantifiers and
demonstratives.

\pex
\a \ird{tak študencí tóm} \trsl{this student's book}
\a \ird{jak ruščevní svirkošc} \trsl{which Russian author}
\xe

The functional layers are motivated by their independent nominal
contributions. \synhead{DP} remains the cover term defined in
§\,\ref{sec:nm-root}, not an additional obligatory head above them.
Table~\ref{tab:nm-projections} sets out the working sequence from the
lexical root to the demonstrative domain.

The working interpretation of the Mirror Principle in
§\,\ref{sec:vm-overview} relates morphological constituency to syntactic
composition, subject to independently specified realisation mechanisms
\cite{baker1985,harley2011}. Cumulative case-number exponence does not make
K and Num identical in the syntax, and the absence of a separate exponent
in one cell does not remove the corresponding structural relation.

Head-final linearisation orders an inflectional complement before its head,
whereas formation of a morphological word requires an attachment relation;
affixation and head movement are themselves distinct properties
\cite{harley2013b}. In Iridian, PF combines the nominal host with the
immediately following fused [K$+$Num] exponent. The schematic morphological
constituency is $[N\text{--}\mathrm{K.Num}]$; the attachment does not
incorporate prenominal dependents or cross into a possessor's nominal domain.
Class-conditioned allomorphy and stem grades remain those of the ordinary
paradigms (§§\,\ref{sec:nm-case}, \ref{sec:nm-num}).

\noindent The higher projections are shown where their independently

prenominal quantificational material rather than asserting one uniform
semantic type or a finer numeral--quantifier hierarchy. Composition must
therefore respect the distinctions in §\,\ref{sec:nm-semantic-types}; the
diagram does not by itself license every combination or establish its scope.
No silent head is inserted merely to derive a definite interpretation or to
force the overt material into specifier positions.

\subsection{The oblique possessor in the DP}
\label{sec:nm-poss}
\label{sec:nm-poss-struct}

Iridian expresses possession by means of an oblique (indirect-case) noun phrase
in prenominal position (§\,\ref{sec:nm-ext-indirect}). The oblique possessor
\ird{Marcí} in \ird{Marcí dum} \trsl{Marėk's house} occupies a structural
position immediately above the head \synhead{NP} and inside the \synhead{KP}
domain, below noun-internal \synhead{DemP}. In terms
of the \synhead{DP} hierarchy, this is the prenominal dependent position of
\synhead{PossP}.

The possessor bears the general indirect case, whose nominal uses include
material, source, and partitive dependence. The same morphological value also
marks core arguments in verbal clauses, including imperfective patients and
causative causees; indirect case therefore does not diagnose peripheral
argument status. Nominal relational licensing and clausal argument licensing
are distinct dependencies which receive the same K realisation.

\begin{center}
\begin{forest}
  syntax tree
  [\synhead{KP}
    [\synhead{NumP}
      [\synhead{PossP}
        [{\textit{oblique possessor}.\Ind{}}]
        [{\synhead{NP}\\\textit{head noun}}]
      ]
      [{Num\textsuperscript{0}\\{\footnotesize number}}]
    ]
    [{K\textsuperscript{0}\\{\footnotesize case ending}}]
  ]
\end{forest}
\end{center}

\noindent The possessor appears before the head noun in the surface string,
consistent with head-final base-generation of specifiers. The entire possessive
phrase [\synhead{PossP} oblique-N head-N] forms a constituent that is then
inflected for number and case by Num and K. The possessor retains its own
indirect case independently of the containing phrase's case.

\subsection{Implicit possessors and relational nominal interpretation}
\label{sec:nm-implicit-poss}

Body-part, kinship and part--whole nouns permit an unexpressed semantic
possessor when the relation is contextually recoverable. In a relational
interpretation, \ird{čás} \trsl{head} denotes a head of some individual:
\[
 \lambda p.\lambda h[\mathrm{HEAD}(h)\land\mathrm{PART\_OF}(h,p)].
\]
Licensing the implicit relation and identifying its referent are distinct.
The relational nominal permits the dependency to remain unexpressed;
independently licensed binding, accessible discourse referents and utterance
perspective constrain its resolution. This lexical-semantic distinction is
independent of nominal declension class.

\pex\label{ex:nm-implicit-poss}
\a
\begingl
\gla Čás konca.//
\glb head.\Dir{} hurt-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{My / his / her / your head hurts.}
\lingcontext{possessor resolved from the utterance context}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Že čás konca.//
\glb 1\textsc{sg}.\Ind{} head.\Dir{} hurt-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{My head hurts.} \lingcontext{explicit possessor; clarification or contrast}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Marcí čás konca.//
\glb Marėk.\Ind{} head.\Dir{} hurt-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Marėk's head hurts.} \lingcontext{explicit nominal possessor}//
\endgl
\xe

In a spontaneous self-report, the first example favours the speaker's head.
A report about a patient or a narrative centred on Marėk can instead favour
that individual. These are defeasible contextual readings: neither the noun
nor \ird{konca} carries first-person possessor agreement. The head is the
sole clausal argument in these examples. An overt possessor remains an
ordinary prenominal indirect-case dependent inside \synhead{PossP}; it is
available for clarification, contrast or a less accessible referent. It adds
no clausal experiencer, external possessor or applied participant.

Resolving the possessor first supplies a property of heads belonging to
\(p\); identifying a head satisfying that property is a further operation.
Thus \ird{že čás} and \ird{Marcí čás} fix the possessor overtly while
leaving identification of the head to the resulting description and context.
A unique head relative to a resolved possessor need not have been mentioned
previously; a speaker-oriented interpretation of bare \ird{čás} requires no
covert article or additional specificity morpheme.

The semantic argument \(p\) is not itself a claim that the nominal
contains a silent pronoun. Provisional \(pro_{\mathrm{POSS}}\) in the
existing \synhead{PossP}, inside \synhead{KP}, can represent the dependency;
direct contextual saturation of the relational argument remains a distinct
analysis. Neither representation entails mechanical deletion of overt
\ird{že}, and omission is not defined as first-person pronoun deletion.

An overt possessor or independently licensed binding dependency fixes the
relation before discourse preferences apply. A continuing aboutness topic
can favour an accessible possessor, but neither the most recent subject nor
the grammatical pivot determines that referent automatically. Several equally
salient candidates can make the bare nominal ambiguous and favour an overt
possessor without making omission categorically ungrammatical.
Converbial same-pivot continuity likewise imposes no possessor-identity rule
(§\,\ref{sec:switch-reference}).

Ordinary alienables such as \ird{dum} \trsl{house} and \ird{tóm}
\trsl{book} need no obligatory lexical possessor argument. Their association
with an owner can be recovered where the context supplies it; a bare house
or book does not automatically mean the speaker's possession. Identifying
an individual and assigning an ownership relation remain distinct operations
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-def}). The possessed noun remains overt throughout:
this nominal dependency is separate from clausal argument omission and its
construction-specific licensing (§\,\ref{sec:ref-management}).

\subsection{Number within the possessive DP}
\label{sec:nm-poss-ne}
\label{sec:nm-poss-number}

A possessor and its containing nominal each bear their own fused case-number
form. The indirect plural \ird{bylac} therefore marks several children as
possessors without requiring the possessed object to be plural, while the
direct plural \ird{lúbce} marks several apples independently of the number
of their possessor (§\,\ref{sec:nm-num}). The following constructed pair
combines these established forms with indirect singular \ird{bylam} and
direct singular \ird{lobėk}:

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla bylac lobėk//
\glb child.\Ind{}.\textsc{pl} apple.\Dir{}.\textsc{sg}//
\glft \trsl{an apple belonging to children}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla bylam lúbce//
\glb child.\Ind{}.\textsc{sg} apple.\Dir{}.\textsc{pl}//
\glft \trsl{apples belonging to a child}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent Number is local to each nominal spine: plurality in one does
not require plurality in the other. The possessor and the possessed entity
can each be identifiable or newly introduced, independently of their
respective numbers. An indirect possessor's contextual accessibility does
not require its containing nominal to have the same discourse status.

Possession remains internal to the nominal when its head changes number
or receives a different clausal case:

\pex\label{ex:nm-slot-possessive-independence}
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[sailor]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}.\Sg{}} \textnormal{[[net]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Sg{}} \textnormal{[[sway]]}\textsubscript{\Prog{}}.//
\glb sailor.\Ind{}.\Sg{} net.\Dir{}.\Sg{} sway-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{A sailor's net is swaying.} \lingcontext{one sailor and one net}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[sailor]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}.\Pl{}} \textnormal{[[net]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Sg{}} \textnormal{[[sway]]}\textsubscript{\Prog{}}.//
\glb sailor.\Ind{}.\Pl{} net.\Dir{}.\Sg{} sway-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{A net belonging to several sailors is swaying.} \lingcontext{several sailors jointly possess one net}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[sailor]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}.\Sg{}} \textnormal{[[net]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Pl{}} \textnormal{[[sway]]}\textsubscript{\Prog{}}.//
\glb sailor.\Ind{}.\Sg{} net.\Dir{}.\Pl{} sway-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{A sailor's nets are swaying.} \lingcontext{one sailor possesses several nets}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[inspector]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Sg{}} \textnormal{[[sailor]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}.\Sg{}} \textnormal{[[net]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}.\Sg{}} \textnormal{[[check]]}\textsubscript{\Prog{}}.//
\glb inspector.\Dir{}.\Sg{} sailor.\Ind{}.\Sg{} net.\Ind{}.\Sg{} check-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{An inspector is checking a sailor's net.} \lingcontext{the net is now a patient; the sailor remains its nominal possessor}//
\endgl
\xe

The first three clauses vary the possessor's number independently of
the head's. In the fourth, the sailor and the net both bear indirect case,
but their dependencies differ: possession licenses the sailor's case, while
the imperfective verb licenses the patient's. The containing phrase's case
does not add a second case ending to its possessor.

\subsection{Stative verbs and property roots as \synhead{NP} modifiers}
\label{sec:nm-prop}

Ordinary property modification uses a dedicated non-finite \synhead{AttrP}
before the noun. ATTR selects a property projection or reduced state
predication, not a finite verb with its aspect ending removed. The lexical
property roots that also feed stative verbs belong to this lower property
domain. Prenominal property modification follows the possessive layer and
precedes the \synhead{NP}.

The head-final principle correctly predicts that all prenominal modifiers —
whether stative-verb-based or property-root-based — appear before the head noun,
never after it. Postnominal modification is not a productive construction in
standard Iridian; the prenominal restriction on modifiers is a direct
consequence of the head-final parameter.

\pex
\a \ird{múžna nuž} \trsl{a beautiful city}
\a \ird{hradná byl} \trsl{a good child}
\a \ird{svorná dum} \trsl{a big house}
\a \ird{všihňa byl} \trsl{a happy child}
\a \ird{žbirňa stol} \trsl{a wet table}
\xe

The listed root-strong Class~I \ird{múžna}, suffix-strong Class~II
\ird{hradná}/\ird{svorná}, and soft Class~III
\ird{všihňa}/\ird{žbirňa} realise this same ordinary attributive
function (§\,\ref{sec:stative-attr}). Class membership supplies neither
nominal definiteness nor agreement or temporal anchoring; it does not create
a universal contrast between enduring and episodic properties.

\noindent These ordinary attributives are restricted to adnominal use within
the \synhead{DP}. Clause-level manner modification is expressed instead by the
derived adverbs in \ird{-ě} discussed in §\,\ref{sec:stative-adv} of
Chapter~\ref{ch:derivational}; the attributive itself is not extended to ordinary
adverbial use.

\subsection{The \ird{-ovy} attributive and its embedded DP}
\label{sec:nm-ovni}

The relative head \ird{-ovy} selects the same voice--aspect
configuration as a participant nominal in \ird{-ou}, but forms a prenominal
modifier of an overt noun (§\,\ref{sec:th-relativization}). Its
\synhead{RelP} contains a silent operator linked to the privileged participant
gap in the embedded predicate; abstraction over that position supplies the
property intersected with the head noun's denotation. The modifier precedes the
noun, and its suffix does not agree with the noun in case.

The embedded configuration retains viewpoint and participant orientation,
while nominal relational heads license indirect-case thematic dependents
independently of matrix case. Thus perfective \ird{pjáščikovy} describes an
agent and \ird{védnikovy} a patient. The head noun receives case from its
matrix clause or, when it is itself a dependent within another relative,
from that containing nominal domain. It does not receive the case that its
silent counterpart would bear in a finite clause.

\pex
\a \ird{lúbcí pjáščikovy pres} \trsl{the man who ate the apple}
\a \ird{[Jancí védnikovy lúbcí] pjáščikovy pres}
   \trsl{the man who ate the apple that Janėk saw}
\xe

The operator in each relative corresponds to the participant privileged by its
own voice--aspect configuration. In the nested example, \ird{Jancí} is an
agent dependent of patient-oriented \ird{védnikovy}, whereas \ird{lúbcí},
modified by that relative, is the theme dependent of agent-oriented
\ird{pjáščikovy}. Neither relative assigns matrix case to \ird{pres}.
The exact attachment of \synhead{RelP} relative to possessive material remains
undetermined; its prenominal position and independent case domain do not depend
on that choice. An ordinary continuous property modifier uses the dedicated
attributive with quantity-conditioned \ird{-á}/\ird{-a}
(§\,\ref{sec:stative-attr}). A continuous stative relative in \ird{-ovy}
is separately licensed by an explicit episodic, stage-level, temporal or
contrastive context: \ird{mužnovy nuž} identifies a city beautiful at the
relevant stage, whereas \ird{múžna nuž} simply attributes beauty. The former
retains Asp and abstracts over the sole state holder; the latter is a reduced
property modifier without Asp. The
selected \synhead{AspP} includes inner valency, causative and orientation
structure, but ends below clausal Neg, Phas, the Comm-field, Mood and clausal
evidentiality (§\,\ref{sec:nominalised}). Thus the bracket in
\ird{[lúbcí pjáščikovy] pres} contains the positive participant description;
placing \ird{vy}, \ird{so}, \ird{oh}, \ird{kamo} or clausal negation
inside that reduced description is excluded. An operator in the matrix clause
may take scope over a predication involving the entire DP; it does not become
part of the relative. The restriction on reduced \ird{-ovy} does not ban
negative finite relatives.

\subsection{The \ird{ty}-supplement}
\label{sec:nm-appos}

Iridian possesses an appositional particle \ird{ty} that introduces a
right-peripheral supplement: a phrase that is coreferent with a preceding
nominal and presents it as a clarification, identification, or elaboration. The
semantics are those of identity---\textit{that is}, \textit{namely}---and the
\ird{ty}-phrase is always postnominal, set off from its antecedent by a prosodic
break.

The \ird{ty}-phrase is not a restrictive modifier. It does not narrow the
denotation of the head noun the way an adjective or relative clause does: a
phrase like \ird{Karel ty mlazka} does not pick out a subset of Karels who are
brothers, it simply adds the information that the referent already identified as
Karel is the speaker's brother. The \ird{ty}-phrase is therefore best analysed
as a \term{supplement}---a non-restrictive phrase linked to a nominal antecedent that is
coreferent with its antecedent but structurally peripheral to it, not integrated
into the \synhead{NP}'s extended projection. This distinguishes \ird{ty}-phrases
categorically from adjectives and relative modifiers, which are adjuncts within
the \synhead{NP} and compositionally restrict the head. Because \ird{ty}-phrases
are supplements rather than adjuncts, they are not governed by the head-final
principle: modifiers fall within the \synhead{NP} and must precede their head;
supplements are peripheral and their position---always to the right of the
antecedent---is determined by information structure and discourse organisation.

A map-room introduction illustrates the difference between identifying an
individual by an occupation and supplementing an already identified
individual with that information. The schemata use a new occupational slot
rather than assigning a lexical form or declension to it:

\pex\label{ex:nm-slot-supplement}
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[cartographer]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Sg{}} méžime.//
\glb cartographer.\Dir{}.\Sg{} wait-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{A cartographer is waiting.} \lingcontext{the occupation supplies the nominal description}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Karel ty \textnormal{[[cartographer]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Sg{}} méžime.//
\glb Karel.\Dir{} namely cartographer.\Dir{}.\Sg{} wait-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{Karel---that is, a cartographer---is waiting.} \lingcontext{the name identifies the individual before the supplement adds the occupation}//
\endgl
\xe

The occupational nominal itself heads the supplement in the second example,
so no anaphoric \ird{i} follows it. Although both clauses may describe the
same waiting individual, the supplement presents the occupational information
as a separate clarification rather than using it to restrict the name.

The antecedent nominal is introduced first; \ird{ty} then opens the supplement
phrase. When the supplement consists of a bare nominal, no further element is
required: the nominal is itself the head and the identity relation is immediate.

\pex
\a \ird{Karel ty mlazka}
   \trsl{Karel---that is, my brother}
\xe

When the supplement consists of non-nominal material---an adjective, a
\ird{-ovy}-relative, a postpositional phrase---a pronominal head is required to
host it. Modifiers in Iridian must precede a nominal head; without one, the
modifier cannot form a well-formed phrase. This head is supplied by the
anaphoric pronoun \ird{i}, which appears at the right edge of the supplement and
corefers with the antecedent nominal.

\pex
\a \ird{nuž ty múžna i}
   \trsl{the city---that is, the beautiful one}
\a \ird{pres ty že védnikovy i}
   \trsl{the man---that is, the one I saw}
\xe

\noindent The internal structure of the \ird{ty}-phrase respects head-finality
throughout: \ird{múžna} and \ird{že védnikovy} precede their head \ird{i} in
the ordinary left-to-right modifier-before-head order. Head-finality is not
suspended in the supplement; it applies within it. The distribution of \ird{i}
follows directly: it is required whenever the \ird{ty}-phrase contains a
non-nominal modifier that needs a nominal head to attach to, and absent when the
\ird{ty}-phrase already contains a nominal head.

Ordinary attributive modification---adjectives, \ird{-ovy}-relatives, and
similar prenominal material---remains the unmarked strategy for adnominal
description and carries no special discourse implication. The
\ird{ty}-supplement is marked for deliberate elaborative intent: it presents the
clarification as a distinct informational act, separate from the introduction of
the referent. In spoken language this implicature is weak, since production
pressures naturally favour establishing the referent before specifying it; in
formal and written language the implicature is stronger, and a \ird{ty}-phrase
reads as emphatic, contrastive, or rhetorically foregrounded. The construction
is not excluded from formal writing but is marked there, and is most natural for
heavy adnominal material---long nominalised relative clauses, for
instance---that would be unwieldy in prenominal position.

The full discourse-interactional properties of the \ird{ty}-supplement,
including its use as a repair and afterthought device in spoken interaction, are
treated in Chapter~\ref{ch:spoken} (\S\,\ref{sec:spoken-ty}).

\section{Indirect nominal dependence and postpositions}
\label{sec:nm-ext}

\subsection{Material, partitive, source, and other \synhead{NP}-external
  dependencies}
\label{sec:nm-ext-indirect}

The indirect case is the general dependency form of the nominal
system. Possessors are simply its most tightly integrated \synhead{DP}-internal
use. The same K-form appears with material nouns, partitives, source
expressions, and other semantically dependent nominals that are not the
privileged head of the phrase. In this wider domain the indirect does
not mark a special inventory of separate morphological cases; rather, it
is the single oblique realisation of K whose interpretation is narrowed
by the lexical relation between nouns or by the head that selects the
dependent nominal.

\pex
\a \ird{kuní prosc} \trsl{silver spoon}
\a \ird{ona pivum štava} \trsl{one mug of beer}
\a \ird{Študencím na hroná} \trsl{three of the students}
\a \ird{Marcí cy houba} \trsl{a gift from Marėk}
\xe

In the partitive \ird{študencím na hroná}, the indirect plural
\ird{študencím} denotes the containing set of students, while \ird{na}
closes its postpositional phrase before the cardinal. The cardinal therefore
does not impose singular morphology on this separate nominal complement
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-quant}).

Material, partitive, and source dependents therefore stand on a
continuum with possessors. The possessor is the most tightly integrated
prenominal use of indirect \synhead{KP} inside the \synhead{DP}. Material and partitive
dependents are looser nominal relations. Postpositional phrases are the
next step outward: the same indirect nominal domain serves as the
complement of a relational head that closes the phrase from the right.

\subsection{Postpositional complements and phrase structure}
\label{sec:nm-postpositions}
\label{sec:nm-postp-structure}
\label{sec:nm-postp-attachment}

Iridian adpositions are head-final and are best analysed as
\term{postpositions}. The constant generalisation is that the nominal
complement bears indirect case. In the smallest representation, the
postposition combines with an indirect \synhead{KP}. In fuller noun phrases, that
\synhead{KP} may itself be embedded inside demonstrative or quantificational layers, but the
selectional requirement remains the same: the nominal core is oblique,
and the postposition closes the complement phrase at its right edge.

The indirect nominal complement combines with a following postposition whose
lexical meaning specifies a spatial, participant, source, privative, or
substitutive relation.

The structural asymmetry is straightforward. The nominal complement is
assembled first: the lexical/possessive domain combines with Num and
receives indirect case in K, and may then be expanded by independently licensed
demonstrative or quantificational material. Only after that nominal phrase is complete does the
postposition merge as the head of the larger postpositional phrase.
Head-finality then yields the surface order \textit{complement} +
\textit{postposition}.

\begin{center}
\begin{forest}
  syntax tree
  [PostP
    [\synhead{KP}
      [\synhead{NumP}
        [\synhead{PossP}
          [{\textit{oblique possessor} / $\varnothing$}]
          [{\synhead{NP}\\\textit{head noun}}]
        ]
        [{Num\textsuperscript{0}\\{\footnotesize number}}]
      ]
      [{K\textsuperscript{0}\\\Ind{}}]
    ]
    [{Post\textsuperscript{0}\\\ird{na} / \ird{de} / \ird{še} / \dots}]
  ]
\end{forest}
\end{center}

\noindent The tree is intentionally conservative. What matters is not whether
every postpositional complement contains the same upper nominal layers, but
that the complement is at least an indirect \synhead{KP} and that any higher
nominal material remains inside the complement phrase rather than intervening
between the complement and the postposition.

The postposition attaches to the right edge of the governed phrase as a whole,
not to the noun stem in isolation. A bare noun, a demonstrative phrase, a possessed
noun phrase, and a noun phrase with prenominal modifiers all behave alike in
this respect: the nominal material comes first, and the postposition closes the
entire phrase.

\pex
\a \ird{dumě na} \trsl{in the house; at home}
\a \ird{bylam my} \trsl{for the child}
\a \ird{Marcí dumě na} \trsl{at Marėk's house}
\a \ird{svorná mostě ny} \trsl{over the big bridge}
\xe

\noindent The same logic extends to larger complements. Demonstratives,
possessors, property-root modifiers, stative modifiers, and \ird{-ovy} phrases
all belong to the nominal complement if they modify the noun phrase itself. The
postposition does not split them apart or attach to the lexical noun before they
are assembled. This is the chief diagnostic for analysing the forms as
postpositions over phrasal complements rather than as suffixes on nouns or as
free adverbs loosely associated with them.

A possessed complement remains intact when the postposition changes its
relation to the clause:

\pex\label{ex:nm-slot-pp-complement}
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[scout]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Sg{}} \textnormal{[[fisher]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}.\Sg{}} \textnormal{[[shelter]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}.\Sg{}} na méžime.//
\glb scout.\Dir{}.\Sg{} fisher.\Ind{}.\Sg{} shelter.\Ind{}.\Sg{} in wait-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{A scout is waiting in a fisher's shelter.} \lingcontext{na follows the complete possessed location expression}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[scout]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Sg{}} \textnormal{[[fisher]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}.\Sg{}} \textnormal{[[shelter]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}.\Sg{}} u méžime.//
\glb scout.\Dir{}.\Sg{} fisher.\Ind{}.\Sg{} shelter.\Ind{}.\Sg{} near wait-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{A scout is waiting near a fisher's shelter.} \lingcontext{only the postposition changes; the nominal remains indirect}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla \textnormal{[[scout]]}\textsubscript{\Dir{}.\Sg{}} \textnormal{[[fisher]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}.\Sg{}} \textnormal{[[shelter]]}\textsubscript{\Ind{}.\Sg{}} de ócňime.//
\glb scout.\Dir{}.\Sg{} fisher.\Ind{}.\Sg{} shelter.\Ind{}.\Sg{} to walk-\Prog{}//
\glft \trsl{A scout is walking towards a fisher's shelter.} \lingcontext{the goal postposition follows the same possessed complement}//
\endgl
\xe

The shelter bears indirect case under each postposition, while the
fisher's indirect case is licensed within the possessive nominal. Location,
proximity and goal are supplied by the final relational head, which takes
the whole possessed expression as its complement.

\subsection{Spatial and participant relations}
\label{sec:nm-postp-classes}
\label{sec:nm-postp-extended}

The common postpositions form a compact but semantically varied system. Their
lexical meanings differ, but their syntax is uniform.

\begin{table}[ht]
  \footnotesize
  \caption{Core postpositions and their main semantic classes.}
  \label{tab:nm-postpositions}
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{l l L}
    \toprule
    {\sc class} & {\sc members} & {\sc core relation} \\
    \midrule
    Locative & \ird{na} & location, interiority, surface contact \\
    Goal & \ird{de} & direction, endpoint \\
    Proximity & \ird{u} & nearness \\
    Accompaniment & \ird{še} & accompaniment, comitative association \\
    Benefactive & \ird{my} & benefit, intended recipient, purpose-oriented relation \\
    Source / separation & \ird{cy} & source, origin, movement away, extraction \\
    Privative & \ird{nam} & absence \\
    Replacement & \ird{pale} & substitution, `instead' \\
    Exception & \ird{ahte} & exclusion, `except' \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}
\FloatBarrier

\noindent The core spatial and participant relations are illustrated
below:

\pex
\a \ird{dumě na} \trsl{in the house}
\a \ird{vruham de} \trsl{to the town}
\a \ird{vruham u} \trsl{near the town}
\a \ird{bylam my} \trsl{for the child}
\a \ird{Jancí še} \trsl{with Janėk}
\a \ird{Marcí cy} \trsl{from Marėk}
\a \ird{záhárí nam} \trsl{without sugar}
\a \ird{Jancí pale} \trsl{instead of Janėk}
\a \ird{Jancí ahte} \trsl{except for Janėk}
\xe

Benefactive \ird{bylam my} contains an indirect-case complement of a
postposition, whereas an applied beneficiary is a DP licensed within the verbal
argument structure. In a simple indicative applicative, the child takes direct
case, as in \ird{Byl Marcí travě pjaštébime} \trsl{Marėk is eating bread
for the child's benefit} (§\,\ref{sec:vm-appl}). Both constructions can
express benefaction, although only the applicative selects that beneficiary
for the participant nominal and corresponding relative. A beneficiary retained
in indirect case under higher causative or directive orientation remains an
applied argument; its lack of \ird{my} and its licensing by Appl distinguish
it from the postpositional construction.

Alongside the broad locative and goal markers, Iridian also admits a set of more
specialised spatial and path postpositions. These behave like the core set in
selecting the same indirect nominal complement and in appearing at the right
edge of the phrase.

\pex
\a \ird{stolě po} \trsl{under the table}
\a \ird{mostě ny} \trsl{over the bridge}
\a \ird{domě měz} \trsl{between the houses; among the houses}
\a \ird{vruham do} \trsl{up to the town}
\a \ird{leše ňe} \trsl{through the forest; via the forest}
\xe

The same head-final syntax thus extends across broad location, bounded path,
vertical relation, interstitial relation, and traversal.

\subsection{Temporal complements of postpositions}
\label{sec:nm-postp-temporal}

Event-denoting nominals in \ird{-ou} combine with \ird{cy}, \ird{do},
\ird{na}, and \ird{u} to identify a temporal source, endpoint, location, or
nearby occasion. These temporal constructions extend the existing
postpositional syntax: the complete nominal complement bears indirect case,
and the relational head follows its right edge. The event reading is
essential, since lexical \ird{pjaštou} \trsl{food} supplies a substance
rather than an eating interval. The following constructed phrases use the
specified indirect singular \ird{pjaštově}
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-derived-inflection}).

\begin{table}[ht]
\footnotesize
\caption{Temporal postpositions with an event nominal.}
\label{tab:nm-temporal-postpositions}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{l L L}
\toprule
Construction & Core relation & Interpretation \\
\midrule
\ird{pjaštově cy} & temporal source & since/after eating \\
\ird{pjaštově do} & temporal endpoint & until eating \\
\ird{pjaštově na} & temporal overlap & during eating \\
\ird{pjaštově u} & temporal proximity & around the time of eating \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

Temporal \ird{cy} normally takes completion as its source, whereas \ird{do}
normally takes onset as the endpoint of an extended matrix interval; a
different boundary requires explicit contextual identification. Spatial
\ird{vruham do} \trsl{up to the town} supplies the existing boundary
postposition, distinct from goal \ird{de}. Temporal \ird{na} requires overlap,
without requiring equal duration or containment, while \ird{u} permits a
contextually near occasion before, during, or after the nominal event.
The detailed source and endpoint interpretations, including the effects of
matrix aspect and negation, are specified in
§\,\ref{sec:temporal-event-nominals}.

The event token and its temporal anchoring are supplied by the nominal
expression and discourse. An affirmative episodic context can establish an
actual reference event, while a future or modal context may supply a projected
one; neither \ird{-ou} nor contextual identification of the nominal alone
guarantees occurrence.
Nominal dependents retain their independently licensed indirect forms
(§\,\ref{sec:nm-derived-temporal}), and the temporal postposition introduces
no finite inflection inside the nominal. The forms remain separate written
words with the prosodic attachment described below. In particular,
postpositional \ird{u} is distinct from converb \ird{-u}; homophony supplies
no common historical origin.

\subsection{Clausal distribution and prosodic attachment}
\label{sec:nm-postp-clausal}
\label{sec:nm-postp-status}

The postpositional phrase is not confined to nominal modification. The same
structure appears as a clausal adjunct, as the location complement of
existential predication, and as a dependent nominal expression inside larger
noun phrases. Its nominal complement retains the same internal structure in each use.

\pex
\a
\begingl
\gla Marėk dumě na jeca.//
\glb Marėk.\Dir{} house.\Ind{} in \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{Marėk is at home.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Kroumaště na pivo so jeca.//
\glb refrigerator.\Ind{} in beer.\Dir{} still \Exst{}-\Cont{}//
\glft \trsl{There is still some beer left in the refrigerator.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Bylam my Jancí še stojáž.//
\glb child-\Ind{} for Janėk-\Ind{} with go-\Ctpl{}//
\glft \trsl{(I am) going for the child with Janėk.}//
\endgl
\a
\begingl
\gla Marcí cy houba.//
\glb Marėk-\Ind{} from gift.\Dir{}//
\glft \trsl{a gift from Marėk}//
\endgl
\xe

\noindent The existential examples connect directly to §\,\ref{sec:jec} in
Chapter~\ref{ch:predicates}: the entity whose existence is asserted stands in the
direct case, while the locative PP contributes the place relation. More broadly,
postpositional phrases behave like ordinary head-final dependent phrases in the
clause. They precede the final predicate and preserve their internal noun-first,
head-last organisation.

The common postpositions attach prosodically to the preceding independent word
at the right edge of their nominal complement. They remain outside that word's
minimal lexical stress domain and ordinarily bear no primary stress of their
own, and their syllables are excluded from its subsidiary footing
(§§\,\ref{sec:stress} and~\ref{sec:secondary-stress}). In \ird{dumě na},
\ird{na} leans on \ird{dumě}; in \ird{Marcí dumě na}, its immediate host is again
\ird{dumě}, while \ird{Marcí} retains its separate lexical stress. The
syntactic complement is the whole nominal phrase, whereas the immediate
phonological host is its final independent word.

These \term{postposed clitic adpositions} are written separately. Prosodic
attachment neither requires orthographic fusion nor turns the postposition into
a nominal suffix. The construction combines a phrase that bears indirect case
with a head-final relational marker; its weak pronunciation and its phrasal
syntax describe different properties of the same element.
Head-final order and obligatory adjacency to the nominal complement distinguish
this construction from a free clause adverb.

\subsection{Postpositions and lexical preverbs}
\label{sec:nm-postp-preverbs}

The bound lexical preverbs \ird{de-}, \ird{cy-}, and \ird{ňe-}
share goal, source, and traversal meanings with postpositional \ird{de},
\ird{cy}, and \ird{ňe}; positional \ird{ny-} and \ird{po-} likewise
share the above and below relations of \ird{ny} and \ird{po}. A preverb
attaches to a verbal root, whereas a postposition selects an indirect nominal
complement (§\,\ref{sec:lexical-preverbs}). Upward \ird{je-} and downward
\ird{o-} combine with ordinary goal, source, locative, or traversal phrases,
whose postpositions identify the Ground independently of the preverb
(§\,\ref{sec:preverb-ground}). Separately written
postpositional \ird{po} is distinct from complementizer \ird{po}, which
introduces a clause
(Chapter~\ref{ch:embedding}).

Replacement \ird{pře-} and privative \ird{ne-} correspond semantically
to postpositional \ird{pale} and \ird{nam}. Accumulating \ird{na-}
belongs to the verbal stem, whereas locative \ird{na} selects an indirect
nominal complement; these correspondences establish no common historical
origin. Accumulation supplies no comitative participant of the kind expressed
by \ird{še}; an additional applied argument requires ordinary applicative
derivation.
